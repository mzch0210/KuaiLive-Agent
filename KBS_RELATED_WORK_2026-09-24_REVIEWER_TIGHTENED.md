# 2. Related Work

> **Status:** manuscript draft; this version is tightened for KBS positioning and cites formally published versions when available.

## 2.1 Sequential, Long-Term, and Repeat-Aware Recommendation

Sequential recommendation models evolving user preference from ordered interaction histories. Self-attentive architectures such as SASRec and BERT4Rec established strong Transformer-style baselines by learning dependencies within historical sequences (Kang and McAuley, 2018; Sun et al., 2019). Later work has improved sequential representation through augmentation, auxiliary knowledge, richer context, and expert-style modules. For example, CeDRec combines hierarchical data augmentation with an adaptive in-context expert network to improve robust sequence representation under sparsity, noise, and cold-start conditions (Sun et al., 2025). Related work has also shown that long-term preference and repeated behavior carry predictive value beyond immediate short-term intent, including in live-streaming settings where viewers frequently revisit the same channels (Rappaz et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2025).

These studies establish that richer or longer history can improve recommendation, but that is not the methodological claim of this paper. We assume a strong sequential Base and study the residual value of a separately maintained relationship-evidence source. The question is therefore not how to encode broader history into the Base, but whether persistent relationship evidence still provides positive marginal value after accounting for what the Base already represents.

## 2.2 Auxiliary Evidence Integration, Reliability, and Harmful Information

A closely related research line studies how heterogeneous auxiliary evidence should be incorporated into recommendation. Recent KBS work illustrates several increasingly refined strategies. Xie et al. (2026) combine collaborative, semantic, and structural views through multi-head gating and explicitly distinguish users' long-term interests from short-term preferences. Zhu et al. (2026) formulate multimodal sequential fusion through mutual information, seeking complementary cross-modal information while reducing redundancy and modality noise. Zhang et al. (2026) further address irrelevant and imbalanced multimodal content through collaborative denoising and modality balancing. CollaPPA integrates collaborative knowledge with personalized sequential preference alignment in an LLM-based recommender (Yue et al., 2026). These methods primarily improve the joint representation learned from multiple available sources.

A second group of methods focuses on the quality or reliability of auxiliary evidence. Huo et al. (2026) model content-level and interaction-level reliability in multimodal recommendation, while information-theoretic and knowledge-aware methods increasingly distinguish informative from noisy or redundant relations. Such work is a natural conceptual neighbor because it rejects the assumption that every auxiliary signal is equally useful. However, **evidence quality and Base-relative marginal value are different quantities**. A signal may be reliable yet add little when the Base already represents overlapping information; conversely, a simple but valid source may be valuable when it contains information absent from the current Base representation.

The distinction between evidence integration and evidence valuation also appears outside recommendation. Yerragunta et al. (2026) propose dynamic conditional gating for question answering over incomplete knowledge bases, controlling how structured KB evidence and textual evidence are aggregated. This work is particularly relevant conceptually because it treats evidence use as conditional rather than uniform. Its objective, however, is to improve evidence aggregation under incomplete KB coverage. Our study instead treats a fixed relationship-memory specialist as an explicit object of event-level valuation and asks whether its ranking contribution is positive **relative to a strong Base representation**.

Research on negative transfer provides a complementary warning against assuming that more information is always beneficial. Peng et al. (2025) show that multimodal pretraining can harm downstream sequential recommendation and develop a re-learning strategy to mitigate that effect. We use negative transfer only as a conceptual connection: our setting does not study pretraining mismatch or transferred representations, but the marginal ranking utility of one persistent relationship-evidence source relative to a Base.

Taken together, these studies mostly ask how auxiliary information should be integrated, aligned, denoised, weighted, or gated. Our focus is a preceding decision question: **is this evidence source incrementally useful relative to the Base for this event and operating regime?** We refer to this as *Base-relative evidence valuation*. It complements rather than replaces evidence-integration methods.

## 2.3 Expert Routing, Selective Prediction, and Learning-to-Defer

Selective prediction and Learning-to-Defer (L2D) provide the closest general decision-theoretic background. Mozannar and Sontag (2020) formulate L2D as jointly learning a predictor and a rejector that can defer decisions to an expert, with consistency results through cost-sensitive learning. Narasimhan et al. (2022) move closer to a fixed-Base setting by learning a post-hoc deferral function on top of a pretrained model and optimizing an accuracy-cost trade-off relative to an expert. Mao et al. (2025) develop stronger theoretical guarantees for single- and multiple-expert routing in both one-stage and two-stage settings, and recent work extends L2D to dynamically varying expert pools (Montreuil et al., 2026). These results make clear that **selectively invoking an expert, or learning a routing policy on top of a fixed predictor, is established and is not a novelty claim of this paper**.

Recommendation also contains expert-style architectures. CeDRec, for instance, uses adaptive in-context experts as components of a sequential representation (Sun et al., 2025). Such systems learn which representational experts should contribute to the model itself. Our relationship-memory specialist differs in role: it is maintained as a separate, transparent evidence source, and the central object of study is its **specialist-minus-base ranking utility**. The methodological emphasis is therefore not on inventing a new router, but on characterizing why this particular specialist carries incremental evidence for some recommendation events and not for others.

This boundary is important for interpreting our Difficulty control. Difficulty is included to test whether identifying cases where the Base is weak is sufficient to identify cases where relationship Memory can help. It is not intended as a representative baseline for the broader L2D literature. The Utility-versus-Difficulty comparison therefore tests whether Base weakness and specialist usefulness are equivalent decision signals; it does not claim superiority over general deferral theory.

## 2.4 Live-Streaming Recommendation

Live-streaming recommendation introduces domain-specific dynamics that are absent from static-item settings: rooms become available and unavailable over time, viewers repeatedly return to channels, and interactions between viewers and streamers evolve during sessions. Zhang et al. (2021) model the two-sided dynamics of viewers and anchors through bi-directional prediction. Rappaz et al. (2021) explicitly identify dynamic availability and repeat consumption as defining characteristics of live-stream recommendation and propose LiveRec to model them. Later work further develops dynamic room representation and interaction-aware preference modeling (Gao et al., 2023; Chen and Liu, 2024), while hypergraph and graph-based approaches capture richer viewer-channel interactions and repeated behaviors (Yu et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025).

These studies demonstrate that dynamic availability, repeated consumption, and viewer-streamer interactions matter for live recommendation. Accordingly, we do not claim that previous work ignores persistent relationships or repeat behavior. Instead, live streaming provides an especially useful setting for studying **Base-relative evidence value**: target-specific relationship evidence may already be represented in the Base-visible sequence, may remain recoverable only from older history, or may be unavailable, while the candidate environment itself changes with room availability. KuaiLive provides a real-time benchmark with detailed room timing and multiple user-streamer interactions, whereas Twitch/LiveRec provides an availability- and repeat-aware sequential setting (Qu et al., 2026; Rappaz et al., 2021). We use these environments to study when persistent relationship evidence remains incrementally useful relative to a strong Base rather than to introduce a new live-stream benchmark or another generic model of repeated behavior.

### Positioning summary

The nearest research lines differ from this study mainly in their primary object of adaptation:

| Research line | Primary question | Typical adaptation unit | Position of this study |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sequential / long-term modeling | How should broader history be encoded? | User or sequence representation | Assume a strong Base and evaluate residual evidence value |
| Auxiliary / multimodal fusion | How should heterogeneous signals be integrated? | Joint representation | Ask whether a source is incrementally useful relative to the Base |
| Reliability / denoising | Which signals are trustworthy or noisy? | Modality, item, relation, or interaction signal | Reliability does not imply positive Base-relative marginal value |
| L2D / expert routing | Which predictor or expert should handle an instance? | Predictor or expert assignment | Characterize why a transparent evidence specialist is useful on particular events |
| Live-stream recommendation | How should dynamic rooms, interactions, and repeated behavior be modeled? | Domain-specific representation | Use live streaming as the empirical setting for Base-relative evidence valuation |

The table should remain in the main text because the principal review risk is misclassification of the paper as another long-history, fusion, or routing method. It should be kept compact and should not duplicate detailed prose.
