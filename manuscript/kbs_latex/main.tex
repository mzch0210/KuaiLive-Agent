\section{Introduction}

Sequential recommender systems infer users' evolving preferences from ordered interaction histories, and self-attentive models such as SASRec and BERT4Rec established strong sequence encoders for next-item recommendation \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. Yet stronger sequence modeling does not remove a more basic question: when does an additional source of historical evidence still contain information that is incremental relative to what the Base recommender already represents? More evidence is not necessarily more useful. An auxiliary source can be redundant when the Base already represents overlapping information, ineffective when no target-specific evidence exists, or complementary when it retains useful information outside the Base's current representation. The relevant problem is therefore not whether more historical evidence can improve recommendation in general, but whether that evidence has positive marginal value relative to what the Base already captures.

Recent recommendation research has developed increasingly sophisticated mechanisms for integrating heterogeneous evidence, including adaptive gating across collaborative, semantic, structural, and long-/short-term preference signals and information-theoretic fusion that explicitly seeks complementary multimodal cues while reducing redundancy \cite{xie2026mhgfcl,zhu2026mifusr}. These advances primarily address how available evidence should be represented, weighted, aligned, or fused. This leaves a different methodological question: does a particular auxiliary evidence source contribute positive marginal value beyond a strong Base for the current recommendation event? We refer to this perspective as \emph{Base-relative evidence valuation}. The unit of analysis is not the standalone quality of an auxiliary model, but the incremental utility of its evidence conditional on what the Base already represents.

Live-streaming recommendation provides a useful setting in which to study this question because candidate availability changes over time and repeat consumption of specific channels is substantial \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. KuaiLive further provides a real-time interactive benchmark with live-room timing information and multiple forms of user--streamer interaction, enabling evaluation under dynamic live-streaming conditions \cite{qu2026kuailive}. We therefore use an explicit relationship-memory specialist as a transparent source of persistent user--creator evidence. For a target creator, this evidence can occupy three Base-relative analysis states: \emph{represented}, when the target relationship appears in the Base-visible sequence; \emph{recoverable but unrepresented}, when it exists only in older history; or \emph{unavailable}, when no previous target-specific relationship exists. These states are not assumed to determine specialist utility by themselves. Rather, they describe how the specialist's evidence relates to the current Base representation.

Our empirical results show why this Base-relative distinction matters. On KuaiLive, the aggregate ordering between relationship Memory and a strong Base reverses across candidate regimes even when the evaluated events and evidence-state composition are unchanged, indicating that regime dependence cannot be reduced to state prevalence alone. On Twitch/LiveRec, frozen DEV analysis identifies strong conditional heterogeneity across the three evidence states, and the same sign structure reproduces on untouched TEST. More importantly, a DEV-only context-length intervention holds canonical relationship Memory fixed while expanding the Base context from 8 to 16 and from 16 to 32 interactions. For the same events that move from recoverable-but-unrepresented to represented as the Base context expands, specialist-minus-base utility falls by approximately 0.42--0.45 NDCG@10. This provides interventional support for a Base-relative visibility interpretation: the same persistent evidence becomes markedly less valuable once the Base representation comes to include it. The result does not imply a universal causal law, because operating regime and finer recency structure also affect realized utility.

This heterogeneity leads naturally to a selective decision problem. Let $B$ denote the Base recommender and $M$ the relationship-memory specialist, and define event-level specialist-minus-base utility as
\begin{equation}
\Delta_m(x)=u(M,x)-u(B,x).
\end{equation}
Selective specialist use should therefore target the expected marginal value of $\Delta_m(x)$, rather than simply identifying where the Base is weak. This distinction is substantive: Base difficulty identifies hard events, whereas specialist-relative utility identifies events that this particular evidence source can improve. A difficult event may still contain no target-specific evidence recoverable by the relationship specialist. In our matched-budget analysis, utility-based selection is more strongly concentrated on recoverable-but-unrepresented events, whereas a generic Difficulty control disproportionately selects unavailable events. Generic expert assignment and Learning-to-Defer already provide established formulations for allocating instances among predictors or experts under accuracy and cost trade-offs \cite{mao2025mastering}. Our aim is therefore not to introduce a new general deferral or gating theory, but to characterize and exploit the structured conditional utility of a transparent relationship-evidence specialist in recommendation.

This work makes three main contributions. First, we formulate \emph{Base-relative evidence valuation} for recommendation by evaluating a transparent auxiliary relationship-evidence source through event-level marginal ranking utility relative to a strong Base, rather than through standalone specialist performance or Base difficulty alone. Second, we provide an interpretable characterization of represented, recoverable-but-unrepresented, and unavailable relationship evidence, combine frozen DEV-to-TEST replication with a Base-context intervention, and show that the marginal value of the same persistent evidence contracts sharply once the Base comes to represent it; KuaiLive regime decomposition further shows that evidence state structures but does not deterministically fix utility. Third, we connect this mechanism to selective specialist use and evaluate it across recommendation regimes, strong Base controls, independent training seeds, and a frozen second-platform test. On untouched Twitch/LiveRec TEST, Selective Utility improves NDCG@10 by 0.00772 over the Base and by 0.00644 over an exact-budget Difficulty control. Taken together, the results support a view of auxiliary recommendation evidence as \emph{Base-relative rather than intrinsically valuable}: whether persistent historical evidence is worth using depends on what the Base already represents and on the operating recommendation regime.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Sequential, Long-Term, and Repeat-Aware Recommendation}

Sequential recommendation models evolving user preference from ordered interaction histories. Self-attentive architectures such as SASRec and BERT4Rec established strong Transformer-style baselines by learning dependencies within historical sequences \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. Later work has improved sequential representation through augmentation, auxiliary knowledge, richer context, and expert-style modules. For example, CeDRec combines hierarchical data augmentation with an adaptive in-context expert network to improve robust sequence representation under sparsity, noise, and cold-start conditions \cite{sun2025cedrec}. Related work has also shown that long-term preference and repeated behavior carry predictive value beyond immediate short-term intent, including in live-streaming settings where viewers frequently revisit the same channels \cite{rappaz2021liverec,zhu2025repeated}.

These studies establish that richer or longer history can improve recommendation, but that is not the methodological claim of this paper. We assume a strong sequential Base and study the residual value of a separately maintained relationship-evidence source. The question is therefore not how to encode broader history into the Base, but whether persistent relationship evidence still provides positive marginal value after accounting for what the Base already represents.

\subsection{Auxiliary Evidence Integration, Reliability, and Harmful Information}

A closely related research line studies how heterogeneous auxiliary evidence should be incorporated into recommendation. Recent KBS work illustrates several increasingly refined strategies. Xie et al. combine collaborative, semantic, and structural views through multi-head gating and explicitly distinguish users' long-term interests from short-term preferences \cite{xie2026mhgfcl}. Zhu et al. formulate multimodal sequential fusion through mutual information, seeking complementary cross-modal information while reducing redundancy and modality noise \cite{zhu2026mifusr}. Zhang et al. further address irrelevant and imbalanced multimodal content through collaborative denoising and modality balancing \cite{zhang2026mcd4sr}. CollaPPA integrates collaborative knowledge with personalized sequential preference alignment in an LLM-based recommender \cite{yue2026collappa}. These methods primarily improve the joint representation learned from multiple available sources.

A second group of methods focuses on the quality or reliability of auxiliary evidence. Huo et al. model content-level and interaction-level reliability in multimodal recommendation \cite{huo2026reliability}, while information-theoretic and knowledge-aware methods increasingly distinguish informative from noisy or redundant relations. Such work is a natural conceptual neighbor because it rejects the assumption that every auxiliary signal is equally useful. However, \emph{evidence quality and Base-relative marginal value are different quantities}. A signal may be reliable yet add little when the Base already represents overlapping information; conversely, a simple but valid source may be valuable when it contains information absent from the current Base representation.

The distinction between evidence integration and evidence valuation also appears outside recommendation. Yerragunta et al. propose dynamic conditional gating for question answering over incomplete knowledge bases, controlling how structured KB evidence and textual evidence are aggregated \cite{yerragunta2026gating}. This work is particularly relevant conceptually because it treats evidence use as conditional rather than uniform. Its objective, however, is to improve evidence aggregation under incomplete KB coverage. Our study instead treats a fixed relationship-memory specialist as an explicit object of event-level valuation and asks whether its ranking contribution is positive relative to a strong Base representation.

Research on negative transfer provides a complementary warning against assuming that more information is always beneficial. Peng et al. show that multimodal pretraining can harm downstream sequential recommendation and develop a re-learning strategy to mitigate that effect \cite{peng2025negative}. We use negative transfer only as a conceptual connection: our setting does not study pretraining mismatch or transferred representations, but the marginal ranking utility of one persistent relationship-evidence source relative to a Base.

Taken together, these studies mostly ask how auxiliary information should be integrated, aligned, denoised, weighted, or gated. Our focus is a preceding decision question: \emph{is this evidence source incrementally useful relative to the Base for this event and operating regime?} We refer to this as \emph{Base-relative evidence valuation}. It complements rather than replaces evidence-integration methods.

\subsection{Expert Routing, Selective Prediction, and Learning-to-Defer}

Selective prediction and Learning-to-Defer (L2D) provide the closest general decision-theoretic background. Mozannar and Sontag formulate L2D as jointly learning a predictor and a rejector that can defer decisions to an expert, with consistency results through cost-sensitive learning \cite{mozannar2020defer}. Narasimhan et al. move closer to a fixed-Base setting by learning a post-hoc deferral function on top of a pretrained model and optimizing an accuracy-cost trade-off relative to an expert \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc}. Mao et al. develop stronger theoretical guarantees for single- and multiple-expert routing in both one-stage and two-stage settings \cite{mao2025mastering}, and recent work extends L2D to dynamically varying expert pools \cite{montreuil2026online}. These results make clear that selectively invoking an expert, or learning a routing policy on top of a fixed predictor, is established and is not a novelty claim of this paper.

Recommendation also contains expert-style architectures. CeDRec, for instance, uses adaptive in-context experts as components of a sequential representation \cite{sun2025cedrec}. Such systems learn which representational experts should contribute to the model itself. Our relationship-memory specialist differs in role: it is maintained as a separate, transparent evidence source, and the central object of study is its \emph{specialist-minus-base ranking utility}. The methodological emphasis is therefore not on inventing a new router, but on characterizing why this particular specialist carries incremental evidence for some recommendation events and not for others.

This boundary is important for interpreting our Difficulty control. Difficulty is included to test whether identifying cases where the Base is weak is sufficient to identify cases where relationship Memory can help. It is not intended as a representative baseline for the broader L2D literature. The Utility-versus-Difficulty comparison therefore tests whether Base weakness and specialist usefulness are equivalent decision signals; it does not claim superiority over general deferral theory.

\subsection{Live-Streaming Recommendation}

Live-streaming recommendation introduces domain-specific dynamics that are absent from static-item settings: rooms become available and unavailable over time, viewers repeatedly return to channels, and interactions between viewers and streamers evolve during sessions. Zhang et al. model the two-sided dynamics of viewers and anchors through bi-directional prediction \cite{zhang2021bidirectional}. Rappaz et al. explicitly identify dynamic availability and repeat consumption as defining characteristics of live-stream recommendation and propose LiveRec to model them \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. Later work further develops dynamic room representation and interaction-aware preference modeling \cite{gao2023driver,chen2024preference}, while hypergraph and graph-based approaches capture richer viewer-channel interactions and repeated behaviors \cite{yu2024hypergraph,zhu2025repeated}.

These studies demonstrate that dynamic availability, repeated consumption, and viewer-streamer interactions matter for live recommendation. Accordingly, we do not claim that previous work ignores persistent relationships or repeat behavior. Instead, live streaming provides an especially useful setting for studying \emph{Base-relative evidence value}: target-specific relationship evidence may already be represented in the Base-visible sequence, may remain recoverable only from older history, or may be unavailable, while the candidate environment itself changes with room availability. KuaiLive provides a real-time benchmark with detailed room timing and multiple user-streamer interactions, whereas Twitch/LiveRec provides an availability- and repeat-aware sequential setting \cite{qu2026kuailive,rappaz2021liverec}. We use these environments to study when persistent relationship evidence remains incrementally useful relative to a strong Base rather than to introduce a new live-stream benchmark or another generic model of repeated behavior.
