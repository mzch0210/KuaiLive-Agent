\title{Candidate-Aligned Knowledge Valuation for Selective Relationship Memory Routing in Live-Streaming Recommendation}

\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{abstract}
Selecting when to use historical viewer--creator relationships requires estimating their value relative to the incumbent ranking on the candidates available at each request. We study how sampled- versus full-candidate supervision of this Base-relative value shapes the use of a fixed relationship Memory ranker. Offline NDCG@10 increments provide sampled- or full-candidate labels for selection policies trained on pretarget history and Base-score features; both frozen policies are then evaluated on identical full-active candidates. In 10,222 paired KuaiLive room-ranking events, Memory-minus-Base utility shifts from $-0.04120$ with sampled-active candidates to $+0.04631$ with full-active candidates. The primary evaluation uses 30,000 additional author-ranking targets from the same recording period. Relative to policies fitted using the original uncorrected sampled labels, full-candidate-supervised policies improve weighted full-ranking NDCG@10 by $0.005391$ (SASRec) and $0.003585$ (GRU4Rec), with lower population-weighted Memory-call fractions under their naturally realized policies. Exploratory development analyses of 50,049 requests find different Memory-use decisions on 21.32\% (SASRec) and 23.01\% (GRU4Rec) of requests. On a nested 4,096-target cohort, authentic histories outperform constrained historical randomizations by $0.031145$ NDCG@10 under fixed candidates. The comparisons concern complete frozen policies, with unequal realized calls and an uncorrected sampled-label baseline. Together, the results show that candidate-aligned supervision is consequential for the allocation and full-ranking quality of selective historical knowledge use.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sequential recommendation has to use a bounded history to rank the alternatives available at a given moment \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. Live-streaming platforms make this constraint especially visible: room availability changes from session to session, whereas a viewer's relationships with creators may extend across many broadcasts. A separate historical relationship source can recover useful evidence beyond the incumbent model's recent input, but it may also repeat preferences the incumbent already captures. The question for a knowledge-based recommender is therefore when accessing that source improves the ranking that would otherwise be produced.

Conditional expert selection provides a natural way to express this question, but its usefulness depends on the target used to supervise the decision. Knowledge-enhanced recommendation and memory-management methods primarily study how relational evidence is represented, integrated or retained \cite{xie2026mhgfcl,zhong2026samd}. Learning-to-defer and adaptive inference study how to choose between predictors or inference paths \cite{mozannar2020defer,mao2024regression,yuan2026dsframe}. In the present setting, these concerns meet at the incremental value of an \emph{already fixed} Memory ranker relative to a designated Base. That value determines which requests could benefit from additional historical evidence.

The candidate universe creates a further, consequential distinction. A relationship source and a Base may appear differently matched when ranked against a small sampled subset rather than the full set of eligible alternatives. Sampling-induced changes in ranking metrics are well established \cite{krichene2020kdd}, and prediction targets need not align with downstream decisions \cite{mandi2024decisionfocused}. Here the practical question is more specific: when sampled- and full-candidate evaluations provide different \emph{Base-relative utility labels}, do the resulting fitted selectors invoke the historical specialist on different requests, and does that change full-candidate ranking performance? We examine this question through separately fitted, DEV-calibrated selection policies for the same fixed incumbent and relationship specialist. Pretarget history and Base-score descriptors support the selection decision; the observed target supplies offline supervision but never becomes a deployment-time input.

Our empirical design separates the main policy consequence from the evidence needed to interpret it. Twitch streamer rankings and paired KuaiLive room rankings establish why relationship value must be evaluated relative to the incumbent and the candidate roster (RQ1). The central M3 experiment compares independently frozen sampled- and full-supervised complete selection policies on 30,000 previously unevaluated author-ranking targets from the same recording period (RQ2); a development-cohort analysis describes the requests whose Memory allocation differs. A parallel test then holds current full candidates and the Memory scorer fixed while comparing authentic viewer--creator histories with constrained historical randomizations on a nested 4,096-target cohort (RQ3). The different ranking units and evidence roles are specified in Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}.

We make three connected empirical contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Candidate-aligned supervision and policy-level allocation.} On 30,000 previously unevaluated M3 author-ranking targets from the same recording period, the full-candidate-supervised frozen policies achieve higher weighted full-candidate ranking quality and lower population-weighted Memory-call fractions than policies trained with the original, uncorrected sampled-candidate labels. A separate DEV allocation trace describes which requests change specialist use; it is not an independent confirmation of the additional-target result.
    \item \textbf{Context for incumbent-relative relationship value.} Historical-state diagnostics and paired KuaiLive room-ranking comparisons show how the measured gain from the same fixed knowledge source depends on the incumbent context and ranking-candidate regime.
    \item \textbf{Parallel historical-source evidence.} Under identical full candidates and specialist scoring, a parallel nested study compares authentic viewer--creator histories with constrained historical randomizations, testing the informational contribution of the relationship source.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Knowledge Representation, Relationship Memory, and Memory Management}

Sequential recommendation represents preferences from ordered interactions, including attention-based and multi-interest architectures \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec,ni2025idmarec,sheng2025dsmgrec}. Knowledge-aware methods extend that representation by integrating relational information: CKLF combines graph propagation and sequential interests \cite{chen2024cklf}; MHGFCL fuses collaborative, semantic and structural views \cite{xie2026mhgfcl}; and KAPER selects preference-aware graph paths for reranking \cite{kim2026kaper}. These methods principally address how auxiliary knowledge enters a recommender's representation or ranking function.

A related line of work studies what information should remain available for later decisions. SAMD evaluates which personal interactions merit retention \cite{zhong2026samd}; MemRec organizes collaborative memory for LLM recommendation \cite{chen2026memrec}; and denoising or reliability-aware approaches examine the quality of additional signals \cite{peng2025negative,zhu2026mifusr,zhang2026mcd4sr}. Information-value reasoning also links a source's usefulness to its consequences for an action \cite{abbas2025voi}. These developments motivate evaluating a retained relationship source at the moment of use. In our experiments the source and incumbent are fixed, allowing the marginal contribution of choosing the specialist's complete ranking to be measured directly.

\subsection{Utility-Based Expert Selection and Adaptive Inference}

Learning-to-defer provides established tools for selecting among predictors according to their relative losses, consistency properties and potential invocation costs \cite{mozannar2020defer,narasimhan2022posthoc,mao2024regression,mao2025mastering}. In recommendation, DS-Frame studies a learned selector between fast predictions and more expensive refinement \cite{yuan2026dsframe}. Uncertainty-based routing, including LLM-guided approaches such as GUIDER, offers additional selection strategies \cite{xu2026guider}. Together, these studies provide the methodological context for selective use of a fixed specialist.

The supervision target is central to that selection. Decision-focused learning shows why predictive accuracy on a surrogate need not coincide with decision quality \cite{mandi2024decisionfocused}. For a historical Memory ranker, predicting Base difficulty, Memory accuracy and Memory-minus-Base ranking utility are different objectives. We evaluate the last of these with identical Base, specialist and deployment-time feature definitions while varying the candidate regime used to construct its training labels and the associated DEV-calibrated selection policies. This isolates a concrete empirical comparison between \emph{complete policies}, rather than proposing a general expert-routing algorithm. Invocation quotas define the selection objective; realized serving costs must be assessed separately.

\subsection{Candidate-Dependent Ranking in Live Recommendation}

Live recommendation combines recurring viewer--creator interactions with time-varying broadcast eligibility. LiveRec models streamer consumption \cite{rappaz2021liverec}, KuaiLive distinguishes persistent creators from active room sessions \cite{qu2026kuailive}, and DCGLive models interactions among users, rooms and creators \cite{guo2026dcglive}. Research on repeated viewing and engagement further motivates explicit historical context \cite{gao2023driver,zhu2025repeated}. These settings provide complementary evidence, although ranking streamers, rooms and persistent authors entails distinct candidate definitions.

Sampled evaluation complicates comparisons of ranking utility. Krichene and Rendle show that sampled ranking metrics need not preserve full-ranking model comparisons \cite{krichene2020kdd}. Subsequent work estimates rank distributions or full-ranking metrics from samples using weighted likelihood, maximum-entropy and EM-based approaches, and studies small-$K$ blind spots and adaptive sampling \cite{jin2021sampledeval,li2023reliable,li2024itemsampling}. Candidate sampling can interact with popularity bias \cite{ihemelandu2023candidatesampling}, while negative-sampling corrections have also been proposed for training ranking losses \cite{subbiah2024rankestimation}. Reliability analyses further examine the interplay of logged exposure and sampling in offline model comparisons \cite{pereira2025samplingreliability}. This literature establishes that the candidate and observation protocols matter for estimating ranking performance.

Our question concerns a downstream consequence of that established sensitivity. Sampled-candidate evaluation supplies a label for the \emph{relative value of invoking} a fixed relationship specialist; learning from that label can change which requests use the specialist when full-active candidates are available. We first characterize this dependence with paired room-ranking evidence, then compare frozen sampled- and full-supervised author-ranking policies on identical full candidate rosters. A separate fixed-candidate history comparison assesses the source supplying the Memory signal. These experiments connect candidate-dependent evaluation to selective knowledge access while retaining the distinct experimental roles of each ranking task.

\section{Knowledge Valuation and Selective Use}
\label{sec:problem}
\label{sec:framework}

The framework separates three objects: the utility of a fixed historical relationship source relative to an incumbent, a candidate-regime-specific supervised estimate of that utility, and a selective-use policy based only on pretarget information. Base and Memory share the eligible alternatives within each ranking decision. Observed outcomes define offline labels; the deployment path in Figure~\ref{fig:framework} uses pretarget features to decide whether to invoke Memory.

\subsection{Ranking Events and Candidate-Dependent Utility}
\label{sec:event-utility}

For user $u$, decision time $t$ and candidate regime $r$, let $\xi_r=(u,t,\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t},\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$ denote the pre-outcome context. The set $\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ contains the eligible ranking alternatives and $\mathcal{H}_{u,t}$ the interactions observed strictly before $t$. An offline evaluated event is $x_r=(\xi_r,y)$, where $y$ is the subsequently observed target in that eligible set. Rankers operate on $\xi_r$ without seeing $y$; the target is used only to evaluate their outputs. Candidate regime $r$ specifies how candidates and their scores are constructed, and a given event can be evaluated under more than one regime. We suppress $r$ when it is fixed.

Let $r_A(y\mid\xi)$ be the one-indexed position of target $y$ in the candidate ranking returned by model $A$. For one observed relevant item, event-level normalized discounted cumulative gain at cutoff $K$ is
\begin{equation}
    u_K(A,x)=
    \begin{cases}
        \displaystyle \frac{1}{\log_2\!\left(r_A(y\mid\xi)+1\right)}, & r_A(y\mid\xi)\le K,\\[6pt]
        0, & r_A(y\mid\xi)>K.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:event-ndcg}
\end{equation}
Mean NDCG@$K$ is calculated across an explicitly specified evaluation sample, with $K=10$ throughout the experiments. Since ranks depend on the eligible alternatives, changing the candidate regime may change the observed value of the same ranker without changing its historical evidence or scoring function.

\subsection{Fixed Incumbent and Explicit Relationship Knowledge}
\label{sec:framework-base}
\label{sec:framework-memory}

The trained \emph{Base} ranker $B$ is the designated incumbent within each dataset's evaluation protocol. Twitch uses availability-aware LiveRec \cite{rappaz2021liverec}; the original KuaiLive room-ranking model combines room- and creator-identity SASRec branches, standardizing scores within each candidate set before their development-selected fusion; and KuaiLive-M3 uses author-ranking SASRec and GRU4Rec. These are protocol-specific trained models, not a single incumbent transferred between streamer-, room- and author-ranking tasks. Their training and checkpoint-selection procedures are described in Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup} and Supplementary S2--S3.

The \emph{Memory} ranker $M$ is a fixed and auditable specialist defined by prior user--creator relationships. Let $n_{u,i}(t)$ count interactions with creator $i$ before the event and $n_{u,\max}(t)=\max_j n_{u,j}(t)$ across eligible recorded history. The long-term score is $\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)=n_{u,i}(t)/\max\{1,n_{u,\max}(t)\}$. For the user's last $K_s=10$ creator interactions, indexed backward from the most recent by $d=0$, define $\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)=\max_{\{d:i_d=i\}}\exp(-d/3)$, with zero if no matching event exists. The popularity component $\mathrm{Pop}(i)$ is the log-count transform of TRAIN-split creator frequency followed by min--max scaling; it is zero for a creator absent from that reference. The specialist score is
\begin{equation}
s_M(u,i,t)=
0.45\,\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)
+0.45\,\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)
+0.10\,\mathrm{Pop}(i).
\label{eq:memory-scoring}
\end{equation}
The weights remain fixed across ranking events and the specialist does not retrain or fuse its coefficients for a particular user. Eligible rooms in the original KuaiLive protocol may share a creator-level score, whereas Twitch and M3 rank persistent streamer or author identities directly. In M3, Short uses the last ten \emph{all-author} interactions, and Long's history normalization considers past authors even if they are not in the current TRAIN-known vocabulary. Such historical observations affect recency and normalization, but they do not create additional eligible current candidates. Both $B$ and $M$ rank the \emph{same} event-specific candidates; eligibility and tie-breaking rules are recorded in Section~\ref{sec:exp-data} and Supplementary S1--S2.

\subsection{Incumbent-Relative Knowledge Value and Candidate Regimes}
\label{sec:relative-utility}
\label{sec:conditional-utility}

Knowledge value is defined as the difference between two fully specified ranking decisions, rather than the specialist's isolated predictive quality. For the same event and eligible candidates, write
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_M(x)=u_K(M,x)-u_K(B,x).
    \label{eq:delta-memory}
\end{equation}
Positive or negative values mean that replacing $B$ with $M$ improves or worsens the realized top-$K$ rank utility. The target-dependent $\Delta_M$ is observable during supervised development and offline evaluation; it is \emph{not} itself a decision-time feature.

Let $Z$ denote features computed from pretarget history, current candidate availability and Base scores. The regime-specific conditional value function is
\begin{equation}
    \eta_r(z)
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid Z=z,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
A model fitted to these labels yields $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ as the decision-time prediction of using $M$ instead of $B$. Candidate regime is part of the value target even if the selector is not given a separate categorical regime indicator.

For the paired development tasks, $\Delta_S$ and $\Delta_F$ are the incremental utilities measured on sampled and full-active candidate sets, respectively. An observed target may be retained in a retrospectively constructed sampled \emph{training label}; this construction is separate from the information available to a deployed selector. Let $\pi_S$ and $\pi_F$ be policies trained and calibrated under their respective labels and evaluated on the same full-active events. Define $V_F(\pi)=\mathbb{E}[u_{10}(B,X_F)+\pi(Z)\Delta_F(X)]$. The policy transfer comparison is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{S\rightarrow F}
    =V_F(\pi_F)-V_F(\pi_S),
    \label{eq:candidate-supervision-transfer}
\end{equation}
This is a contrast between the realized full-candidate performance of \emph{frozen fitted policies}, not an identity guaranteeing that aligned supervision is superior or that their realized specialist-call rates are equal.

\subsection{Pretarget Estimation and Observable Evidence}
\label{sec:framework-estimation}

The feature vector $Z$ combines summaries of historical creator repetition and support with the incumbent's score distribution over the currently available candidates. These descriptors characterize the knowledge that could be useful and the incumbent decision it could change. The Twitch selector uses six history and eight Base-score features, while M3 uses seven history/candidate and five Base-score features; the fitted estimators and candidate-scoring pipelines are protocol-specific. M3's historical-support share, for example, is derived from creator identities already observed and the current candidate roster, \emph{before} computing the full Memory ranking.

Training labels contain the subsequently observed target and its realized ranking positions, but no such target-specific information enters $Z$. The same separation applies to features that could only be calculated from the true target's membership in the Base's input or historical sequence: these are reserved for retrospective diagnostics. Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparators} specifies the fitted estimators and comparator targets; full feature transformations and audit rules appear in Supplementary S2 and S7.

\subsection{Invocation-Constrained Use and Retrospective Diagnostics}
\label{sec:decision-rule}
\label{sec:framework-controls}

A policy first computes the Base ranking and the observable features. For each event it either returns $B$ or computes the ranking from $M$ using the identical eligible candidates. Denoting this pretarget decision by $a(Z)\in\{0,1\}$, the realized selected utility is $u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(x;a)=(1-a)u_K(B,x)+a\,u_K(M,x)$. Taking expectations conditional on a regime yields
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}\!\left[u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(X;a)-u_K(B,X)\mid R=r\right] \\
    &\qquad=\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\Delta_M(X)\mid R=r\right]
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\eta_r(Z)\mid R=r\right].
    \label{eq:expected-selective-gain}
\end{align}
The identity connects conditional incremental value with expected selection quality, following established learning-to-defer formulations \cite{mozannar2020defer,narasimhan2022posthoc}. It motivates using the estimated value to calibrate a selective policy.

The Utility policy compares the fitted gain estimate with a DEV-selected threshold. With $\succeq_r$ denoting the protocol's deterministic boundary convention, the policy is
\begin{equation}
    a_{\tau_r}(z)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[
    \hat{\eta}_r(z)\mathrel{\succeq_r}\tau_r
    \right].
    \label{eq:utility-threshold}
\end{equation}
A threshold is chosen on development data for an invocation-fraction design limit $q_r$: $\hat q_{\mathrm{DEV}}=n_{\mathrm{DEV}}^{-1}\sum_i a_{\tau_r}(z_i)\le q_r$. Calibration constrains the \emph{development} call count and does not guarantee an identical realized fraction on the evaluation cohort. It is a limit on specialist invocations, rather than an observed cap on production compute time or monetary expense. The same frozen Base--Memory pair supports comparisons with predicted Base difficulty, standalone Memory performance and available pretarget heuristics; see Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparators}.

To analyze the conditions behind RQ1 after decisions have been evaluated, we also use outcome-dependent historical evidence categories. These are explanatory descriptors rather than inputs to the selective policy.

\paragraph{Retrospective relationship-evidence states.}
\label{sec:framework-states}
Let $V_L(x)$ indicate that the \emph{observed target creator} occurs in the Base's most recent $L$ input interactions, and let $H(x)$ indicate that the creator occurs in eligible earlier user history. For explanatory analysis after the target outcome, define
\begin{equation}
S_L(x)=
\begin{cases}
\text{represented},&V_L(x)=1,\\
\text{recoverable but unrepresented},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=1,\\
\text{unavailable},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:diagnostic-states}
\end{equation}
The three states distinguish evidence represented in the input window, recoverable outside that window and not observed in accessible history. The label \emph{represented} describes sequence membership, not proof that the trained embedding retained every relationship. Since these states depend on the observed target, they are \emph{never} features for the real selector.

\paragraph{Regime-level retrospective decomposition.}
\label{sec:regime-decomposition}
Let $S$ denote the preceding mutually exclusive historical-evidence states. Their contribution to mean specialist value obeys
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\Delta}_r
    =\sum_s P(S=s\mid R=r)
    \,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid S=s,R=r\right],
    \label{eq:regime-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The decomposition separates state prevalence from conditional Memory--Base utility. It supports retrospective candidate-regime analysis (Section~\ref{sec:exp-regimes}) and does not enter decision-time features or introduce another training target.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

The primary evaluation compares frozen policies trained with sampled- versus full-candidate relative-value labels on additional M3 author-ranking events (RQ2). Two supporting analyses explain the problem and its implications: Twitch and paired KuaiLive room rankings characterize the contextual value of relationship evidence (RQ1), and a nested M3 historical-source comparison examines whether authentic relationships matter on fixed candidates (RQ3). The M3 DEV decision-allocation analysis explains fitted policy differences without supplying a separate held-out confirmation. Table~\ref{tab:exp-datasets} separates the ranked units, cohorts and evidence roles before the detailed protocols.

\subsection{Ranking Tasks and Evidence Hierarchy}
\label{sec:exp-data}

\paragraph{Twitch/LiveRec: historical complementarity and frozen selection.}
In an availability-aware, ten-minute observation-step protocol, Twitch ranks eligible persistent streamers \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. The filtered data comprise 46,878 development (DEV) and 44,221 held-out TEST requests, each with one recorded relevant streamer. LiveRec uses the 16 most recent input interactions, while relationship histories contain only interactions preceding the target observation. Eligibility is determined by the recorded crawl-step boundary; these records do not establish exact physical session entry or exit. Training data determine the Memory popularity reference (Supplementary S1). The observed target is used to score the frozen ranking and retrospectively characterize evidence recoverability, never to construct an online selector feature.

\paragraph{Original KuaiLive room ranking.}
The original KuaiLive protocol ranks an active \emph{room}, although relationship knowledge is indexed by its persistent creator \cite{qu2026kuailive}. A per-user chronological leave-last-two-out split yields 10,222 eligible users and paired DEV/previously examined TEST room targets. The sampled-active evaluation ranks the positive alongside 574 distinct active non-target rooms (575 total); the full-active evaluation uses the recorded contemporaneous active room roster. Half-open session intervals determine candidate availability, with the observed positive retained when its source interval conflicts with the event. Sampled alternatives are not assumed to be verified disliked items. The same 10,222 previously examined TEST events, Base checkpoint and Memory history are used in both candidate regimes. This is a retrospective within-event comparison rather than a new, prospectively frozen room-ranking evaluation.

\paragraph{KuaiLive-M3: original forward-time author ranking.}
M3 ranks persistent \texttt{author\_id} identities; active room sessions determine which TRAIN-known authors are available at a decision. The recorded-time TRAIN boundary is 23 March 2026, 12:00, and DEV ends on 27 March 2026, 14:00; the subsequent interval supplies TEST targets. No time zone absent from source metadata is imputed. From 24,995,148 eligible TRAIN interactions, a deterministic outcome-independent construction provides 50,049 DEV and 47,575 original TEST requests, the latter from 12,533 users. Each selected target belongs to the TRAIN-known author vocabulary and has at least two earlier valid user interactions. The full-active candidate set contains all distinct TRAIN-known authors with at least one active room at the event time (median 31,065 authors on original TEST). The separate 4,096-request, 575-author DEV panel selects neural checkpoints; it is not the full-active TEST ranking environment. The globally chronological split does not make users disjoint across time.

\paragraph{M3 additional targets and auxiliary analyses.}
A separate pre-outcome freeze selected 30,000 additional author-ranking targets (10,387 users) from the \emph{same} original March 2026 TEST-source collection interval, with no overlap in \emph{evaluated event IDs} with the 47,575 original TEST requests. A nested 4,096-target/3,059-user sample tests authentic versus perturbed relationship history. Neither sample is a newly collected later-period TEST; users and earlier interaction streams may overlap the initial evaluation. M3 exploratory DEV analyses compare candidate-supervision regimes and historical components; additional DEV historical nulls assess source controls. A matched 256-request DEV hardware replay measures scoring-path time, not online service cost. Their specific methodological roles are kept separate from the original forward-time and additional-target inference.

\subsection{Fixed Rankers and Selection Objectives}
\label{sec:exp-comparators}

Each evaluated event is ranked by a designated trained Base and the fixed Short--Long--Popularity Memory on \emph{the same available alternatives} (Section~\ref{sec:framework}). The Twitch incumbent is availability-aware LiveRec; the original KuaiLive room incumbent is the development-selected Dual-ID SASRec fusion; and M3 uses author-ranking SASRec or GRU4Rec with three separately trained initialization seeds (20261012, 20261013 and 20261014). The six M3 fits share the original TEST requests; they measure checkpoint robustness rather than six independent test populations.

To distinguish estimated marginal knowledge value from general recommendation difficulty, the supervised policies predict either \emph{Utility}, $u_{10}(M,x)-u_{10}(B,x)$; \emph{Difficulty}, $-u_{10}(B,x)$; or \emph{Specialist Quality}, $u_{10}(M,x)$. M3 additionally uses two pretarget heuristic scores: \emph{Uncertainty}, the negative Base top-two ranking-score margin, and \emph{History}, the user's previous interaction count. The margin is a heuristic, not a separately calibrated uncertainty estimator. Always Base and Always Memory form unconditional references. Random@budget is an expected-allocation control, and the hindsight Oracle uses realized outcomes only as an offline upper bound. Utility, Difficulty and Specialist Quality share the relevant protocol's regression class and available pre-outcome feature family; their prediction targets, not the underlying Base or Memory rankers, differ.

\subsection{Development Calibration and Pre-Outcome Information}
\label{sec:exp-freeze}

Neural incumbents and the deterministic Memory formulation are fixed before the corresponding held-out evaluation. Twitch uses five DEV out-of-fold (OOF) partitions for utility regression and freezes the Utility threshold before TEST. The Twitch Difficulty and hindsight Oracle references retrospectively match the \emph{realized Utility TEST Memory-call count}; they are equal-count quality controls, not independently frozen deployable policies. The original room experiment reuses previously examined TEST events solely for its paired candidate diagnostic.

For each of the six original M3 Base fits, a common sampled-candidate DEV screening panel selects the neural checkpoint, while five \emph{user-group-disjoint} DEV OOF folds train and calibrate each supervised selection objective on full-active ranking events. Five policy scores (the three fitted objectives and two deterministic heuristics) receive independently chosen, inclusive-tie thresholds maximizing DEV OOF incremental ranking utility subject to at most 30\% DEV Memory invocations. These thresholds and predictors are fixed before the 47,575-event original TEST. The policy is applied pointwise without using outcomes, recalibrating on TEST, or selecting the top requests of a TEST batch. Consequently a common DEV cap does not imply equal realized TEST call fractions, which may exceed 30\%.

M3 ranking and history updates follow recorded event time. Past interactions, including TEST-period observations that have already occurred but are not among evaluated TEST target events, may update a subsequent request's history. No current or future outcome participates in the live candidate set, Base/Memory scoring, policy descriptors or threshold selection. Candidate rank ties use a deterministic author-identity convention. The saved decisions and rank outputs precede revelation of their corresponding target for metric computation. This causal information boundary differs from the retrospective sampled \emph{training-label} construction, where the observed author is retained in the sampled DEV ranking roster solely to define $\Delta_S$. The latter is never used as an online candidate generator or a predictor feature (Supplementary S1--S2).

\subsection{Additional-Target Freeze and Prespecified Comparisons}

To evaluate candidate-aligned supervision on targets not previously scored in the original M3 TEST, an outcome-blind selection freezes 30,000 eligible event IDs (10,387 users) drawn from the same previously studied March 2026 TEST-source interval. Selection is stratified into twenty population-weighted cells: five recorded source dates crossed with four within-date \emph{pretarget-history} quartiles, with 1,500 selected events per cell. Event selection uses source identities and earlier-observable activity, not current target authors or model utility. A nested, independently frozen 4,096-event subset (3,059 users) supports the historical-source test. Neither selection creates new future-period source data or user-disjoint replication.

For each primary original M3 Base family (SASRec and GRU4Rec, checkpoint seed 20261012), the sampled-supervised and full-supervised gates use the same fixed Base/Memory pair and twelve \emph{full-candidate} decision-time features. Their four-fold user-disjoint DEV OOF scores determine separate thresholds under the nominal 30\% DEV call limit; a final predictor of unchanged capacity is then fitted on all DEV data and used with its arm's previously selected OOF threshold, without recalibration on the additional targets. The nominal constraint applies to OOF selection, not to the final refit's realized call fraction or any TEST-period quota. Gate structure and capacity are held constant; the labels differ because sampled training utilities are calculated with target-retained DEV subsets, whereas full labels use complete active candidates. Four final predictors, thresholds and target-blind rankings were sealed before the additional-event author outcomes were revealed. All four choices are evaluated on the same original full-active candidates, without test-time threshold adjustment or equalizing realized calls after the fact.

For the 4,096 nested targets, the unchanged original Full Memory scorer uses either the authentic historical source or three previously sealed, closed-hour historical author-label permutations. Each condition ranks identical full-active candidates, with source event times, author-hour activity margins and candidate availability preserved as specified in the DEV control. The three nulls are alternate histories of the \emph{same} target sample. The original protocol included a cohort-specific, pre-unseal comparison to TRAIN-only Popularity as a nondegeneracy acceptance condition; \emph{this additional cohort's Popularity margin was not scored and sealed before the outcome revelation}, so that original condition is not retrospectively certified. The completed DEV popularity control and other observed null nonfloor diagnostics do not establish the missing acceptance check (Supplementary S10.15 for supervision, S10.16 for the historical control, and S10.18 for the acceptance audit).

\subsection{Candidate-Regime and Historical-Source Controls}
\label{sec:exp-regimes}

The following paired candidate and historical perturbations provide contextual and explanatory evidence alongside the frozen policy comparison, rather than additional confirmatory TEST populations.

\paragraph{Room-ranking candidate-regime comparison (RQ1).}
For original KuaiLive, the same histories, targets and fixed raw Base/Memory scorers are evaluated on sampled-active $S$ and full-active $F$ candidates. The Base combines its branches after a score standardization whose candidate reference may vary with the roster. Event-level sampled-to-full changes are
\begin{equation}
D_i =
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})\big]
-
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})\big].
\label{eq:paired-regime-shift}
\end{equation}
To distinguish membership from score-normalization reference, write $g_i(C,R)$ for Memory-minus-Base utility with ranked candidates $C\in\{S,F\}$ and score-reference roster $R\in\{S,F\}$. Order-averaged allocations are
\begin{align}
A_{C,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(F,S)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(S,F)\big],\nonumber\\
A_{R,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(S,F)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(F,S)\big].
\label{eq:factorial-allocations}
\end{align}
This is a within-event accounting of the observed regime shift: candidate membership changes the number, identities and competitive ranks of alternatives jointly. Three checkpoint pairs and nine sampled candidate draws on the same previously examined room cohort provide sensitivity checks (Supplementary S5 and S8), not new independent TEST populations.

\paragraph{Exploratory M3 supervised-label transfer (RQ2).}
A distinct DEV analysis uses the original 50,049 full-active M3 author-ranking DEV events from 12,628 users and primary SASRec/GRU4Rec Base seeds. Holding the trained Base, deterministic Memory and twelve full-active pretarget features fixed, a five-outer/four-inner \emph{user-disjoint} design fits and calibrates sampled-label $S\!\rightarrow\!F$ and full-label $F\!\rightarrow\!F$ selectors. The sampled label retains the target retrospectively, whereas all policies are evaluated on identical full-active candidates using target-blind features. An adapted condition uses full-active labels from outer-training users and is not an unsupervised or label-free transfer. Inner DEV caps are 30\%; outer call fractions arise naturally. These HGB fits use 120 iterations rather than the original M3 policies' 200. Follow-up DEV source-component (Long-only, Short-only, TRAIN-popularity and placement) and reduced-feature controls provide exploratory mechanisms; 2,000 paired user-cluster bootstrap replicates describe their differences (Supplementary S10). They are not new chronological TESTs or members of the original TEST hypothesis family.

\paragraph{DEV historical identity perturbation (RQ3).}
The historical-source diagnostic freezes 1,024 requests from 992 DEV users in 16 pretarget-time/activity strata, excluding a preceding 128-request feasibility pilot. An original Full/Long scorer parity check precedes constrained null construction. Historical author labels are exchanged between different viewers within the same source hour, preserving each viewer's observed event times and counts, source author-hour totals and contemporaneous creator availability. A swap affects target histories only after its source hour fully closes; the active source hour stays authentic. Three fixed seeds (20261014--20261016) leave the evaluated $F$ and retrospective $S$ candidate rosters unchanged. Original-versus-null Full Memory NDCG@10 on common $F$ candidates is analyzed with 2,000 paired user-cluster bootstrap draws per seed. These are three perturbations on \emph{one} DEV cohort, not population replicates. The DEV control also checks its own TRAIN-only Popularity ranking floor; that check does not substitute for a different cohort's prospective acceptance requirement (Supplementary S10.14 and S10.18).

\subsection{Endpoints, Statistical Inference, and System Measurements}
\label{sec:exp-statistics}
\label{sec:exp-implementation}

The primary rank endpoint is event-level NDCG@10 (Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg}); secondary reporting includes HR@10 and the observed Memory invocation fraction. All stated ranking comparisons are paired by evaluated event, and uncertainty estimation resamples at the user level. Target-relative evidence states (Eq.~\eqref{eq:diagnostic-states}) and recurrence are assigned retrospectively; they do not enter a live selector. Oracle and random allocation at a given call level provide hindsight and expected-allocation references.

Twitch TEST reports 5,000 user-paired bootstrap replicates. The paired original KuaiLive room candidate shift uses 3,000 paired user resamples. For the initial M3 47,575-event/12,533-user TEST, 5,000 user-cluster paired bootstrap draws form percentile intervals, and 5,000 user-cluster sign randomizations provide one-sided tests under an approximate within-user sign-symmetry assumption. The original M3 predeclared family compares Utility against Always Base, Difficulty and Specialist Quality, with Holm adjustment \cite{holm1979} for three comparisons \emph{separately within each} of the six Base--seed fits. Shared evaluation users prevent interpretation as six independent population replications or blanket correction across eighteen tests. Uncertainty, History and Always Memory comparisons are additional evaluations.

For the 30,000 additional M3 target events, the twenty-cell poststratification estimator weights each sampled cell by its frequency in the eligible residual TEST-source population. User-cluster pairing is maintained in 5,000 bootstrap draws for two-sided 95\% intervals and 5,000 cluster sign randomizations for one-sided tests. Holm adjustment covers exactly three precommitted directional comparisons: SASRec full-supervised minus sampled-supervised ranking utility, the analogous GRU4Rec contrast, and authentic Full Memory minus the \emph{within-event mean} of three historical null utilities on the nested cohort. This is a distinct comparison family from the initial original TEST. Different realized F/S Memory-call counts are reported explicitly, not retrospectively equalized. The additional targets share the source recording period with the initial TEST and do not constitute external future-time validation.

The original KuaiLive Base comprises 64-dimensional ReChorus SASRec room/creator branches, one attention layer, four heads and maximum input length 50. LiveRec uses context length 16; M3 Base models use 64-dimensional embeddings, 16-item histories and one SASRec self-attention or GRU4Rec recurrent layer. Original Twitch and M3 supervised gates use histogram gradient boosting (200 iterations, learning rate 0.05, depth 3, minimum leaf size 50 and $\ell_2$ penalty 1); the later exploratory DEV gates use the explicitly different capacity reported above. Full training, history processing and reproducibility specifications remain in Supplementary S1--S3 and S7--S10.

A separately timed \emph{DEV-only} scoring experiment executes the original primary-seed SASRec pointwise Utility selector, Always Base and Always Memory on the same 256 distinct author-ranking requests, each repeated three times in counterbalanced order on an RTX 3090, with CUDA synchronization. Pretarget history-source scanning and author-roster construction are timed separately from the warmed per-request scoring paths (Supplementary S10.17). This is neither production end-to-end serving latency nor a timing evaluation of the additional-target vectorized F-aligned gate. Memory-call constraints, absolute ranking utility and realized computation time are therefore reported as different outcomes.
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The evaluation addresses three related but distinct research questions about one fixed relationship-knowledge source: the context in which its incremental ranking value arises (RQ1), the consequences of predicting that value for selecting between two rankers (RQ2), and, in a parallel source-validity comparison, the contribution of authentic historical relationships when current candidates remain unchanged (RQ3). We then examine invocation limits and measured ranking-path costs as conditions on practical use. All comparisons retain the ranked units, frozen models and evidence classifications of Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}.

\subsection{RQ1: When Is Relationship Knowledge Incrementally Valuable?}
\label{sec:results-heterogeneity}
\label{sec:results-regimes}

\paragraph{Complementarity depends on the incumbent's usable historical context.}
On 44,221 held-out Twitch streamer-ranking requests, unconditional Memory obtains NDCG@10 $0.53980$, below the LiveRec incumbent's $0.58211$ (paired difference $-0.04232$, 95\% user-bootstrap CI $[-0.04521,-0.03950]$). The aggregate, however, combines different historical situations. On 5,539 \emph{recoverable-but-unrepresented} events—where the relevant creator is present in accessible earlier history but absent from the Base input—Memory contributes $+0.22045$ NDCG@10 relative to Base (95\% CI $[+0.21140,+0.23001]$). The corresponding gains are $-0.01935$ for 24,285 represented events and $-0.18215$ for 14,397 events without prior accessible target history. Figure~\ref{fig:results-evidence-states} presents this stable pattern across DEV and frozen TEST; all group-specific Base/Memory means, counts and uncertainty intervals are preserved in Supplementary Table S10.13. These are \emph{outcome-defined diagnostic groups}, not labels supplied to the online selector.

In the distinct M3 full-active \emph{author}-ranking TEST, fixed Memory attains NDCG@10 $0.34679$ on 47,575 requests, exceeding each of the six trained SASRec/GRU4Rec incumbents ($0.25800$--$0.26065$), including a $+0.08879$ advantage over the primary SASRec Base. The difference between aggregate Twitch and M3 values is consistent with the definition of knowledge value as \emph{incumbent-relative}, while the protocols change Base architecture, ranking unit, source history and candidate universe together. It is not an isolated treatment effect of any one of those factors.

\subsection{RQ2: Does Candidate-Aligned Value Supervision Improve Knowledge Use?}

The principal supervision comparison asks whether labels derived from the full-active candidate regime change the outcomes of frozen knowledge-use policies evaluated on that same regime. \paragraph{Actual-candidate labels improve frozen policy outcomes.}
The S-supervised comparator uses the original, uncorrected 575-candidate labels obtained by retrospectively retaining the observed target; the regime-adapted DEV control in Supplementary Table S10.14 uses full-candidate training labels and is not a sampled-only statistical correction. The separate 30,000-target additional M3 evaluation tests the RQ1 implication that the supervision candidate regime matters for policy learning. \subsection{How Supervision Reallocates Historical Knowledge Calls}
\label{sec:results-allocation}

Exploratory DEV evidence traces how supervision affects call allocation rather than constituting an additional confirmatory TEST. On the common 50,049 full-active M3 DEV requests, F-supervised versus S-supervised policies achieve $0.302658$ versus $0.296499$ NDCG@10 for SASRec (difference $+0.006159$; CI $[+0.004835,+0.007543]$) and $0.303093$ versus $0.298123$ for GRU4Rec ($+0.004970$; CI $[+0.003550,+0.006369]$). Their naturally realized calls are similar, but 10,672 (21.32\%) and 11,517 (23.01\%) event decisions disagree. The cases newly selected by F-supervision have larger observed Memory-minus-Base gain than those no longer selected (SASRec 0.133417 versus 0.076194; GRU4Rec 0.126600 versus 0.079135). 

More precisely, the 5,350 SASRec F-only calls contribute $+0.014262$ NDCG@10 across the whole DEV cohort while 5,322 displaced S-only calls contribute $-0.008102$, for net $+0.006159$. For GRU4Rec, 5,639 added and 5,878 displaced calls contribute $+0.014264$ and $-0.009294$, yielding net $+0.004970$. This switch accounting is an algebraic partition of realized DEV policy value, not a causal decomposition of the separate additional 30,000-target policy contrast. Full per-policy values, call counts and the adapted condition appear in Supplementary Table S10.14.

\subsection{Operational Conditions: Invocation Limits and Measured Scoring Cost}
\label{sec:results-efficiency}

Selection only becomes an operational advantage under an explicit resource objective. In the original frozen M3 TEST, Utility invokes Memory on 27.72--29.21\% of requests after DEV-limited calibration; a History heuristic reaches 35.47\% on TEST. In the additional 30,000-target cohort, the unconstrained Always Memory reference obtains weighted full-candidate NDCG@10 $0.348744$, exceeding SASRec and GRU4Rec F-aligned selective policy scores of $0.305446$ and $0.304530$. These values delimit the accuracy benefit of invoking the specialist unconditionally.

On original M3 DEV, raising the inner call limit from 30\% to 50\% improves SASRec selective NDCG@10 from $0.302658$ to $0.319654$ and GRU4Rec from $0.303093$ to $0.320478$; at a 90\% limit both reach approximately $0.34105$, approaching Always Memory $0.343509$. Without a cap the learned policies invoke Memory on 99.91\% and 99.44\% of events, respectively. An additional decision analysis of $J_\lambda(\pi)=V_F(\pi)-\lambda q(\pi)$, with \emph{hypothetical} NDCG-denominated marginal specialist-call disutility, selects the 30\%-inner-cap option only in the observed point-estimate intervals $\lambda\in[0.084516,0.111321]$ for SASRec and $[0.083119,0.104379]$ for GRU4Rec; at zero penalty Always Memory is preferable. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Candidate-Indexed Value as a Knowledge-Use Decision Contract}
\label{sec:discussion-values}

For a fixed historical knowledge source, useful evidence is defined by the ranking decision it improves. Its incremental value depends on the incumbent that would otherwise answer the request and on the candidates eligible at that moment. The Twitch history-state analysis shows how relationship evidence can complement a sequential incumbent; the paired KuaiLive room comparison shows that the same Base--Memory pair can have markedly different measured advantages across sampled and full candidate rosters. Because these rosters differ in membership and ranking context, the room comparison establishes candidate-regime sensitivity rather than the isolated effect of candidate count.

This perspective connects two established bodies of research. Knowledge representation and memory-management methods study the construction and retention of useful evidence \cite{xie2026mhgfcl,zhong2026samd}; learning-to-defer studies how predictors or experts are selected for particular decisions \cite{mozannar2020defer,mao2024regression}. Likewise, sampled ranking evaluation and decision-focused learning explain why a surrogate metric or label may fail to match a downstream objective \cite{krichene2020kdd,mandi2024decisionfocused}. Our empirical addition lies at their intersection: the candidate regime used to supervise \emph{Memory's advantage over Base} can alter the set of requests receiving that already-defined knowledge source. Candidate definition is consequently part of a knowledge-use supervision contract, not simply a technical choice for reporting offline ranking scores.

\subsection{From Supervision Regime to Allocated Knowledge Calls}
\label{sec:discussion-selective-decisions}

The additional M3 evaluation connects this supervision contract to a measurable policy consequence. With the same fixed Base--Memory pairs, decision-time feature definitions and intended full-active evaluation, the full-supervised complete policies achieve higher full-candidate ranking quality and make fewer Memory calls than the original sampled-supervised policies. This result concerns frozen, separately trained and DEV-calibrated policies on additional event IDs from the same recording period. It supports a practical distinction between learning to identify valuable historical evidence on the intended candidate universe and assessing a selector only on the candidate subset from which its labels were obtained.

The DEV request-level analysis clarifies what changes between these policies. Approximately 21--23\% of event decisions disagree, and the requests newly receiving Memory under full-candidate supervision have greater mean full-candidate incremental value than the displaced requests. Both switched groups are beneficial on average, suggesting a problem of \emph{prioritizing valuable knowledge calls}, rather than simply avoiding negative-value calls. These comparisons describe fitted allocation on the existing DEV cohort; they do not constitute an independent confirmation or causal mediation analysis of the separately frozen additional-target outcome.

The observed policy advantage also has a precise comparator and resource interpretation. The S labels came from the original target-retained, uncorrected sampled-candidate protocol; the two gates were calibrated separately, and realized additional-target invocation rates differ by 1.337 and 1.797 percentage points. Thus the complete-policy comparison should not be read as a strict matched-call estimate of the effect of changing labels alone. A statistically defensible sampled-only correction, evaluated with a prespecified resource contract, remains a consequential alternative for future work.

\subsection{Source Validity, Evaluation Limits and Actual Operating Conditions}
\label{sec:discussion-validity}

The fixed-candidate historical-source comparison addresses a different part of the argument. Authentic viewer--creator histories carry more ranking value than the constrained historical alternatives under unchanged current candidates and Memory scoring. This supports the relevance of the information being selectively accessed. The perturbations nevertheless jointly alter repeated relationships and higher-order historical structure: their effect is a composite source-validity contrast, not an isolated semantic mechanism. The original acceptance checks were \emph{partially completed}: the sealed null rankings passed the reported performance, positive-support and substantial-rewiring checks, but the additional cohort's prespecified TRAIN-only Popularity comparison was not measured and sealed before target reveal. The corresponding DEV check cannot replace that missing cohort-specific prerequisite.

The experiments also establish specific limits to transfer. Twitch streamer ranking, KuaiLive room ranking and M3 author ranking use distinct candidate and feedback contracts. The additional M3 events were previously unevaluated but came from the same recording period, and the historical-source cohort was nested within them. Consequently the evidence supports the defined offline ranking comparisons, not causal online user outcomes or future-period generalization. Moreover, a valid sampled-only correction could narrow the observed F-versus-S policy difference; we did not train or evaluate a selector using such corrected labels and cannot rule out that alternative. Single-positive feedback can also depend on historical exposure, which is known to interact with candidate sampling in offline recommender evaluation \cite{pereira2025samplingreliability}.

The relevant resource constraint must likewise be specified rather than assumed. On the measured M3 cohort, Always Memory achieves higher unconstrained ranking quality than the selective policies, and the earlier pointwise Utility route incurred more overhead than invoking Memory unconditionally in the recorded warmed DEV replay. That timing does not measure the newer vectorized F-aligned gate. Lower Memory-call counts alone therefore do not establish a deployment-savings result. The present evidence is most informative for systems with a real access, capacity or invocation quota; an end-to-end serving study would be needed to translate such a policy into compute, latency or economic benefits.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

For selective use of a fixed historical relationship source, the incumbent ranking and eligible candidates jointly define the value being supervised. In the studied live-streaming protocols, changing the candidate regime used for relative-value labels changes the requests assigned to Memory and the full-candidate ranking performance of the resulting separately fitted, frozen policies. Contextual Twitch and room-ranking evidence explains the sensitivity of this target, while a parallel historical-source comparison supports the informational value of authentic viewer--creator relationships.

For selective knowledge use, the incumbent, source, eligible candidates, supervision labels and resource objective form a connected evaluation contract. The current policy comparison uses the original uncorrected sampled baseline and naturally realized invocation rates. Future comparisons with corrected sampled supervision, independent recording periods and measured access constraints can test how broadly this contract improves knowledge-based recommendation.
