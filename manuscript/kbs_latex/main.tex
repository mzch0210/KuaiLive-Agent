\section{Introduction}

Sequential recommender systems infer users' evolving preferences from ordered interaction histories, and self-attentive models such as SASRec and BERT4Rec established strong sequence encoders for next-item recommendation \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. Yet stronger sequence modeling does not remove a more basic question: when can an auxiliary specialist using additional historical evidence improve event-level ranking beyond a trained base recommender? More evidence is not necessarily more useful. An auxiliary specialist may be redundant when its ranking overlaps with the base's effective predictions, unhelpful when target-specific relationships are unavailable, or complementary when persistent relationships outside the base-visible sequence support better ranking. The relevant problem is therefore not whether more historical evidence can improve recommendation in general, but whether a specialist instantiated from that evidence yields positive marginal ranking utility relative to the base.

Recent recommendation research has developed increasingly sophisticated mechanisms for integrating heterogeneous evidence, including adaptive gating across collaborative, semantic, structural, and long-/short-term preference signals and information-theoretic fusion that explicitly seeks complementary multimodal cues while reducing redundancy \cite{xie2026mhgfcl,zhu2026mifusr}. These advances primarily address how information is represented, weighted, aligned, or fused; they do not directly characterize the event-specific ranking advantage of a fixed auxiliary specialist over an already trained base. We refer to the latter operational perspective as \emph{Base-relative evidence valuation}. Its measured quantity is the specialist-minus-base ranking-utility difference, not the model-independent information content of the underlying evidence.

Live-streaming recommendation provides a useful setting in which to study this question because candidate availability changes over time and repeat consumption of specific channels is substantial \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. KuaiLive further provides a real-time interactive benchmark with live-room timing information and multiple forms of user--streamer interaction, enabling evaluation under dynamic live-streaming conditions \cite{qu2026kuailive}. We therefore use a relationship-memory specialist as a transparent source of persistent user--creator evidence. For a target creator, this evidence can occupy three Base-relative analysis states: \emph{represented}, when the target relationship appears in the base-visible sequence; \emph{recoverable but unrepresented}, when it exists only in older history; or \emph{unavailable}, when no previous target-specific relationship exists. These states describe target-specific history visibility relative to the input given to the base, not necessarily what its learned representation contains. They are used only for retrospective mechanism analysis and are unavailable to the pre-outcome selector.

Our empirical results show why this distinction matters. On KuaiLive, the aggregate ordering between the relationship-memory specialist and a strong base recommender reverses across candidate regimes even when the underlying user--time--target instances and evidence-state composition are held fixed across candidate-set constructions, showing that the instantiated specialist's ranking contribution depends on candidate construction beyond evidence-state prevalence. On Twitch/LiveRec, development-set analysis reveals strong conditional heterogeneity across the three evidence states, and the same sign structure reproduces on untouched held-out test data. A development-only context-capacity comparison offers convergent evidence: among matched target instances whose relationship becomes visible at a larger context length, specialist-minus-base utility contracts by approximately 0.42--0.45 NDCG@10 while the relationship-memory specialist is held fixed. Because the base recommender is also retrained at each context length, the contraction cannot be attributed to visibility alone; it is consistent with, but does not isolate, a visibility interpretation: improvements in the separately trained base's ranking utility directly reduce the fixed specialist's measured advantage.

This heterogeneity motivates selective specialist use and connects three testable claims: the instantiated specialist's relative value varies across operating regimes; relationship visibility and recency structure, but do not determine, that value; and estimates of specialist-minus-base utility can guide invocation beyond identifying where the base is weak. The distinction is substantive: base difficulty identifies hard events, whereas conditional relative utility aims to identify events for which this specialist is expected to improve upon the base. A difficult event may still contain no target-specific evidence recoverable by the relationship-memory specialist. In matched-budget analysis, utility-based selection concentrates more strongly on recoverable-but-unrepresented events, whereas a generic difficulty control disproportionately selects unavailable events. Post-hoc deferral and modern Learning-to-Defer already establish how routing rules can be learned on top of fixed predictors under accuracy--cost trade-offs \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc,mao2025mastering}. Our contribution is therefore not a new general deferral or gating formulation, but the characterization and operational use of the structured conditional utility of a transparent relationship-evidence specialist in recommendation.

The contributions of this work are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operational Base-relative valuation.} We measure the event-level marginal ranking utility of a fixed, transparent relationship-memory specialist over a strong base recommender, without interpreting this difference as an intrinsic measure of historical information.
    \item \textbf{Conditional relationship-evidence structure.} We characterize target-history visibility states and recency, examine their association with specialist-relative utility across candidate regimes, and corroborate this structure through untouched held-out replication and context-capacity comparisons of separately trained bases.
    \item \textbf{Conditional decision and validation.} We estimate specialist-minus-base utility for pre-outcome invocation and evaluate its decision value against base-difficulty controls across KuaiLive regimes, independent base seeds, and an untouched Twitch/LiveRec held-out test.
\end{itemize}
Although we instantiate the formulation with relationship memory in live-stream recommendation, the decision formulation applies to fixed auxiliary specialists more generally; the empirical claims of this study remain limited to the evaluated live-streaming platforms, candidate protocols, and ranking outcomes.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Sequential and Long-Horizon Recommendation}

Sequential recommendation models evolving user preference from ordered interaction histories. Self-attentive architectures such as SASRec and BERT4Rec established strong Transformer-style baselines by learning dependencies within historical sequences \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. More recent work has strengthened user-intent representations through multi-level augmentation and contrastive learning \cite{ni2025idmarec}, while multi-interest and multi-granular models capture diverse structure within historical interactions \cite{sheng2025dsmgrec}. Such developments enrich historical representation beyond the original self-attentive baselines. This literature establishes that broader history can improve recommendation quality. The present study assumes a strong sequential base and asks a different question: whether a separately instantiated relationship-memory specialist provides a positive ranking-utility advantage relative to the trained base under the same candidate protocol.

\subsection{Auxiliary Evidence Integration, Reliability, and Harmful Information}

A closely related research line studies how heterogeneous auxiliary evidence should be incorporated into recommendation. Recent KBS work illustrates several increasingly refined strategies. Xie et al. combine collaborative, semantic, and structural views through multi-head gating and explicitly distinguish users' long-term interests from short-term preferences \cite{xie2026mhgfcl}. Zhu et al. formulate multimodal sequential fusion through mutual information, seeking complementary cross-modal information while reducing redundancy and modality noise \cite{zhu2026mifusr}. Zhang et al. further address irrelevant and imbalanced multimodal content through collaborative denoising and modality balancing \cite{zhang2026mcd4sr}. Evidence-centric recommendation has also moved beyond fusion: KAPER explicitly selects preference-relevant graph-path evidence using temporal and structural weighting and incorporates the selected evidence into candidate re-ranking and explanation generation \cite{kim2026kaper}. These studies improve how auxiliary information is represented, filtered, selected, or used downstream.

A second group of methods focuses on the quality or reliability of auxiliary evidence. Huo et al. model content-level and interaction-level reliability in multimodal recommendation \cite{huo2026reliability}. Related information-theoretic and knowledge-aware methods likewise distinguish informative signals from noisy or redundant ones. Reliability is therefore a natural conceptual neighbor of the present problem, but it is not equivalent to Base-relative marginal value: a signal may be reliable yet add little when the base already represents overlapping information.

Conditional evidence use has also been studied outside recommendation. Yerragunta et al. propose dynamic conditional gating for question answering over incomplete knowledge bases, controlling how structured KB evidence and textual evidence are aggregated \cite{yerragunta2026gating}. Negative-transfer research provides a complementary warning against assuming that additional information is uniformly beneficial; Peng et al. show that multimodal pretraining can harm downstream sequential recommendation and develop a re-learning strategy to mitigate that effect \cite{peng2025negative}. These studies motivate conditional treatment of auxiliary information without implying the same mechanism as the one studied here.

A broader decision-theoretic connection is the value of information (VOI), which quantifies the benefit that additional information can provide to a decision, often through expected utility improvement \cite{abbas2025voi}. Our setting differs from classical information acquisition: the auxiliary source is not an unobserved feature whose revelation must be purchased before acting, but a fixed relationship-memory specialist whose ranking contribution is evaluated relative to an already trained base recommender.

Collectively, these lines of work address the representation, reliability, selection, conditional integration, or potential harm of auxiliary information. The present work instead measures the event-level marginal ranking utility of a fixed relationship-evidence specialist relative to a trained base. This operational comparison complements, rather than replaces, evidence-integration and evidence-selection methods; it does not identify an evidence source's intrinsic information value.

\subsection{Expert Routing, Selective Prediction, and Learning-to-Defer}

Selective prediction and Learning-to-Defer (L2D) provide the closest general decision-theoretic background. Mozannar and Sontag formulate L2D as jointly learning a predictor and a rejector that can defer decisions to an expert, with consistency results through cost-sensitive learning \cite{mozannar2020defer}. Narasimhan et al. study post-hoc deferral on top of a pretrained base model and optimize an accuracy--cost trade-off relative to an expert \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc}. Mao et al. develop stronger theoretical guarantees for single- and multiple-expert routing in both one-stage and two-stage settings \cite{mao2025mastering}, and recent work extends L2D to dynamically varying expert pools \cite{montreuil2026online}. Selective invocation of an expert, including on top of a fixed predictor, is therefore an established decision problem.

Recommendation also contains expert-style architectures. CeDRec, for example, uses adaptive in-context experts as components of a sequential representation \cite{sun2025cedrec}. Such systems adapt how representational experts contribute within the model, whereas the relationship-memory specialist studied here is maintained as a separate, transparent evidence source. The primary object of analysis is the specialist's ranking-utility difference relative to the base and its empirical variation across candidate conditions and historical-relationship states. Accordingly, the generic difficulty control in our experiments serves a narrower mechanistic purpose: it tests whether base weakness alone is sufficient to identify cases where the relationship-memory specialist helps, rather than standing in for the broader L2D literature.

\subsection{Live-Streaming and Repeat-Aware Recommendation}

Live-streaming recommendation introduces domain-specific dynamics that are absent from static-item settings: rooms become available and unavailable over time, viewers repeatedly return to channels, and interactions between viewers and streamers evolve during sessions. Zhang et al. model the two-sided dynamics of viewers and anchors through bi-directional prediction \cite{zhang2021bidirectional}. Rappaz et al. identify dynamic availability and repeat consumption as defining characteristics of live-stream recommendation and propose LiveRec to model them \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. Later work develops dynamic room representation and interaction-aware preference modeling \cite{gao2023driver,chen2024preference}, while hypergraph and graph-based approaches capture richer viewer--channel interactions and repeated behaviors \cite{yu2024hypergraph,zhu2025repeated}.

These studies establish that dynamic availability, repeated consumption, and viewer--streamer interactions are central to live recommendation. Live streaming is therefore not introduced here as a new source of repeat behavior, but as an empirical setting in which target-specific relationship evidence can be represented in the base-visible sequence, remain recoverable only from older history, or be unavailable while the candidate environment itself changes. KuaiLive and Twitch/LiveRec provide complementary environments for studying this Base-relative evidence value \cite{qu2026kuailive,rappaz2021liverec}.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

We formalize Base-relative evidence valuation as an event-level ranking-utility problem. The formulation distinguishes the offline evaluation of a fixed relationship-memory specialist from the pre-outcome decision of whether to invoke it. This distinction is central to the paper: the realized marginal benefit of the specialist can be measured only after the relevant target is observed, whereas a serving policy must act using information available beforehand.

\subsection{Recommendation Setting and Event-Level Ranking Utility}
\label{sec:event-utility}

For user $u$ at recommendation time $t$, let the pre-outcome decision context under evaluation regime $r$ be $\xi_r=(u,t,\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t},\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$, where $\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the candidate set under that regime and $\mathcal{H}_{u,t}$ is the interaction history available strictly before $t$. An evaluated event is $x_r=(\xi_r,y)$, where $y\in\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the observed relevant target. The underlying interaction instance $(u,t,y,\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$ may be held fixed while different candidate-set constructions produce distinct decision contexts and, hence, distinct full events. Ranking models receive the context but not $y$, which is used only for offline evaluation. For concision, the subscript $r$ on $\xi$ and $x$ is omitted when a regime is fixed.

Let $A$ be a ranking model and let $r_A(y\mid\xi)$ denote the 1-indexed rank assigned to $y$ among $\mathcal{C}_{u,t}$. For cutoff $K$, define the event-level ranking utility
\begin{equation}
    u_K(A,x)=
    \begin{cases}
        \displaystyle \frac{1}{\log_2\!\left(r_A(y\mid\xi)+1\right)}, & r_A(y\mid\xi)\le K,\\[6pt]
        0, & r_A(y\mid\xi)>K.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:event-ndcg}
\end{equation}
With one relevant target per event, Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg} is the event-level contribution to NDCG@$K$; aggregate NDCG@$K$ is its empirical mean over evaluation events. Because rank utility is defined relative to the event-specific candidate set, changing candidate construction can change Base-relative specialist value even when the underlying user--target history is unchanged. The formulation below requires only a scalar event-level utility; NDCG@$K$ is used because it is the primary ranking metric in our evaluation.

\subsection{Base-Relative Specialist Utility}
\label{sec:relative-utility}

Let $B$ denote a fixed base recommender and $M$ a fixed relationship-memory specialist. For a given selective-decision experiment, both rank the same event-specific candidate set and are fixed before the invocation policy is learned. We define the specialist-minus-base utility
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_m(x)=u_K(M,x)-u_K(B,x).
    \label{eq:delta-memory}
\end{equation}
A positive value indicates an event-level ranking gain from using the specialist instead of the base, zero indicates no marginal gain, and a negative value indicates that replacing the base ranking with the specialist ranking is harmful for that event. Importantly, $\Delta_m(x)$ is an \emph{operational} marginal value of persistent relationship evidence as instantiated by the fixed specialist $M$ relative to the fixed base $B$. It should not be interpreted as an intrinsic or model-independent information value of the underlying history. Its magnitude can depend on the base, specialist scoring rule, popularity component, ranking cutoff $K$, candidate environment, and operating regime. In particular, a positive difference does not by itself distinguish novel relationship information from differences in scoring behavior or inductive bias.

The realized $\Delta_m(x)$ is an offline supervisory quantity because computing it requires the observed target $y$ and the rankings produced by both $B$ and $M$. Selective specialist use must therefore estimate its conditional expectation from information available before $y$ is known.

\subsection{Conditional Relative Utility}
\label{sec:conditional-utility}

Let $R$ index the operating recommendation condition under which candidate construction, temporal availability, or related protocol elements are fixed, and let $Z$ denote the information available at invocation time. We condition on $R=r$ to describe a regime-specific event distribution; $R$ need not be an explicit feature of $Z$. The variable $Z$ is constructed only from pre-outcome information, such as user and history summaries, candidate-set descriptors, and confidence signals produced by the base recommender. Define the conditional specialist-minus-base utility as
\begin{equation}
    \eta_r(z)
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_m(X)\mid Z=z,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
When training and evaluating within a fixed regime, we suppress $r$ for notational simplicity. A learned estimator $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ scores events by expected specialist-minus-base value; Section~4 specifies its implementation.

Let $a(Z)\in\{0,1\}$ denote a pre-outcome selection policy, where $a(Z)=0$ uses the base ranking and $a(Z)=1$ uses the specialist ranking. This includes development-frozen score-threshold policies but excludes any dependence on the realized target. For an evaluated event $x$, define $u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(x;a)=(1-a)u_K(B,x)+a\,u_K(M,x)$. The expected improvement over always using the base satisfies
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}\!\left[u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(X;a)-u_K(B,X)\mid R=r\right] \\
    &\qquad=\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\Delta_m(X)\mid R=r\right]
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\eta_r(Z)\mid R=r\right].
    \label{eq:expected-selective-gain}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:expected-selective-gain} gives the decision rationale for estimating $\eta_r$: under a fixed regime, expected gain over the base is determined by which events are selected and their conditional relative utility.

The information boundary is strict. The observed target $y$, realized ranks, realized $\Delta_m(x)$, and the Base-relative evidence states used later for mechanism analysis are offline quantities and are excluded from $Z$. In particular, the represented, recoverable-but-unrepresented, and unavailable states are diagnostic variables rather than selector inputs. This formulation also distinguishes conditional relative utility from generic base difficulty: a difficulty score concerns the performance or uncertainty of $B$ alone, whereas $\eta_r(z)$ depends on the relative ranking utility of both $M$ and $B$. Base weakness therefore does not imply positive specialist-minus-base utility.

\subsection{Development-Frozen Selective Invocation and Matched-Budget Evaluation}
\label{sec:decision-rule}

The primary learned policy uses a score threshold determined without held-out test outcomes. Let $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ be fitted on the development split, with out-of-fold predictions used to select a development-only threshold $\tau_r$. The policy is
\begin{equation}
    a_{\tau_r}(z)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{\eta}_r(z)>\tau_r\right],
    \label{eq:utility-threshold}
\end{equation}
where the model, feature transformations, and threshold are fixed before the held-out test is evaluated. The threshold is selected by development ranking utility, with a deterministic tie rule favoring fewer invocations. This pointwise policy makes no claim of enforcing an exact test-set invocation budget.

To compare selectors without confounding their invocation rates, a matched-budget analysis uses the number $m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{\tau_r}(z_i)$ of invocations realized by the fixed utility policy on $n$ evaluation instances. The difficulty control then selects the $m$ largest predicted base-difficulty scores, with ties broken by stable instance index; the analysis-only Oracle selects the $m$ largest realized offline $\Delta_m(x_i)$ values. Both controls use the same $m$ as the learned utility policy. Thus, exact-budget Top-$m$ is an \emph{offline batch-comparison rule for controls}, not the serving rule of the primary utility policy or a method that assumes the future test batch is visible online. The realized Oracle upper bound is never available at decision time.

The ordering of predicted utility is sufficient to determine Top-$m$ selection for an offline equal-budget comparison. In contrast, the development-frozen threshold policy is sensitive to the absolute score scale and its transfer between splits. This distinction is particularly important when per-event utility is only imperfectly estimated.

A deployment system may additionally price the incremental cost of invoking the specialist. If $C_{\mathrm{inv}}\ge0$ denotes that cost and $\kappa_r(z)=\mathbb{E}[C_{\mathrm{inv}}\mid Z=z,R=r]$, the pointwise cost-sensitive benchmark is
\begin{equation}
    a_r^{*}(z)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\eta_r(z)>\lambda\kappa_r(z)\right],
    \qquad \lambda\ge0.
    \label{eq:cost-rule}
\end{equation}
A deployable version replaces the unknown $\eta_r$ by $\hat{\eta}_r$ and would require a separately specified cost model and threshold. We do not claim to optimize such a calibrated cost model in the present experiments. The selection principles in this subsection follow established post-hoc expert-routing ideas rather than constituting a new deferral theorem.

\subsection{Regime-Level Utility Decomposition}
\label{sec:regime-decomposition}

To analyze why aggregate specialist value changes across operating conditions, let $S$ denote a mutually exclusive and exhaustive partition of Base-relative relationship-evidence states, instantiated operationally in Section~4 and used only for analysis. Writing $\bar{\Delta}_r=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_m(X)\mid R=r]$, the law of total expectation gives
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\Delta}_r
    =\sum_s P(S=s\mid R=r)
    \,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_m(X)\mid S=s,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:regime-decomposition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:regime-decomposition} separates changes in evidence-state prevalence from changes in state-specific specialist utility. Both can vary across regimes. This decomposition describes conditional ranking outcomes, not causal effects of evidence visibility; a fixed specialist's measured advantage can decrease solely because the separately trained base improves. The identity is not a new theoretical result, and $R$ need not be a selector feature.

Sections~4--6 instantiate these quantities, describe their leakage-safe estimation and analysis, and evaluate the resulting selective decisions across recommendation regimes.


\section{Base-Relative Evidence Valuation Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

We instantiate the operational comparison of Section~\ref{sec:problem} using a trained sequential base recommender $B$, a separately specified relationship-memory specialist $M$, and a conditional relative-utility estimator. The base produces the default ranking, the specialist offers an alternative ranking from explicit creator-level history, and a pre-outcome selector determines which ranking to return. The design serves three separate scientific purposes. First, a fixed, credible base makes relative utility a meaningful operational comparison rather than a comparison with an intentionally weak reference. Second, a separately scored, transparent specialist allows persistent user--creator relationship evidence to be examined and decomposed without joint representation learning. Third, development-supervised relative-utility prediction tests whether differences in the specialist's observed ranking value can inform pre-outcome decisions. These choices support interpretable analysis rather than a claim of new fusion or deferral theory. Figure~\ref{fig:framework} distinguishes serving-time computation from offline supervision and diagnostic analysis.

\subsection{Strong Sequential Base Recommenders}
\label{sec:framework-base}

The base $B$ is a trained ranking model evaluated only on the candidate set permitted by the current recommendation regime. For KuaiLive, we use an identity-aware dual-branch sequential base (Dual-ID), combining room-identity and streamer-identity sequence encoders. Room identity represents an individual live session, whereas streamer identity provides continuity across sessions. For each candidate set, the two branches produce separately standardized score vectors. Their linear combination uses development-selected room and streamer weights of $0.1$ and $0.9$, respectively; the resulting scores rank eligible live rooms. Thus, the streamer branch is not itself a change of the prediction target from rooms to creators. Candidate construction, within-candidate normalization, and the resulting base-confidence descriptors are regime dependent. This base serves as the fixed candidate-regime reference for computing relative utility; its same-protocol comparator evidence is presented with the experiments rather than treated as a new architecture claim.

For Twitch/LiveRec, we retain the official availability-aware, repeat-aware LiveRec architecture \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. Its contextual and repeat-consumption components are both enabled (the official \texttt{fr\_ctx} and \texttt{fr\_rep} switches), and the primary input contains at most $16$ preceding interactions. Eligibility follows the platform's time-dependent availability: a streamer is ranked only when available at the recommendation step. The two platforms therefore use different strong base instantiations, while the definition of $\Delta_m$ remains unchanged. Base checkpoints and development-selected settings are fixed within each specialist-selection experiment. Comparisons among context lengths use separately trained base checkpoints; they are not treated as a manipulation of input visibility alone.

\subsection{Transparent Relationship-Memory Specialist}
\label{sec:framework-memory}

The specialist $M$ scores eligible candidates from historical user--creator interactions available before the target time. Let $i$ denote a candidate creator, let $n_{u,i}(t)$ be its count in the eligible pre-target history, and let $n_{u,\max}(t)$ be the largest such creator-specific count for user $u$. Define a long-term relationship strength as $\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)=n_{u,i}(t)/\max\{1,n_{u,\max}(t)\}$. For the ordered last $K_s=10$ eligible user--creator interactions, let $d$ be the reverse interaction index ($d=0$ for the most recent). The short-term component is $\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)=\max_{\{d:i_d=i\}}\exp(-d/3)$, with a maximum over an empty set defined as zero. Hence repeated appearances contribute their most recent decayed strength rather than an unbounded sum.

The popularity component $\mathrm{Pop}(i)$ is derived from eligible training-split creator counts by applying $\log(1+n_i)$ and min--max scaling over observed creators; unseen creators receive zero. The fixed specialist score is
\begin{equation}
s_M(u,i,t)=
0.45\,\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)
+0.45\,\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)
+0.10\,\mathrm{Pop}(i).
\label{eq:memory-scoring}
\end{equation}
The specialist scoring structure, component weights, short-term horizon, and decay are carried into Twitch without test-driven retuning. The Twitch dataset is formed from ten-minute snapshots of users observed in streamer chats: \emph{start} and \emph{stop} denote the first and last observed crawl steps, not verified entry and exit instants \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. In the primary development and held-out exports, pre-target history contains records whose first observed step strictly precedes the target step. Popularity uses records whose last observed step precedes the training split boundary. An auxiliary strict-split analysis additionally restricts its eligible history pool by the last observed step before the corresponding split endpoint. These are different retrospective eligibility protocols, and neither establishes that all earlier-observed interactions had ended before the target. The ten-minute observation granularity supports time-ordered offline replay but not claims of exact sub-interval real-time availability.

The ranking is formed by descending scores, with deterministic identifier-based tie-breaking as implemented for each platform. This rule matters particularly when multiple candidate rooms map to the same creator. For KuaiLive, room candidates inherit relationship components through their room-to-streamer mapping, so distinct rooms of the same creator may share the same memory score. In Twitch, candidates are streamers directly.

The three terms provide an inspectable mixture of relationship recency, persistent repetition, and global popularity; neither their weights nor the aggregation is a new learning architecture. In particular, the absence of a previous interaction with a target creator does \emph{not} imply that $s_M$ is zero, because popularity may still contribute. The specialist is evaluated separately from the sequential base rather than as a fused representation. Equation~\eqref{eq:memory-scoring} serves as a transparent, fixed evidence carrier; the resulting measured ranking gain remains specific to $B$, $M$, cutoff, and candidate regime.

\subsection{Base-Relative Relationship-Evidence States}
\label{sec:framework-states}

We operationalize the diagnostic variable $S$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:regime-decomposition} using the target creator and pre-target history. Let $V_L(x)$ indicate that the target creator occurs in the sequence visible to a base with input length $L$, and let $H(x)$ indicate that the same creator occurs anywhere in the eligible pre-target relationship history. Then
\begin{equation}
S_L(x)=
\begin{cases}
\text{represented},&V_L(x)=1,\\
\text{recoverable but unrepresented},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=1,\\
\text{unavailable},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:diagnostic-states}
\end{equation}
These states are exhaustive and mutually exclusive. The second state is also referred to as \emph{long-horizon-only} and the first as \emph{recent-visible} in the evaluation outputs. The classification is defined relative to the target creator and the particular base-visible sequence: it is not a fixed recency taxonomy shared across different input lengths or platforms. Here ``represented'' is retained as a short label for \emph{visible in the base input}, not for demonstrated internal representation learning. Appearing in the input does not establish that the model effectively encodes or exploits the relationship. The three labels are descriptive, not assumed to imply universal signs of specialist-minus-base utility.

Both indicators are target-relative and can only be evaluated retrospectively. The diagnostic state $S_L$, its target-specific relationship distance, realized target rank, and realized $\Delta_m(x)$ are excluded from the serving feature vector $Z$. The categories therefore support conditional analysis; they neither determine invocation nor constitute causal evidence that visibility alone changes utility.

\subsection{Observable Features and Conditional Relative-Utility Estimation}
\label{sec:framework-estimation}

The estimator $\hat\eta_r(z)$ is trained to predict the realized development-event target $\Delta_m(x)$ defined by Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta-memory}. Its inputs comprise only pre-outcome user/history summaries and base-ranking descriptors. In Twitch, six behavioral descriptors summarize the eligible history: log history length, repeat rate (one minus the fraction of distinct creators), normalized preference entropy, old-versus-recent Jensen--Shannon drift, circular temporal regularity, and composite history complexity. Regularity uses phases modulo $144$, corresponding to a daily cycle of ten-minute crawl steps. The eight base-score descriptors are candidate-score standard deviation and range, top-1 versus top-2, top-1 versus top-5, and top-10 versus top-11 score margins, top-score standardized value, normalized score entropy, and top-ten softmax mass. These descriptors use eligible-candidate scores; the top-10/top-11 margin requires at least $11$ candidates, checked in the frozen export. Neither score entropy nor softmax mass implies calibrated predictive uncertainty. Behavioral-history summaries must not be confused with the target-relative diagnostic state $S_L$.

The history-complexity descriptor combines percentile transforms of entropy, preference drift, and log history length with weights $0.4$, $0.4$, and $0.2$. The reference distributions are computed once from the full \emph{unlabeled} development feature columns before the five-fold OOF fitting; they are not refitted separately within each fold. The frozen development empirical distributions are reused when transforming test features, and no test outcomes, target identities, or target-relative diagnostic labels enter these transformations. Consequently, the OOF predictions exclude each held-out fold's outcomes from regressor fitting, but the outcome-free percentile reference is shared across the full development split.

For the primary selector we use histogram-based gradient-boosted regression (HGB) with $200$ boosting iterations, learning rate $0.05$, maximum depth $3$, minimum leaf size $50$, and $\ell_2$ regularization $1$. Five-fold shuffled out-of-fold (OOF) predictions on development events are used to search prediction-score quantile thresholds, including no-invocation and full-invocation endpoints, for the highest development mean switched NDCG@$10$; ties favor fewer invocations. This \emph{supervised threshold selection} uses realized development utilities and is not an unbiased estimate of policy performance. The final regressor is fitted on all development events, and its model, feature transformations, and chosen threshold $\tau_r$ are fixed before the untouched held-out test. Within KuaiLive, native candidate-regime policies use development-only fitting and threshold selection, whereas the strict sampled-active-to-full-active transfer keeps the sampled-candidate estimator and threshold fixed while recomputing candidate-dependent scores and descriptors. Twitch/LiveRec is a different form of validation: the specialist definition and fixed weights are reused, but a new conditional-utility estimator and its threshold are fitted on Twitch development data. We therefore test cross-platform replication of a methodological principle, \emph{not} zero-shot transfer of the learned selector. HGB and threshold routing are implementation choices rather than independent algorithmic novelty claims.

The estimator is assessed by held-out ranking utility under the development-frozen decision rule rather than pointwise regression accuracy alone. Threshold transfer also requires sufficient score-scale stability, unlike the ordering-only offline controls.

\subsection{Specialist Selection and Matched-Budget Controls}
\label{sec:framework-controls}

The primary selector uses the development-frozen threshold in Eq.~\eqref{eq:utility-threshold} to choose between two complete candidate rankings. Its features are observed before the target, and a conditional-execution implementation computes the specialist ranking only on invoked events. Offline evaluation instead computes both rankings for each event to obtain supervision and retrospective diagnostics.

Two controls characterize the learned selection. A \emph{base-difficulty} regressor uses the same observable feature family and HGB model family, but is supervised to predict $1-u_K(B,x)$ on development events. This target is computed offline; actual base loss is not provided to the selector at serving time. On the test set it selects, by an offline Top-$m$ operation, exactly as many events as the fixed utility policy happened to invoke, $m=\sum_i a_{\tau_r}(z_i)$. An \emph{Oracle} selects the same number of events by the realized specialist-minus-base utility $\Delta_m(x_i)$, yielding an analysis-only hindsight upper bound. Stable row-index tie-breaking makes these budget-matched controls deterministic. Neither control changes the frozen utility policy. Oracle uses test outcomes only for a nondeployable hindsight bound, while Difficulty selects by predicted—not realized—base loss.

Matched invocation counts ensure that the offline control comparison isolates event selection rather than invocation frequency. They do not impose a hard online budget on the pointwise Utility policy. Actual serving latency, cache footprint, and any cost optimization must be distinguished from these offline ranking comparisons.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

The experiments assess two complementary questions: how the ranking contribution of a fixed relationship-memory specialist varies across base-relative evidence states and operating regimes, and whether estimates of this contribution support more effective selective decisions than generic base-difficulty scores. We evaluate two live-streaming platforms with different recommendation units and availability protocols. We distinguish model development and frozen held-out evaluation from subsequent explanatory diagnostics, and interpret ranking gains as operational contrasts rather than intrinsic information value.

\subsection{Datasets and Evaluation Protocols}
\label{sec:exp-data}

\paragraph{KuaiLive.}
We use the public KuaiLive dataset \cite{qu2026kuailive}, focusing on the live-shopping interactions and associated live-room metadata. The prediction target is the next \emph{live room}; historical relationship evidence, in contrast, is attached to its \emph{streamer} and transferred to eligible rooms through an explicit room--streamer map. Distinct rooms of the same streamer thus remain distinct ranking items even when they share relationship-memory scores. After removal of duplicate user--room--timestamp records, we retain users with eligible preceding interactions and distinct development and test targets. The primary chronological leave-last-two-out export assigns each retained user's last interaction to test, the penultimate interaction to development, and earlier interactions to training. Both development and test contain 10,222 user-level target events. Test-time history includes the preceding development interaction; the popularity prior is estimated from training only.

Live-room eligibility uses metadata intervals: a room is active at time $t$ when its start timestamp is no later than $t$ and its end timestamp is later than $t$. Under the \emph{sampled-active} protocol, each observed target is ranked with 574 distinct active-room negatives, yielding 575 candidates. The \emph{full-active} protocol evaluates the same underlying user--time--target instance against every metadata-active room; the target is retained if metadata would otherwise exclude it, and such exceptions are recorded. Changing the candidate universe changes candidate-wise standardization and the evaluated ranking problem even if history and target are unchanged. A separate global-time protocol uses chronological windows across the platform, with different event eligibility and potentially multiple evaluation events per user. It is treated as temporal robustness evidence, not as a paired variant of the user-level leave-last-two-out data.

\paragraph{Twitch/LiveRec.}
The second platform follows the public Twitch benchmark and official LiveRec evaluation conventions \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. Its ranking items are streamers rather than individual rooms. User--streamer records are observed at discrete ten-minute crawl steps. Their start and stop fields indicate first and last \emph{observations}, not precisely known viewer arrival or departure times. The chronological train/development/test windows and candidate availability follow the frozen official protocol, in which an eligible streamer must be available at the target step. There is one observed relevant target per evaluation event. The primary base has at most 16 preceding sequence interactions, and the frozen exports contain 46,878 development events and 44,221 held-out test events.

For the primary relationship-memory replay, historical records enter the candidate-specific past when their first observed step precedes the target step; popularity uses records ending before the training boundary. A supplementary strict-split analysis additionally requires history records to end before the respective split boundary. These are distinct evidence-eligibility definitions; neither warrants a claim about exact within-step viewing times. Comparisons across the two platforms concern within-protocol Base-relative specialist utility rather than cross-platform comparisons of absolute NDCG.

\subsection{Baselines and Comparison Methods}
\label{sec:exp-comparators}

The KuaiLive reference is the Dual-ID sequential base described in Section~\ref{sec:framework-base}, whose room- and streamer-identity SASRec branches are independently standardized over the current candidates before weighted score combination. The room/streamer coefficients $0.1/0.9$ stated in Section~\ref{sec:framework-base} refer to the original development-selected sampled-active configuration. Native full-active models or a subsequent same-checkpoint candidate-swap diagnostic may use distinct, explicitly reported development-selected coefficients; they must not be treated as the same reference configuration. We additionally examine the room-only and streamer-only SASRec branches, a popularity comparator and common-candidate trained alternatives such as GRU4Rec and a contrastive sequential recommender, where available, to establish the credibility of the trained reference. These checks are protocol-matched comparator evidence, not an exhaustive state-of-the-art competition.

For Twitch we retain the official LiveRec contextual and repeat-consumption components, rather than substituting the KuaiLive architecture. In every within-protocol comparison, the fixed specialist $M$ and base $B$ rank the same eligible candidate set. The specialist retains the fixed components, history horizon, coefficients and tie policy of Section~\ref{sec:framework-memory}; held-out outcomes are not used to tune its parameters.

The decision comparisons comprise Always-Base, Always-Memory, the development-frozen Utility selector, a base-difficulty predictor and an analysis-only Oracle. The difficulty regressor predicts $1-u_{10}(B,x)$ from the same observable feature family as Utility. In the offline exact-count comparison, Difficulty selects the $m$ highest predicted-difficulty events, where $m$ is the number of test invocations realized by the frozen Utility threshold. Oracle uses the same $m$ but ranks events by the \emph{realized} $\Delta_m(x)$; it is explicitly nondeployable. These offline Top-$m$ controls do not alter the pointwise Utility policy or impose a hard online invocation budget.

\subsection{Training, Development, and Policy Freezing}
\label{sec:exp-freeze}

Training data are used to fit the specified base checkpoints and global popularity reference, while permitted fusion and selection choices use development information. Models re-trained for context-capacity stress tests or candidate-regime diagnostics are considered distinct reference models, even when architecture and dataset coincide. At decision time the selector can access only eligible past behavior, candidate information and the base's pre-outcome score descriptors. Neither the observed target nor the diagnostic relationship-evidence state is an invocation feature.

The relative-utility target $\Delta_m(x)$ is constructed offline for development examples by scoring the \emph{same} candidate set with both $B$ and $M$. Following Section~\ref{sec:framework-estimation}, the Utility regressor uses six history descriptors and eight base-confidence descriptors. The primary histogram-gradient-boosting configuration has 200 iterations, learning rate $0.05$, maximum depth $3$, minimum leaf size $50$, $\ell_2$ regularization $1$, and fixed random seed $20260918$. Five-fold shuffled out-of-fold (OOF) predictions on development data are used for supervised selection of a score threshold maximizing development mean switched NDCG@10, with ties resolved in favor of fewer specialist invocations. The final regressor is fitted on the complete development split.

For the composite history-complexity descriptor, percentile reference distributions are computed once from the full \emph{unlabeled} development features, shared across OOF folds, and transferred unchanged to test. OOF regressor fits therefore exclude the evaluation fold's outcome labels, although the outcome-free percentile reference is not separately rebuilt within each fold. Before canonical held-out evaluation, model parameters, the specialist, feature transformations, and threshold are frozen. Test predictions apply the fixed pointwise threshold without recalibration, and the realized invocation count fixes only the batch size of the offline Difficulty and Oracle controls. Because development OOF scores are used to select the threshold, the resulting development-selected score is not presented as an unbiased generalization estimate. Subsequent diagnostic work does not retrospectively alter the original frozen policy; this experimental freeze is not a claim of formal external preregistration.

\subsection{Candidate-Regime Comparability and Controlled Diagnostics}
\label{sec:exp-regimes}

We distinguish two estimands. The first compares historically evaluated sampled-active and native full-active KuaiLive regimes under their own development-defined models and eligible candidate universes. These experiments share user--time--target instances but may differ in candidate count, fusion coefficients and checkpoint provenance. The native full-active protocol learns its regime-specific choices from full-active development data; the strict-transfer comparison instead retains sampled-regime learned choices while recomputing candidate-dependent scores on full-active candidates. Consequently, aggregate differences across historical protocols are descriptive of operational regimes and cannot isolate candidate-set membership as their sole cause.

A supporting \emph{same-checkpoint candidate-swap diagnostic} controls the most direct model-configuration differences. It uses one newly trained, frozen pair of room- and streamer-identity checkpoints and a \emph{single} fusion coefficient chosen on sampled-active development data. For each matched test event, raw room and streamer scores are computed over the full-active candidate set once, and sampled candidates are recovered by subsetting those very scores. Candidate-wise branch standardization is applied separately to the two sets, according to the original ranking definition. The history and fixed Memory rule are identical on both sides. Input checks enforce event identity, a unique positive target, candidate-set inclusion, room--streamer mapping integrity and model-file hashes. A candidate-swap therefore changes the candidates and their induced score normalization, but not model weights, fusion coefficient, target or historical information.

For matched test instance $i$, we compute
\begin{equation}
D_i =
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})\big]
-
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})\big].
\label{eq:paired-regime-shift}
\end{equation}
The paired difference is evaluated overall and within the retrospective target-relative states of Eq.~\eqref{eq:diagnostic-states}. This test is a \emph{post-hoc protocol-controlled diagnostic}, not a randomized causal intervention or a second independent frozen-policy validation. Its outcome is reported regardless of effect direction. Historical and newly paired comparisons retain separate protocol identities and are never numerically pooled solely because they use the same source dataset.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:exp-statistics}

The primary ranking metric is NDCG@10, computed as the mean of the one-positive event utility in Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg}; HR@10 is secondary. We compare Base, Memory, and selected rankings within each protocol and report paired Memory--Base, Utility--Base and Utility--Difficulty differences. Invocation rate is the fraction of events assigned to Memory by the development-frozen Utility selector. Evidence-state means are decomposed by observed state prevalence and checked against the aggregate contrast; target-dependent states are used only retrospectively.

Uncertainty for ranking differences is quantified with paired percentile bootstrap intervals, resampling users so that all observations from the same user remain together. In one-target-per-user protocols the user and event bootstrap units coincide; global-time multi-event analyses use user-level clustering. The new same-checkpoint comparison uses 3,000 paired user-bootstrap replicates; other frozen evaluations follow their recorded bootstrap settings. Repeated-seed variation reflects independently trained models and is reported separately from within-checkpoint sampling uncertainty. Fine-grained state, distance, component, and context-capacity contrasts are explanatory or exploratory rather than unqualified multiplicity-free confirmatory tests. In particular, context lengths $8$, $16$, and $32$ compare separately trained LiveRec bases against an unchanged specialist, so context visibility is not isolated from accompanying changes in base ranking quality.

Efficiency is assessed through invocation frequency and reported latency, throughput and model/cache footprint under specified batch sizes, hardware and measurement conditions. Offline ranking comparisons do not measure causal changes in viewer satisfaction, watch time, sales or conversion, and we do not claim a calibrated cost-optimal serving policy.

\subsection{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:exp-implementation}

The KuaiLive sequential references use pinned ReChorus SASRec code with 64-dimensional embeddings, one self-attention layer, four attention heads, and a maximum history of 50. The Twitch experiments use the official LiveRec implementation with contextual and repeat-consumption components enabled. Full training schedules, optimizer settings, random seeds, source revisions, chronology and candidate generation, model/policy hashes, evaluation scripts and statistical configurations are recorded per protocol in reproducibility manifests. The newly trained same-checkpoint diagnostic is not asserted to reproduce an expired historical checkpoint merely because model architecture and data preparation coincide.

Supplementary materials document dataset provenance, exact eligibility rules, comparator hyperparameters, alternative temporal and history protocols, seed and ablation matrices, efficiency environments and artifact-to-claim traces. At submission, data and code availability statements will distinguish published source data, re-runnable scripts and actually retained model or result files; temporary hosted artifacts are not treated as permanent public archives without verification.
