\section{Introduction}

Sequential recommenders learn preferences from interaction histories, with self-attentive models such as SASRec and BERT4Rec strengthening the representation of recent behavior \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. Persistent user--creator relationships may nevertheless extend beyond the histories available to an incumbent ranker. Some of these relationships may already be reflected in its learned scores, whereas others may provide complementary information. Their availability alone therefore does not determine whether additional historical evidence will improve a ranking decision. The central question is \emph{when a specified relationship-knowledge specialist contributes positive ranking utility relative to the trained incumbent on the same eligible candidates}.

Research on auxiliary recommendation knowledge has developed methods for integrating collaborative, structural and temporal evidence \cite{xie2026mhgfcl,zhu2026mifusr}. Collaborative-memory systems also extend how relationships can be represented and supplied to recommendation models \cite{chen2026memrec}. These advances motivate a complementary evaluation of knowledge use: for an incumbent Base and a separately scored, fixed relationship-memory specialist, which requests benefit from the alternative ranking, and can that benefit be anticipated before the target outcome is known? The event-wise difference in ranking utility provides an operational measure of this contribution. Established expert-choice and post-hoc deferral methods inform selective allocation \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc,mao2025mastering}; the present study examines their decision logic for a concrete historical-knowledge source whose advantage depends on both the incumbent and the ranking environment.

Live-streaming recommendation provides an informative setting because user--creator relationships persist while eligible alternatives vary over time. Twitch/LiveRec recommends available streamers and incorporates repeated-consumption behavior \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. KuaiLive additionally distinguishes transient live rooms from persistent streamer identities \cite{qu2026kuailive}. These tasks expose two related dimensions of knowledge value: whether relationship history complements the Base's available input, and how the candidate set changes the specialist's measured ranking contribution. Target-relative history states characterize observed outcomes retrospectively; the proposed selector instead uses information available before each recommendation outcome.

We examine these dimensions through complementary empirical analyses. On frozen Twitch TEST, relationship Memory is inferior to Base in aggregate but provides substantial average benefit for targets whose eligible relationships remain recoverable beyond the Base input window. A selector trained to predict Memory's advantage over Base improves held-out ranking relative to Base and a retrospectively invocation-matched Difficulty control. On paired KuaiLive events, changing sampled-active to full-active candidates reverses the ranking comparison of fixed Base and Memory models. The Twitch analysis thus evaluates knowledge-guided specialist choice, while KuaiLive shows why the measured value of a knowledge source must be interpreted under the relevant candidate environment.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operational valuation of relationship evidence (C1).} We evaluate the incremental ranking utility of a fixed relationship specialist against a designated trained Base and identify the conditional contribution of recoverable relationship history.
    \item \textbf{Candidate-regime dependence (C2).} We characterize the change in specialist-relative utility under paired KuaiLive candidate protocols, distinguish ranked-candidate membership from score-standardization reference in a descriptive allocation, and assess directional stability within the evaluated cohort.
    \item \textbf{Relative-utility-guided decisions (C3).} We estimate specialist-minus-Base utility from pre-outcome descriptors and evaluate a DEV-frozen Twitch selection policy against Base and a retrospectively equal-count Base-Difficulty comparator.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Historical Representation and Auxiliary Knowledge}

Sequential recommendation research has progressively expanded the information captured from interaction histories. SASRec and BERT4Rec learn self-attentive sequential representations \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}; intent-aware augmentation and multi-granular interest modeling further enrich history-dependent preferences \cite{ni2025idmarec,sheng2025dsmgrec}. These methods strengthen an incumbent's ability to use observed behavior. They also make the marginal contribution of additional history an empirical question: evidence that is informative in isolation may overlap with patterns already reflected in the incumbent's ranking.

Knowledge-enhanced recommendation addresses complementary information through fusion, selection and reliability modeling. Multi-head gating of collaborative and structural evidence \cite{xie2026mhgfcl}, mutual-information-based feature integration \cite{zhu2026mifusr}, collaborative denoising \cite{zhang2026mcd4sr} and knowledge-path selection \cite{kim2026kaper} improve how auxiliary signals enter recommendation. MemRec constructs and distills a collaborative memory graph for an LLM recommender \cite{chen2026memrec}, showing how relational information can be synthesized into usable context. Reliability-aware learning \cite{huo2026reliability} and work on negative transfer \cite{peng2025negative} further establish that the quality and consequences of auxiliary information require attention. Conditional source gating has also been examined for incomplete knowledge bases \cite{yerragunta2026gating}. These approaches principally study evidence representation, delivery or integration; the present comparison measures the ranking improvement obtained by substituting a specified relationship specialist for an already trained Base.

Decision-theoretic value-of-information analysis provides a broader conceptual connection \cite{abbas2025voi}. Here, value is instantiated as an observed difference in recommendation utility under a fixed model pair and eligible candidate set. It is consequently sensitive to the incumbent's existing capabilities and the alternatives being ranked, complementing studies that seek more informative or more reliable knowledge representations.

\subsection{Selective Prediction and Specialist Allocation}

Learning-to-Defer establishes decision rules for choosing between a predictive model and an expert. Consistent estimators \cite{mozannar2020defer} and post-hoc deferral for previously trained predictors \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc} provide foundations for allocating requests according to comparative prediction quality and cost; subsequent work extends the setting to multiple experts and varying expert availability \cite{mao2025mastering,montreuil2026online}. Adaptive expert networks are also used in sequential recommendation \cite{sun2025cedrec}. Accordingly, using an estimated advantage to choose an expert belongs to an established methodological family.

The unresolved empirical question for historical relationship knowledge is which decision target identifies occasions when a \emph{particular} alternative ranking is beneficial. Incumbent difficulty alone describes the expected weakness of Base, whereas specialist-relative utility compares what Base and Memory would each contribute on the same event. GUIDER uses data and model uncertainty to adjust LLM-based recommendation re-ranking \cite{xu2026guider}; its uncertainty-guided adaptation illustrates the relevance of conditional routing but targets a different decision signal. Our Twitch evaluation tests relative-utility prediction against a Base-Difficulty score using the same pre-outcome feature family and the same realized number of specialist calls. The Difficulty selection is retrospectively count-matched, while the Utility threshold is fixed on development data before TEST. This comparison studies the selection of a fixed historical-knowledge specialist, without proposing a new general deferral algorithm.

\subsection{Live-Streaming Recommendation and Candidate-Context Evaluation}

Live-streaming recommendation must connect persistent interests with transient viewing opportunities. Viewer--anchor interactions, dynamic rooms, behavioral preferences, hypergraph representations and repeated consumption have been modeled in prior work \cite{zhang2021bidirectional,gao2023driver,chen2024preference,yu2024hypergraph,zhu2025repeated}. LiveRec incorporates temporal availability and repeat behavior \cite{rappaz2021liverec}; KuaiLive makes room sessions and enduring streamer identities explicit \cite{qu2026kuailive}. DCGLive captures evolving room--streamer--user collaboration \cite{guo2026dcglive}. These approaches motivate strong task-appropriate Bases and a relationship specialist grounded in persistent creator identity rather than a replacement architecture for room dynamics.

Candidate construction also determines which ranking comparison is measured. Krichene and Rendle show that sampled ranking metrics need not preserve the relative ordering obtained with a full candidate set \cite{krichene2020sampled}. The KuaiLive analysis examines a related consequence for knowledge use: whether a fixed relationship-memory alternative improves on its designated incumbent can change when the same user--target event is ranked against different eligible alternatives. Its crossed candidate-membership and score-reference comparison describes the observed difference within that protocol, rather than isolating a causal candidate-count effect.

Together, these literatures motivate an operational knowledge-decision perspective: evaluate what a fixed relationship specialist adds beyond a trained incumbent, characterize the event and ranking contexts under which that contribution changes, and test whether pre-outcome estimates support selective use. The contribution lies in connecting these measurements and decisions for historical relationship evidence, with separate evidence for frozen Twitch selection and paired KuaiLive candidate-context dependence.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

An auxiliary relationship specialist is valuable for a recommendation event insofar as its ranking improves on the incumbent's result. We define this event-level contribution under a specified candidate regime, then relate its conditional expectation to selection decisions and changes in evaluation context.

\subsection{Recommendation Setting and Event-Level Ranking Utility}
\label{sec:event-utility}

For user $u$ at recommendation time $t$, let the pre-outcome decision context under evaluation regime $r$ be $\xi_r=(u,t,\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t},\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$, where $\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the candidate set under that regime and $\mathcal{H}_{u,t}$ is the interaction history available strictly before $t$. An evaluated event is $x_r=(\xi_r,y)$, where $y\in\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the observed relevant target. The underlying interaction instance $(u,t,y,\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$ may be held fixed while different candidate-set constructions produce distinct decision contexts and, hence, distinct full events. Ranking models receive the context but not $y$, which is used only for offline evaluation. For concision, the subscript $r$ on $\xi$ and $x$ is omitted when a regime is fixed.

Let $A$ be a ranking model and let $r_A(y\mid\xi)$ denote the 1-indexed rank assigned to $y$ among $\mathcal{C}_{u,t}$. For cutoff $K$, define the event-level ranking utility
\begin{equation}
    u_K(A,x)=
    \begin{cases}
        \displaystyle \frac{1}{\log_2\!\left(r_A(y\mid\xi)+1\right)}, & r_A(y\mid\xi)\le K,\\[6pt]
        0, & r_A(y\mid\xi)>K.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:event-ndcg}
\end{equation}
With one relevant target per event, Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg} is its NDCG@$K$ contribution; the mean across events is aggregate NDCG@$K$. Candidate construction enters the metric through the ranked alternatives, allowing the same user--target interaction to have different ranking utilities under different regimes.

\subsection{Base-Relative Specialist Utility}
\label{sec:relative-utility}

Let $B$ denote a fixed base recommender and $M$ a fixed relationship-memory specialist. For a given selective-decision experiment, both rank the same event-specific candidate set and are fixed before the invocation policy is learned. We define the specialist-minus-base utility
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_M(x)=u_K(M,x)-u_K(B,x).
    \label{eq:delta-memory}
\end{equation}
The sign of $\Delta_M(x)$ states whether the specialist improves, ties or worsens Base's ranking for the evaluated request. This is an \emph{operational} measure relative to a specified model pair and candidate regime: history acquires value through the improvement in the decision it enables.

The realized $\Delta_M(x)$ is an offline supervisory quantity defined using the observed target $y$ and both candidate rankings. Decision-time selection instead relies on the expected difference given observable information.

\subsection{Conditional Relative Utility}
\label{sec:conditional-utility}

Let $R$ index the operating recommendation condition under which candidate construction, temporal availability, or related protocol elements are fixed, and let $Z$ denote the information available at invocation time. We condition on $R=r$ to describe a regime-specific event distribution; $R$ need not be an explicit feature of $Z$. The variable $Z$ is constructed only from pre-outcome information, such as user and history summaries, candidate-set descriptors, and confidence signals produced by the base recommender. Define the conditional specialist-minus-base utility as
\begin{equation}
    \eta_r(z)
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid Z=z,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
Within a fixed regime we suppress $r$ when unambiguous. An estimator $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ uses the available descriptors to predict specialist-minus-base ranking value before the event outcome.

Let $a(Z)\in\{0,1\}$ denote a pre-outcome selection policy, where $a(Z)=0$ uses the base ranking and $a(Z)=1$ uses the specialist ranking. This includes development-frozen score-threshold policies but excludes any dependence on the realized target. For an evaluated event $x$, define $u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(x;a)=(1-a)u_K(B,x)+a\,u_K(M,x)$. The expected improvement over always using the base satisfies
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}\!\left[u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(X;a)-u_K(B,X)\mid R=r\right] \\
    &\qquad=\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\Delta_M(X)\mid R=r\right]
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\eta_r(Z)\mid R=r\right].
    \label{eq:expected-selective-gain}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:expected-selective-gain} connects knowledge valuation to the selection objective: expected ranking improvement depends jointly on the chosen events and their conditional specialist-relative utility.

Decision-time predictors use $Z$; target-defined history states, recurrence distances and realized relative utility are available only for retrospective diagnosis or supervision. Base Difficulty concerns $B$ alone, whereas $\eta_r$ evaluates the advantage of substituting $M$. A poor Base ranking therefore need not imply a profitable specialist substitution.

\subsection{Selective Invocation and Equal-Count Comparison}
\label{sec:decision-rule}

Given $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$, a threshold selects the specialist whenever its predicted incremental value exceeds the development-selected operating threshold $\tau_r$:
\begin{equation}
    a_{\tau_r}(z)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{\eta}_r(z)>\tau_r\right],
    \label{eq:utility-threshold}
\end{equation}
where a selected event uses $M$ and an unselected event uses $B$. The threshold is applied to requests individually; its realized invocation rate depends on the evaluated context distribution.

For a set of $n$ evaluated requests, the frozen policy produces $m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{\tau_r}(z_i)$ specialist calls. Comparisons at this observed count assess the quality of event selection separately from invocation frequency; the comparator's batch-level information access is specified in Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparators}.

\subsection{Regime-Level Utility Decomposition}
\label{sec:regime-decomposition}

A change in aggregate specialist value can reflect a changed distribution of evidence states, changed state-conditional ranking value, or both. Let $S$ be an exhaustive partition of target-relative relationship states, operationalized in Section~\ref{sec:framework-states}. With $\bar{\Delta}_r=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_M(X)\mid R=r]$, total expectation gives
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\Delta}_r
    =\sum_s P(S=s\mid R=r)
    \,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid S=s,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:regime-decomposition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:regime-decomposition} decomposes observed knowledge value into state prevalence and state-conditional ranking utility. Its interpretation remains relative to the specified incumbent and candidate environment; the paired evaluation designs distinguish these sources of variation.

\label{sec:framework}

The framework implements relative-utility valuation through three complementary components (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}): a trained Base that supplies the default ranking, a fixed relationship-memory specialist that supplies the alternative, and a predictor of their expected utility difference constructed from decision-time information. Observed targets provide learning and diagnostic evidence only in offline analysis.

\subsection{Strong Sequential Base Recommenders}
\label{sec:framework-base}

KuaiLive uses a Dual-ID sequential Base with room-level and streamer-level SASRec branches. The room branch represents individual live sessions; the streamer branch connects sessions involving one creator. Both score the eligible \emph{rooms}, and their within-candidate standardized scores are fused by development-selected coefficients. This preserves next-room ranking while exploiting persistent streamer identity as part of the incumbent's own representation. Fusion coefficients are selected separately for the experimental candidate protocols.

For Twitch/LiveRec, Base is the availability-aware LiveRec recommender \cite{rappaz2021liverec}, including context and repeat-consumption components (input length 16). The two platforms instantiate the same Base-relative utility criterion with distinct ranking units and incumbent architectures, making the role of the designated Base explicit in each comparison.

\subsection{Transparent Relationship-Memory Specialist}
\label{sec:framework-memory}

The specialist $M$ scores eligible candidates from historical user--creator interactions available before the target time. Let $i$ denote a candidate creator, let $n_{u,i}(t)$ be its count in the eligible pre-target history, and let $n_{u,\max}(t)$ be the largest such creator-specific count for user $u$. Define a long-term relationship strength as $\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)=n_{u,i}(t)/\max\{1,n_{u,\max}(t)\}$. For the ordered last $K_s=10$ eligible user--creator interactions, let $d$ be the reverse interaction index ($d=0$ for the most recent). The short-term component is $\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)=\max_{\{d:i_d=i\}}\exp(-d/3)$, with a maximum over an empty set defined as zero. Hence repeated appearances contribute their most recent decayed strength rather than an unbounded sum.

The popularity component $\mathrm{Pop}(i)$ is derived from eligible training-split creator counts by applying $\log(1+n_i)$ and min--max scaling over observed creators; unseen creators receive zero. The fixed specialist score is
\begin{equation}
s_M(u,i,t)=
0.45\,\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)
+0.45\,\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)
+0.10\,\mathrm{Pop}(i).
\label{eq:memory-scoring}
\end{equation}

The same scoring coefficients, short-history horizon and decay are used on both platforms; a candidate without prior interaction may still receive a nonzero popularity score. Candidates are ordered by score with deterministic identifier-based tie-breaking. In KuaiLive, distinct rooms mapped to one creator can consequently share a Memory score; Twitch candidates are streamers directly. The eligibility of pre-target records and platform-specific timing conventions are given in Section~\ref{sec:exp-data}. The fixed specialist defines an interpretable alternative ranking against which the incremental value of relationship evidence can be measured.

\subsection{Base-Relative Relationship-Evidence States}
\label{sec:framework-states}

We operationalize the diagnostic variable $S$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:regime-decomposition} using the target creator and pre-target history. Let $V_L(x)$ indicate that the target creator occurs in the sequence visible to a base with input length $L$, and let $H(x)$ indicate that the same creator occurs anywhere in the eligible pre-target relationship history. Then
\begin{equation}
S_L(x)=
\begin{cases}
\text{represented},&V_L(x)=1,\\
\text{recoverable but unrepresented},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=1,\\
\text{unavailable},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:diagnostic-states}
\end{equation}

These target-relative states describe input visibility for retrospective evaluation. In particular, \emph{represented} denotes membership in the Base input window rather than an assumption about what its trained representation has learned. The selector's observable vector $Z$ excludes $S_L$, target-specific recurrence distance and realized $\Delta_M$.

\subsection{Observable Features and Conditional Relative-Utility Estimation}
\label{sec:framework-estimation}

The estimator $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ predicts the development-event utility difference $\Delta_M(x)$ from pre-outcome history and Base-score descriptors. Six history features summarize log history length, creator repeat rate, normalized preference entropy, old-versus-recent Jensen--Shannon drift, circular temporal regularity, and composite history complexity. On Twitch, regularity uses phases modulo $144$ ten-minute steps, representing a daily cycle. Eight Base-score features comprise score standard deviation and range, top-1/top-2, top-1/top-5 and top-10/top-11 margins, the top score's standardized value, normalized score entropy, and top-ten softmax mass. These quantities summarize candidate ranking patterns; entropy and softmax mass are not assumed to be calibrated uncertainty estimates.

The composite history-complexity descriptor weights percentiles of entropy, preference drift and log history length by $0.4$, $0.4$ and $0.2$. The transformations use pre-outcome features and preserve the target-independent decision context. Reference-pool construction and estimator fitting follow the split-specific protocol in Section~\ref{sec:exp-freeze}.

\subsection{Specialist Selection and Matched-Budget Controls}
\label{sec:framework-controls}

For each request, Base scores form part of $Z$, and the utility estimator predicts whether the separately scored Memory ranking is expected to improve the decision. Equation~\eqref{eq:utility-threshold} then returns either the incumbent or specialist ranking, making historical knowledge use conditional on predicted relative benefit.

The competing Base-Difficulty objective predicts $1-u_K(B,x)$ from the same pre-outcome feature family, thereby separating incumbent difficulty from specialist-relative benefit. Their evaluation, including the retrospective matched-count comparator and hindsight Oracle, is defined in Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparators}.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

The two datasets evaluate complementary aspects of specialist-relative value. Twitch tests whether pre-outcome utility estimates improve a frozen ranking policy; KuaiLive evaluates how that value changes with the eligible candidate environment for paired user events and fixed rankers. Table~\ref{tab:exp-datasets} specifies their ranking units, chronological splits and candidate protocols.

\subsection{Datasets and Prediction Tasks}
\label{sec:exp-data}

\paragraph{KuaiLive.}
KuaiLive provides live-shopping interactions associated with time-bounded live-room sessions and streamer identities \cite{qu2026kuailive}. The task is next-\emph{room} recommendation; relationship-memory features are defined at the \emph{streamer} level and assigned to candidate rooms through the room--streamer mapping. The primary evaluation uses a chronological leave-last-two-out split per eligible user: earlier interactions form the training history, the penultimate event supplies the development target, and the final event supplies the test target. After eligibility filtering and interaction deduplication, development and test each contain one target for each of 10,222 users. At test time the preceding development interaction is part of the eligible history; the global streamer-popularity prior uses training interactions.

Room availability follows the recorded half-open session interval $[t_{\mathrm{start}},t_{\mathrm{end}})$. In the \emph{sampled-active} protocol, the observed target is ranked against 574 distinct active non-target rooms sampled without replacement; \emph{full-active} uses the contemporaneous active-room set and retains the target when the recorded session interval conflicts with its observed presence. These protocols share the same evaluated user--time--target events while differing in candidate membership and score-standardization reference. Sampled non-target rooms are evaluation negatives, potentially including previously visited rooms, rather than confirmed dislikes. The candidate protocols are therefore interpreted separately \cite{krichene2020sampled}.

\paragraph{Twitch/LiveRec.}
The second task recommends \emph{streamers} using the chronological, availability-aware Twitch/LiveRec protocol \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. User--streamer activity is recorded in ten-minute crawl steps; start and stop denote first and last observations rather than exact session boundaries. Candidate streamers are restricted to those available at the prediction step. The primary LiveRec base uses a maximum context of 16 interactions. Development and frozen TEST contain 46,878 and 44,221 one-positive events, respectively. The primary Memory history uses records first observed before the target step; its training popularity prior uses records ending before the training cutoff. A stricter split-end history eligibility rule is examined as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary S1). The recorded ten-minute observation intervals define the available temporal precision.

\subsection{Baselines and Compared Methods}
\label{sec:exp-comparators}

The designated KuaiLive Base is the Dual-ID sequential recommender (Section~\ref{sec:framework-base}), whose room and streamer branches are standardized on each eligible candidate set and combined with a development-selected fusion weight. Popularity, single-branch SASRec, GRU4Rec and ContraRec--BERT4Rec provide reference-competitiveness checks with documented, nonuniform tuning budgets (Supplementary S3). Twitch uses the official availability-aware LiveRec configuration with context and repeat-consumption components. Each platform's specialist contribution is computed against its \emph{designated trained Base}, rather than pooling absolute model metrics across the two prediction tasks.

Memory is the fixed Short--Long--Popularity ranking specialist of Eq.~\eqref{eq:memory-scoring}. The primary Twitch decision comparison evaluates always-Base, always-Memory and a DEV-fitted, DEV-thresholded Utility selector on frozen TEST. The Base-Difficulty comparator fits $1-u_{10}(B,x)$ using the same 14 pre-outcome descriptors, while the hindsight Oracle ranks events by realized $\Delta_M$. The Utility policy first determines its actual TEST invocation count $m$; Difficulty and Oracle are then assigned their top $m$ events over the TEST batch. Consequently, the equal-count comparison assesses \emph{which events} each score identifies, whereas the Utility selector alone is evaluated with a previously fixed pointwise threshold. The KuaiLive candidate-regime analysis evaluates the fixed Base--Memory ranking contrast independently of this Twitch policy test.

\subsection{Experimental Protocols}
\label{sec:exp-freeze}
\label{sec:exp-regimes}

\paragraph{Training and model selection.}
Base encoders are fitted on the training split, and their architecture hyperparameters and score-fusion coefficients are selected on development data. The Utility and Base-Difficulty regressors use the same 14 pre-outcome descriptors (Section~\ref{sec:framework-estimation}) with histogram gradient boosting. Five-fold out-of-fold (OOF) development predictions determine the Utility threshold by mean DEV ranking utility, breaking ties toward fewer Memory invocations. One-target-per-user protocols use shuffled KFold; the global-time KuaiLive analysis uses user-grouped folds for repeated users. For the primary Twitch selection test, the estimators are refitted on DEV and the Utility threshold and feature transformations are fixed before held-out TEST evaluation. KuaiLive candidate-regime findings instead use paired evaluation-protocol comparisons on an already examined TEST cohort. Twitch's history-complexity percentiles use an unlabeled DEV feature reference, reused on TEST; KuaiLive constructs split-specific percentiles from eligible preceding histories. Estimation and feature transformations are specified in Supplementary S2.

\paragraph{Candidate-regime comparisons.}
KuaiLive sampled-active and full-active protocols have distinct eligible candidate universes. Supplementary protocol comparisons include development-selected native configurations and a sampled-regime selector applied in the full-active setting. For the main paired ranking analysis, a single Room-SASRec/Streamer-SASRec checkpoint pair, sampled-development fusion coefficient and Memory scoring rule are held fixed. Raw scores are computed over the full-active universe and restricted to the paired sampled subset; each candidate set supplies its own branch-score standardization reference. Thus, the same user event, observed target, eligible history and fixed raw rankers are evaluated under two ranking environments. For event $i$, the paired shift is
\begin{equation}
D_i =
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})\big]
-
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})\big].
\label{eq:paired-regime-shift}
\end{equation}
The KuaiLive comparison uses the same previously examined TEST cohort as an evaluation-protocol diagnostic. Crossing ranked-candidate membership $C\in\{S,F\}$ with score-standardization reference $R\in\{S,F\}$ separates two protocol components while holding the original ranking scores fixed. With $g_i(C,R)$ the event-level Memory-minus-Base utility, the order-averaged allocations are
\begin{align}
A_{C,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(F,S)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(S,F)\big],\nonumber\\
A_{R,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(S,F)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(F,S)\big].
\label{eq:factorial-allocations}
\end{align}
By construction, $A_{C,i}+A_{R,i}=g_i(F,F)-g_i(S,S)=D_i$. This decomposition allocates the observed paired contrast within the fixed-score protocol; candidate membership combines list size, composition and competition. Three separately trained checkpoint pairs assess initialization sensitivity on the same cohort, while nine additional sampled-candidate draws are evaluated for the original fixed pair (Supplementary S5 and S8). Historical-eligibility and context-length checks examine complementary aspects of evidence availability.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:exp-statistics}

The principal metric is NDCG@10, computed from the single-positive event utility in Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg}; hit rate at 10 (HR@10) is secondary. We report mean ranking utility and paired differences for Memory versus Base, Utility versus Base, and Utility versus the equal-count Difficulty control. Specialist invocation rate is the proportion of events assigned to Memory. Evidence-state analyses use the retrospective partition of Eq.~\eqref{eq:diagnostic-states} and distinguish differences in state prevalence from differences in conditional utility.

Reported intervals use percentile bootstrap resampling of users or user clusters where repeated observations require clustering. Primary Twitch frozen TEST comparisons use 5,000 paired resamples; KuaiLive paired candidate evaluations use 3,000. The resulting uncertainty is conditional on the analyzed cohort, trained rankers and fixed selection masks. Variation across model initializations and candidate draws is assessed separately (Supplementary S2, S5 and S8).

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:exp-implementation}

KuaiLive Dual-ID uses ReChorus SASRec encoders (64-dimensional embeddings, one attention layer, four heads, maximum history 50); Twitch LiveRec uses input length 16 with context and repeat modules. The 14-feature Utility estimator is histogram gradient boosting (up to 200 iterations, learning rate $0.05$, depth $3$, minimum leaf size $50$, $\ell_2$ penalty $1$, seed $20260918$). Supplementary S2/S3 record software and model settings; S9 specifies the separate warmed CPU scoring protocol and its HGB gate.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

We examine three empirical aspects of relationship-memory value: its conditional contribution relative to Base, the decision gain from anticipating specialist advantage, and the effect of the ranked candidate environment. Development analyses further characterize sources of complementarity, followed by measured scoring costs.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Utility of Relationship Memory}
\label{sec:results-heterogeneity}

Relationship Memory is less effective than Base in aggregate, but its incremental value varies sharply across historical contexts. Across 44,221 frozen Twitch TEST events, always using Memory yields NDCG@10 $0.53980$, compared with $0.58211$ for Base, a paired difference of $-0.04232$ (95\% CI $[-0.04521,-0.03950]$). The retrospective states defined in Section~\ref{sec:framework-states} distinguish where the same fixed specialist improves or degrades the ranking.

Figure~\ref{fig:results-evidence-states} shows consistent state-wise directions on Twitch DEV and frozen TEST. For 5,539 \emph{recoverable-but-unrepresented} TEST targets, Memory exceeds Base by $+0.22045$ NDCG@10 on average; the represented and unavailable groups instead show differences of $-0.01935$ and $-0.18215$ (Table~\ref{tab:results-evidence-states}). The contrast identifies a substantial source of complementarity beyond the Base's input window despite the specialist's negative aggregate effect.

Because these evidence states are defined by the observed target, they diagnose conditional utility retrospectively. Whether that utility can guide an actual ranking choice depends on its predictability from pre-outcome event information.

\subsection{Decision Value of Predicting Relative Utility}
\label{sec:results-decision}

Predicting specialist-relative utility provides a decision rule for the heterogeneous value identified above. The Utility regressor and pointwise threshold were fitted and selected on Twitch DEV out-of-fold predictions, then fixed before the 44,221 TEST events. The resulting policy invokes Memory on 6,650 events (15.04\%).

The DEV-frozen Utility policy increases Twitch TEST NDCG@10 from $0.58211$ for Base to $0.58983$ (Table~\ref{tab:results-twitch-policy}). The paired gain is $+0.00772$, with 95\% CI $[+0.00641,+0.00903]$. HR@10 increases by $+0.00945$, with 95\% CI $[+0.00776,+0.01117]$. Both intervals use 5,000 user-paired bootstrap resamples with the policy decisions held fixed.

Relative-utility prediction also distinguishes profitable specialist calls from difficult Base requests. At the Utility policy's realized count of 6,650 calls, the DEV-trained Difficulty score yields NDCG@10 $0.58340$, leaving a Utility advantage of $+0.00644$ (paired 95\% CI $[+0.00528,+0.00762]$); the corresponding HR@10 difference is $+0.01196$ (95\% CI $[+0.01033,+0.01364]$). Difficulty chooses its top-$m$ events retrospectively over the TEST batch, whereas Utility applies the threshold fixed on DEV; the matched count compares the value of the selected events.

The realized TEST selection masks support this distinction: Memory-minus-Base NDCG@10 averages $+0.05136$ on the 6,650 Utility-selected events and $-0.05890$ on the remaining 37,571, compared with $+0.00855$ for Difficulty's equal-count selections. Complementary DEV out-of-fold diagnostics show recoverable-history enrichment of $2.72$ for Utility versus $1.67$ for Difficulty; Difficulty more strongly enriches unavailable histories ($1.91$ versus $1.03$; Supplementary S7). These target-defined states describe the resulting selections retrospectively.

The same-count hindsight Oracle reaches NDCG@10 $0.64806$ by selecting events using realized Memory-minus-Base utility, an improvement of $+0.06595$ over Base. Utility captures approximately 11.7\% of this available Oracle gain, with a predicted-versus-realized event-utility Spearman correlation of $0.173$. These results establish a positive selective-ranking benefit while quantifying the remaining predictive headroom for the fixed Base--Memory pair.

\subsection{Candidate-Regime Dependence of Relative Utility}
\label{sec:results-regimes}

Changing the eligible candidate environment reverses which of the two fixed rankers has higher measured utility. For the same 10,222 KuaiLive user--time--target events, sampled-active $S$ (575 rooms including the target) is nested within full-active $F$. Holding the checkpoint pair (seed 20260918), sampled-development fusion weight ($\alpha_{\mathrm{room}}=0.125$), histories, Memory rule and raw scores fixed, Memory-minus-Base NDCG@10 changes from $-0.04120$ on $S$ (Base $0.60709$, Memory $0.56589$) to $+0.04631$ on $F$ (Base $0.38571$, Memory $0.43202$). The paired change is $+0.08751$ (95\% CI $[+0.08210,+0.09326]$; 3,000 user-paired resamples). The ranking environment thus reverses which alternative is superior even though both models' absolute NDCG declines.

The crossed evaluation separates ranked-candidate membership $C$ from the reference set $R$ used to standardize Base branch scores (Table~\ref{tab:results-factorial}). With raw predictions fixed, Eq.~\eqref{eq:factorial-allocations} descriptively allocates $+0.08827$ of the paired shift to candidate membership (95\% CI $[+0.08287,+0.09397]$) and $-0.00076$ to score reference (95\% CI $[-0.00117,-0.00036]$). The two terms sum to $+0.08751$; off-diagonal cells represent analytical ranking configurations.

Candidate changes leave the retrospective target-history composition unchanged: 4,589 represented, 206 recoverable and 5,427 unavailable events. \subsection{Robustness and Alternative Explanations}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Two development analyses clarify the conditions of specialist complementarity. The first examines whether history and Base-score descriptors jointly predict relative utility; the second examines how Memory composition and the incumbent's visible history relate to that utility. The frozen TEST policy evaluation remains the independent decision evidence.

History descriptors and Base-score descriptors jointly provide stronger signals of specialist-relative benefit than either family alone. With 6,563 specialist calls on five-fold out-of-fold Twitch DEV predictions, the six history features yield a $+0.00372$ NDCG@10 gain over Base, the eight Base-score features $+0.00160$, and all 14 features $+0.00838$ (Table~\ref{tab:results-feature-ablation}). The combined features also lead at the other examined retrospectively matched DEV call counts (Supplementary Table~S7.1).

Component variation offers a complementary explanation of the state contrast. Across seven locally examined Memory parameter settings on Twitch DEV, relative utility remains positive for recoverable targets and negative for represented and unavailable targets (Supplementary S4). The pattern is stable within this development sensitivity analysis.

The composition of Memory matters even within the recoverable group. Among 5,879 Twitch DEV recoverable targets, Long-only Memory attains NDCG@10 $0.63890$, versus $0.53298$ for Full Memory and $0.30201$ for Base; Long-only exceeds Full by $+0.10592$ (Supplementary Tables~S4.1--S4.2). On unavailable targets, Long-only instead trails Full by $0.03208$. These contrasting effects relate specialist advantage to the historical signal it retains; the frozen TEST specialist remains Full Memory.

The incumbent's available history also changes the comparison. Separately trained Twitch DEV Bases with input lengths $L=8,16,32$ achieve NDCG@10 $0.52619$, $0.57185$ and $0.59663$, respectively, against an unchanged Memory score of $0.52170$ (Supplementary Table~S6.1). Where targets move from recoverable to represented, Memory's relative benefit declines as the corresponding trained Base improves. Because each Base is trained separately, the contrast reflects both access to history and model adaptation.

Target--creator recurrence distance provides an additional descriptive check: the Memory-minus-Base difference varies nonmonotonically across retrospective distance bins (Supplementary Table~S6.4), consistent with the absence of a simple monotonic relationship between elapsed interaction history and ranking benefit.

Together, these DEV results support two observed sources of utility variation: the joint predictive signal in available history and Base scores, and the changing complementarity between Memory components and the incumbent's trained history representation. Additional Base competitiveness checks are reported in Supplementary S3.

\subsection{Computational Considerations}
\label{sec:results-efficiency}

Selective use incurs prediction and alternative-ranking costs alongside its observed ranking benefit. The Twitch TEST Utility policy invokes Memory in 6,650 of 44,221 events (15.04\%), characterizing the frequency of specialist use rather than the latency of an end-to-end serving system.

In a separate warmed KuaiLive CPU scoring study, Base requires $0.991$\,ms on average (P95 $1.276$\,ms), while Selective HGB requires $2.007$\,ms (P95 $2.905$\,ms) at 575 candidates. The measured increase is $1.016$\,ms ($2.03$ times Base), with 14.25\% Memory invocation. This prepared-input, cached-history benchmark times gate inference and replays archived routing masks; it reports scoring overhead under its defined conditions (Supplementary S9).

Feature construction, gate inference and conditional Memory ranking all contribute to the additional work (Supplementary Table~S9.1, including the $81$-tree HGB configuration). The measured positive overhead distinguishes selective expert choice from a demonstrated reduction in serving cost.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Operational Value Is Base- and Context-Dependent}
\label{sec:discussion-operational-value}

The Twitch results reveal a form of conditional complementarity between historical relationship knowledge and the incumbent ranking model. Memory is weaker than Base when used indiscriminately, but it improves ranking substantially for targets whose eligible relationships remain recoverable beyond the Base's input window (Section~\ref{sec:results-heterogeneity}). The relevant property is therefore the \emph{incremental} benefit of the relationship specialist, assessed against what the trained Base already ranks effectively. Target-history states reveal where the observed rankings differ; the strongest recoverable-state gain is consistent with historical evidence supplying ranking information that is less accessible through the incumbent's limited input.

This complementarity also depends on the competing representations. Separately trained longer-context Bases improve their ranking while the fixed Memory ranking stays unchanged, and Long-only versus Full Memory displays different benefits across recoverable and unavailable targets (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}). The specialist's value is consequently a property of a particular Base--specialist comparison rather than of historical evidence in isolation. This view extends the motivation for evidence integration, reliability assessment and negative-transfer control \cite{zhu2026mifusr,huo2026reliability,peng2025negative} toward evaluating whether available knowledge makes a favorable ranking decision possible.

\subsection{Interpreting Conditional Evidence and Candidate Regimes}
\label{sec:discussion-candidate-regimes}

The candidate environment supplies another condition for knowledge value. In the paired KuaiLive comparison, Memory-minus-Base utility changes from $-0.04120$ with sampled-active candidates to $+0.04631$ with full-active candidates, although the user events, historical evidence and fixed ranking models remain the same (Section~\ref{sec:results-regimes}). Target-history composition is also identical across these regimes. Thus, a specialist's value cannot be inferred from evidence availability or historical-state prevalence alone: it also depends on the alternatives the specialist and Base are asked to rank.

Sampled evaluation is known to affect relative recommender comparisons \cite{krichene2020sampled}. The KuaiLive analysis makes this effect relevant to knowledge use by examining a fixed relationship specialist against its designated incumbent. Within the crossed scoring protocol, ranked-candidate membership accounts for most of the paired change, whereas the score-standardization reference contributes comparatively little (Section~\ref{sec:results-regimes}). Membership jointly changes candidate number, composition and ranking competition. The direction also persists across three trained checkpoint pairs and, for the original fixed pair, nine candidate draws (Supplementary S5 and S8). These observations support evaluating specialist value within an explicitly defined candidate universe before drawing conclusions about the usefulness of historical knowledge.

\subsection{From Relative Utility to Selective Decisions}
\label{sec:discussion-selective-decisions}

Conditional value becomes actionable when the selection criterion targets the advantage of the alternative ranking. Equation~\eqref{eq:expected-selective-gain} links expected gain over Base to the pre-outcome estimate of specialist-relative utility. A difficult request for Base is not necessarily one on which Memory performs better: Base Difficulty predicts the incumbent's loss, whereas Utility estimates the difference between the two available ranking decisions. The selection objective therefore follows directly from the knowledge-value question rather than from request difficulty alone.

The Twitch frozen TEST comparison supports this decision distinction. The Utility policy improves NDCG@10 by $+0.00772$ over Base and $+0.00644$ over the same-call-count Difficulty control (Section~\ref{sec:results-decision}). Utility uses a DEV-selected threshold; Difficulty is matched retrospectively by choosing its highest-scored TEST requests at Utility's realized count. DEV feature-family evidence further indicates that historical descriptors and Base-score characteristics supply complementary information for predicting relative benefit (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}). The ranking gains show that a useful selection order can be learned even when event-wise Utility estimates remain imperfect.

Learning-to-Defer already formalizes allocation between incumbent predictors and external experts \cite{mozannar2020defer,narasimhan2022posthoc}, and uncertainty-guided reranking provides another adaptive recommendation signal \cite{xu2026guider}. The present evidence connects these ideas to the measured contribution of a particular historical-relationship ranker under a defined ranking protocol. A TEST Spearman correlation of $0.173$ and recovery of approximately $11.7\%$ of the same-count hindsight Oracle gain leave substantial room for better prediction, while establishing that specialist-relative estimates can improve the observed allocation.

\subsection{Validity Boundaries}
\label{sec:discussion-validity}

The evidence has distinct inferential roles. The Twitch selector and threshold were fixed on DEV and evaluated once on held-out TEST events; the associated history-state descriptions and feature ablations explain its offline ranking behavior. KuaiLive characterizes candidate-regime dependence using previously examined paired TEST events, with checkpoint and candidate-draw variation assessed within that cohort. Target-defined history states are retrospective, while the Twitch selector uses only pre-outcome history and Base-score features. Both tasks evaluate one-positive rankings over their specified eligible candidates, so their NDCG@10 results measure protocol-dependent ranking quality.

Serving value additionally depends on actual computation. Twitch's $15.04\%$ Memory invocation rate reports selective use; a distinct warmed KuaiLive CPU replay instead measures $+1.016$\,ms selective scoring overhead relative to Base (Section~\ref{sec:results-efficiency}). That replay uses prepared inputs, cached histories and archived routing masks (Supplementary S9). Accordingly, external policy transfer and online performance require independently frozen evaluations and end-to-end measurements beyond the current offline evidence.

\subsection{Implications for Knowledge-Based Recommendation}
\label{sec:discussion-implications}

Together, the results establish a decision-oriented perspective on the use of historical relationship knowledge. A source that contains relevant information need not improve an incumbent ranking; its operational value lies in the incremental utility of a specified specialist on the same request and candidates. History-state heterogeneity reveals conditional complementarity, candidate-regime contrasts specify the ranking context in which that benefit is measured, and pre-outcome utility prediction supports selective use. These are connected aspects of a knowledge-based decision: assessing what additional evidence can contribute, identifying the conditions of that contribution and choosing when it should affect the output.

This perspective suggests evaluating knowledge modules against a strong, clearly specified incumbent and candidate protocol before adopting them as routine enhancements. Decisions to invoke a specialist should reflect predicted relative improvement alongside feature, routing and specialist-computation costs. Future work can independently test these rules under new candidate environments, separate candidate size from composition and hardness, and assess calibrated budget-aware policies with end-to-end latency and prospective user outcomes. Such evaluations would extend the present offline valuation principle to a broader range of knowledge-driven recommendation decisions.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our findings show that persistent relationship memory contributes to recommendation through conditional gains over a trained incumbent rather than uniform improvements from additional history. On Twitch, a fixed Memory specialist underperforms Base in aggregate but has a substantial positive mean advantage for targets whose eligible relationships are recoverable beyond the Base input window. This contrast identifies where historical evidence provides measurable complementary ranking value relative to the incumbent.

For held-out Twitch events, a pre-outcome Utility selector fitted and thresholded on DEV improves ranking over Base and a Difficulty comparator retrospectively matched to the same specialist-use count. The result distinguishes anticipating specialist advantage from identifying requests difficult for the incumbent. Complementary paired KuaiLive analyses show that changing the eligible candidate environment reverses the measured Memory-versus-Base advantage for fixed rankers and user events. This reversal remains directionally stable across the studied training initializations and candidate draws; the descriptive decomposition assigns most of the change to ranked-candidate membership.

Taken together, these results connect the valuation of historical knowledge to the decision to use it. A relationship-memory specialist has operational value relative to a particular incumbent and candidate universe; an estimate of that relative value, available before outcomes, can guide selective ranking. This provides an empirically supported, decision-oriented interpretation of knowledge augmentation in the evaluated offline recommendation settings.
