\section{Introduction}

Sequential recommenders learn preferences from interaction histories, with self-attentive models such as SASRec and BERT4Rec strengthening the representation of recent behavior \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. Persistent user--creator relationships may nevertheless extend beyond the histories available to an incumbent ranker. Some of these relationships may already be reflected in its learned scores, whereas others may provide complementary information. Their availability alone therefore does not determine whether additional historical evidence will improve a ranking decision. The central question is \emph{when a specified relationship-knowledge specialist contributes positive ranking utility relative to the trained incumbent on the same eligible candidates}.

Research on auxiliary recommendation knowledge has developed methods for integrating collaborative, structural and temporal evidence \cite{xie2026mhgfcl,zhu2026mifusr}. Collaborative-memory systems also extend how relationships can be represented and supplied to recommendation models \cite{chen2026memrec}. These advances motivate a complementary evaluation of knowledge use: for an incumbent Base and a separately scored, fixed relationship-memory specialist, which requests benefit from the alternative ranking, and can that benefit be anticipated before the target outcome is known? The event-wise difference in ranking utility provides an operational measure of this contribution. Established expert-choice and post-hoc deferral methods inform selective allocation \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc,mao2025mastering}; the present study examines their decision logic for a concrete historical-knowledge source whose advantage depends on both the incumbent and the ranking environment.

Live-streaming recommendation provides an informative setting because user--creator relationships persist while eligible alternatives vary over time. Twitch/LiveRec recommends available streamers and incorporates repeated-consumption behavior \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. KuaiLive additionally distinguishes transient live rooms from persistent streamer identities \cite{qu2026kuailive}. These tasks expose two related dimensions of knowledge value: whether relationship history complements the Base's available input, and how the candidate set changes the specialist's measured ranking contribution. Target-relative history states characterize observed outcomes retrospectively; the proposed selector instead uses information available before each recommendation outcome.

We examine these dimensions through complementary empirical analyses. On frozen Twitch TEST, relationship Memory is inferior to Base in aggregate but provides substantial average benefit for targets whose eligible relationships remain recoverable beyond the Base input window. A selector trained to predict Memory's advantage over Base improves held-out ranking relative to Base and a retrospectively invocation-matched Difficulty control. On paired KuaiLive events, changing sampled-active to full-active candidates reverses the ranking comparison of fixed Base and Memory models. The Twitch analysis thus evaluates knowledge-guided specialist choice, while KuaiLive shows why the measured value of a knowledge source must be interpreted under the relevant candidate environment.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operational valuation of relationship evidence (C1).} We evaluate the incremental ranking utility of a fixed relationship specialist against a designated trained Base and identify the conditional contribution of recoverable relationship history.
    \item \textbf{Candidate-regime dependence (C2).} We characterize the change in specialist-relative utility under paired KuaiLive candidate protocols, distinguish ranked-candidate membership from score-standardization reference in a descriptive allocation, and assess directional stability within the evaluated cohort.
    \item \textbf{Relative-utility-guided decisions (C3).} We estimate specialist-minus-Base utility from pre-outcome descriptors and evaluate a DEV-frozen Twitch selection policy against Base and a retrospectively equal-count Base-Difficulty comparator.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Historical Representation and Auxiliary Knowledge}

Sequential recommendation research has progressively expanded the information captured from interaction histories. SASRec and BERT4Rec learn self-attentive sequential representations \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}; intent-aware augmentation and multi-granular interest modeling further enrich history-dependent preferences \cite{ni2025idmarec,sheng2025dsmgrec}. These methods strengthen an incumbent's ability to use observed behavior. They also make the marginal contribution of additional history an empirical question: evidence that is informative in isolation may overlap with patterns already reflected in the incumbent's ranking.

Knowledge-enhanced recommendation addresses complementary information through fusion, selection and reliability modeling. Multi-head gating of collaborative and structural evidence \cite{xie2026mhgfcl}, mutual-information-based feature integration \cite{zhu2026mifusr}, collaborative denoising \cite{zhang2026mcd4sr} and knowledge-path selection \cite{kim2026kaper} improve how auxiliary signals enter recommendation. MemRec constructs and distills a collaborative memory graph for an LLM recommender \cite{chen2026memrec}, showing how relational information can be synthesized into usable context. Reliability-aware learning \cite{huo2026reliability} and work on negative transfer \cite{peng2025negative} further establish that the quality and consequences of auxiliary information require attention. Conditional source gating has also been examined for incomplete knowledge bases \cite{yerragunta2026gating}. These approaches principally study evidence representation, delivery or integration; the present comparison measures the ranking improvement obtained by substituting a specified relationship specialist for an already trained Base.

Decision-theoretic value-of-information analysis provides a broader conceptual connection \cite{abbas2025voi}. Here, value is instantiated as an observed difference in recommendation utility under a fixed model pair and eligible candidate set. It is consequently sensitive to the incumbent's existing capabilities and the alternatives being ranked, complementing studies that seek more informative or more reliable knowledge representations.

\subsection{Selective Prediction and Specialist Allocation}

Learning-to-Defer establishes decision rules for choosing between a predictive model and an expert. Consistent estimators \cite{mozannar2020defer} and post-hoc deferral for previously trained predictors \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc} provide foundations for allocating requests according to comparative prediction quality and cost; subsequent work extends the setting to multiple experts and varying expert availability \cite{mao2025mastering,montreuil2026online}. Adaptive expert networks are also used in sequential recommendation \cite{sun2025cedrec}. Accordingly, using an estimated advantage to choose an expert belongs to an established methodological family.

The unresolved empirical question for historical relationship knowledge is which decision target identifies occasions when a \emph{particular} alternative ranking is beneficial. Incumbent difficulty alone describes the expected weakness of Base, whereas specialist-relative utility compares what Base and Memory would each contribute on the same event. GUIDER uses data and model uncertainty to adjust LLM-based recommendation re-ranking \cite{xu2026guider}; its uncertainty-guided adaptation illustrates the relevance of conditional routing but targets a different decision signal. Our Twitch evaluation tests relative-utility prediction against a Base-Difficulty score using the same pre-outcome feature family and the same realized number of specialist calls. The Difficulty selection is retrospectively count-matched, while the Utility threshold is fixed on development data before TEST. This comparison studies the selection of a fixed historical-knowledge specialist, without proposing a new general deferral algorithm.

\subsection{Live-Streaming Recommendation and Candidate-Context Evaluation}

Live-streaming recommendation must connect persistent interests with transient viewing opportunities. Viewer--anchor interactions, dynamic rooms, behavioral preferences, hypergraph representations and repeated consumption have been modeled in prior work \cite{zhang2021bidirectional,gao2023driver,chen2024preference,yu2024hypergraph,zhu2025repeated}. LiveRec incorporates temporal availability and repeat behavior \cite{rappaz2021liverec}; KuaiLive makes room sessions and enduring streamer identities explicit \cite{qu2026kuailive}. DCGLive captures evolving room--streamer--user collaboration \cite{guo2026dcglive}. These approaches motivate strong task-appropriate Bases and a relationship specialist grounded in persistent creator identity rather than a replacement architecture for room dynamics.

Candidate construction also determines which ranking comparison is measured. Krichene and Rendle show that sampled ranking metrics need not preserve the relative ordering obtained with a full candidate set \cite{krichene2020sampled}. The KuaiLive analysis examines a related consequence for knowledge use: whether a fixed relationship-memory alternative improves on its designated incumbent can change when the same user--target event is ranked against different eligible alternatives. Its crossed candidate-membership and score-reference comparison describes the observed difference within that protocol, rather than isolating a causal candidate-count effect.

Together, these literatures motivate an operational knowledge-decision perspective: evaluate what a fixed relationship specialist adds beyond a trained incumbent, characterize the event and ranking contexts under which that contribution changes, and test whether pre-outcome estimates support selective use. The contribution lies in connecting these measurements and decisions for historical relationship evidence, with separate evidence for frozen Twitch selection and paired KuaiLive candidate-context dependence.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

We formalize the ranking contribution of a fixed relationship specialist relative to a trained incumbent. The event-level utility difference defines the quantity to be predicted for selective invocation and to be compared across candidate regimes.

\subsection{Recommendation Setting and Event-Level Ranking Utility}
\label{sec:event-utility}

For user $u$ at recommendation time $t$, let the pre-outcome decision context under evaluation regime $r$ be $\xi_r=(u,t,\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t},\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$, where $\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the candidate set under that regime and $\mathcal{H}_{u,t}$ is the interaction history available strictly before $t$. An evaluated event is $x_r=(\xi_r,y)$, where $y\in\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the observed relevant target. The underlying interaction instance $(u,t,y,\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$ may be held fixed while different candidate-set constructions produce distinct decision contexts and, hence, distinct full events. Ranking models receive the context but not $y$, which is used only for offline evaluation. For concision, the subscript $r$ on $\xi$ and $x$ is omitted when a regime is fixed.

Let $A$ be a ranking model and let $r_A(y\mid\xi)$ denote the 1-indexed rank assigned to $y$ among $\mathcal{C}_{u,t}$. For cutoff $K$, define the event-level ranking utility
\begin{equation}
    u_K(A,x)=
    \begin{cases}
        \displaystyle \frac{1}{\log_2\!\left(r_A(y\mid\xi)+1\right)}, & r_A(y\mid\xi)\le K,\\[6pt]
        0, & r_A(y\mid\xi)>K.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:event-ndcg}
\end{equation}
With one relevant target per event, Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg} is its NDCG@$K$ contribution; the mean across events is aggregate NDCG@$K$. Candidate construction enters the metric through the ranked alternatives, allowing the same user--target interaction to have different ranking utilities under different regimes.

\subsection{Base-Relative Specialist Utility}
\label{sec:relative-utility}

Let $B$ denote a fixed base recommender and $M$ a fixed relationship-memory specialist. For a given selective-decision experiment, both rank the same event-specific candidate set and are fixed before the invocation policy is learned. We define the specialist-minus-base utility
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_M(x)=u_K(M,x)-u_K(B,x).
    \label{eq:delta-memory}
\end{equation}
The sign of $\Delta_M(x)$ states whether the specialist improves, ties or worsens Base's ranking for the evaluated request. Thus, the measured contribution is indexed by both the Base--specialist pair and the eligible ranking candidates.

The realized $\Delta_M(x)$ is an offline supervisory quantity defined using the observed target $y$ and both candidate rankings. Decision-time selection instead relies on the expected difference given observable information.

\subsection{Conditional Relative Utility}
\label{sec:conditional-utility}

Let $R$ index the operating recommendation condition under which candidate construction, temporal availability, or related protocol elements are fixed, and let $Z$ denote the information available at invocation time. We condition on $R=r$ to describe a regime-specific event distribution; $R$ need not be an explicit feature of $Z$. The variable $Z$ contains pre-outcome history summaries and descriptors of the Base's candidate-score distribution, including score margins. These descriptors need not be calibrated uncertainty measures. Define the conditional specialist-minus-base utility as
\begin{equation}
    \eta_r(z)
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid Z=z,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
Within a fixed regime we suppress $r$ when unambiguous. An estimator $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ uses the available descriptors to predict specialist-minus-base ranking value before the event outcome.

Let $a(Z)\in\{0,1\}$ denote a pre-outcome selection policy, where $a(Z)=0$ uses the base ranking and $a(Z)=1$ uses the specialist ranking. This includes development-frozen score-threshold policies but excludes any dependence on the realized target. For an evaluated event $x$, define $u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(x;a)=(1-a)u_K(B,x)+a\,u_K(M,x)$. The expected improvement over always using the base satisfies
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}\!\left[u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(X;a)-u_K(B,X)\mid R=r\right] \\
    &\qquad=\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\Delta_M(X)\mid R=r\right]
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\eta_r(Z)\mid R=r\right].
    \label{eq:expected-selective-gain}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:expected-selective-gain} expresses the selection objective in terms of the specialist's expected incremental ranking contribution on the chosen events.

The policy uses $Z$ at decision time; realized relative utility, target-defined history states and target recurrence distances serve only as supervision or retrospective diagnostics. Base Difficulty characterizes the incumbent's ranking loss, whereas $\eta_r$ measures the value of choosing $M$ instead of $B$.

\subsection{Selective Invocation and Equal-Count Comparison}
\label{sec:decision-rule}

Given $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$, a threshold selects the specialist whenever its predicted incremental value exceeds the development-selected operating threshold $\tau_r$:
\begin{equation}
    a_{\tau_r}(z)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{\eta}_r(z)>\tau_r\right],
    \label{eq:utility-threshold}
\end{equation}
where a selected event uses $M$ and an unselected event uses $B$. The threshold is applied to requests individually; its realized invocation rate depends on the evaluated context distribution.

For $n$ evaluated requests, the frozen pointwise rule yields $m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{\tau_r}(z_i)$ specialist calls. Equal-count comparisons hold invocation frequency fixed for evaluating the selected events; Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparators} specifies the comparator's retrospective batch-level access.

\subsection{Regime-Level Utility Decomposition}
\label{sec:regime-decomposition}

A change in aggregate specialist value can reflect a changed distribution of evidence states, changed state-conditional ranking value, or both. Let $S$ be an exhaustive partition of target-relative relationship states, operationalized in Section~\ref{sec:framework-states}. With $\bar{\Delta}_r=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_M(X)\mid R=r]$, total expectation gives
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\Delta}_r
    =\sum_s P(S=s\mid R=r)
    \,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid S=s,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:regime-decomposition}
\end{equation}
This identity separates state prevalence from state-conditional ranking contributions. The paired candidate protocols in Section~\ref{sec:exp-regimes} then examine changes in measured relative utility while holding the evaluated target-history states fixed.

\subsection{Strong Sequential Base Recommenders}
\label{sec:framework-base}

KuaiLive uses a Dual-ID sequential Base with room-level and streamer-level SASRec branches. The room branch represents individual live sessions; the streamer branch connects sessions involving one creator. Both score the eligible \emph{rooms}, and their within-candidate standardized scores are fused by development-selected coefficients. This preserves next-room ranking while exploiting persistent streamer identity as part of the incumbent's own representation. Fusion coefficients are selected separately for the experimental candidate protocols.

For Twitch/LiveRec, Base is the availability-aware LiveRec recommender \cite{rappaz2021liverec}, including context and repeat-consumption components (input length 16). These platform-specific incumbent architectures define distinct, fixed reference rankings for their respective Base-relative utility comparisons.

\subsection{Transparent Relationship-Memory Specialist}
\label{sec:framework-memory}

The specialist $M$ scores eligible candidates from historical user--creator interactions available before the target time. Let $i$ denote a candidate creator, let $n_{u,i}(t)$ be its count in the eligible pre-target history, and let $n_{u,\max}(t)$ be the largest such creator-specific count for user $u$. Define a long-term relationship strength as $\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)=n_{u,i}(t)/\max\{1,n_{u,\max}(t)\}$. For the ordered last $K_s=10$ eligible user--creator interactions, let $d$ be the reverse interaction index ($d=0$ for the most recent). The short-term component is $\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)=\max_{\{d:i_d=i\}}\exp(-d/3)$, with a maximum over an empty set defined as zero. Hence repeated appearances contribute their most recent decayed strength rather than an unbounded sum.

The popularity component $\mathrm{Pop}(i)$ is derived from eligible training-split creator counts by applying $\log(1+n_i)$ and min--max scaling over observed creators; unseen creators receive zero. The fixed specialist score is
\begin{equation}
s_M(u,i,t)=
0.45\,\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)
+0.45\,\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)
+0.10\,\mathrm{Pop}(i).
\label{eq:memory-scoring}
\end{equation}

The same scoring coefficients, short-history horizon and decay are used on both platforms; a candidate without prior interaction may still receive a nonzero popularity score. Candidates are ordered by score with deterministic identifier-based tie-breaking. In KuaiLive, distinct rooms mapped to one creator can consequently share a Memory score; Twitch candidates are streamers directly. The eligibility of pre-target records and platform-specific timing conventions are given in Section~\ref{sec:exp-data}. This construction provides a transparent, separately scored ranking from persistent creator relationships.

\subsection{Base-Relative Relationship-Evidence States}
\label{sec:framework-states}

We operationalize the diagnostic variable $S$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:regime-decomposition} using the target creator and pre-target history. Let $V_L(x)$ indicate that the target creator occurs in the sequence visible to a base with input length $L$, and let $H(x)$ indicate that the same creator occurs anywhere in the eligible pre-target relationship history. Then
\begin{equation}
S_L(x)=
\begin{cases}
\text{represented},&V_L(x)=1,\\
\text{recoverable but unrepresented},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=1,\\
\text{unavailable},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:diagnostic-states}
\end{equation}

These target-relative states describe input visibility for retrospective evaluation. In particular, \emph{represented} denotes membership in the Base input window rather than an assumption about what its trained representation has learned. The selector's observable vector $Z$ excludes $S_L$, target-specific recurrence distance and realized $\Delta_M$.

\subsection{Observable Features and Conditional Relative-Utility Estimation}
\label{sec:framework-estimation}

The Utility estimator $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ is fitted on development-event $\Delta_M(x)$ labels using pre-outcome user-history features and Base candidate-score descriptors. Six history features summarize log history length, creator repeat rate, normalized preference entropy, old-versus-recent Jensen--Shannon drift, circular temporal regularity, and composite history complexity. On Twitch, regularity uses phases modulo $144$ ten-minute steps, representing a daily cycle. Eight Base-score features comprise score standard deviation and range, top-1/top-2, top-1/top-5 and top-10/top-11 margins, the top score's standardized value, normalized score entropy, and top-ten softmax mass. These quantities summarize candidate ranking patterns; entropy and softmax mass are not assumed to be calibrated uncertainty estimates.

The composite history-complexity descriptor weights percentiles of entropy, preference drift and log history length by $0.4$, $0.4$ and $0.2$. The transformations use pre-outcome features and preserve the target-independent decision context. Reference-pool construction and estimator fitting follow the split-specific protocol in Section~\ref{sec:exp-freeze}.

\subsection{Specialist Selection and Matched-Budget Controls}
\label{sec:framework-controls}

At invocation time, the Base supplies the default ranking and score-based descriptors in $Z$. The selector combines these with history descriptors and applies Eq.~\eqref{eq:utility-threshold}; Memory is scored only for selected requests, and the corresponding ranking is returned.

Base Difficulty predicts $1-u_K(B,x)$ from the same observable feature family but does not model Memory's ranking advantage. Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparators} specifies the evaluation of both targets and the equal-count and Oracle controls.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

We evaluate the relative value of relationship memory through three complementary live-streaming recommendation protocols. Twitch/LiveRec provides a held-out evaluation of selective streamer ranking; the original KuaiLive task isolates candidate-regime differences on matched live-room targets; and KuaiLive-M3 provides a later-period, independently frozen evaluation of author-level specialist selection. These protocols differ in prediction unit, available candidates and evaluation design; their absolute ranking metrics are not treated as cross-task comparisons (Table~\ref{tab:exp-datasets}).

\subsection{Datasets and Prediction Tasks}
\label{sec:exp-data}

\paragraph{Twitch/LiveRec.}
The availability-aware Twitch/LiveRec protocol predicts a user's next \emph{streamer} among streamers observed as available at the prediction step \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. User--streamer activity is recorded in ten-minute crawl steps; the recorded first and last observations do not establish exact session start or end. The designated LiveRec Base uses a context length of 16. DEV and frozen TEST contain 46,878 and 44,221 one-positive ranking events. Memory uses interaction records first observed strictly before the target step and a popularity prior computed from the training period. A stricter split-end history rule is evaluated separately (Supplementary S1).

\paragraph{Original KuaiLive.}
The original KuaiLive protocol predicts a time-bounded \emph{live room} while historical relationships are represented through persistent streamer identities \cite{qu2026kuailive}. A per-user chronological leave-last-two-out split yields 10,222 eligible users with one development and one test target each. The preceding development interaction is available as history for the final test event; the popularity prior is training-only. Recorded room availability uses half-open intervals $[t_{\mathrm{start}},t_{\mathrm{end}})$. In the sampled-active condition, the target is ranked with 574 distinct contemporaneously active non-target rooms; full-active uses the contemporaneous active-room universe. The recorded target is retained if its session interval is inconsistent with its observed presence. The same 10,222 user--time--target events underpin both candidate conditions. Sampled competitors are evaluation negatives, not verified disliked rooms. This paired analysis is a retrospective candidate-regime diagnostic rather than a second prospective gate test \cite{krichene2020sampled}.

\paragraph{KuaiLive-M3.}
We additionally evaluate a distinct \emph{author}-level prediction task from KuaiLive-M3, treating \texttt{author\_id} as a persistent relationship identity while room sessions determine author availability. The global forward-time split uses TRAIN events before 23 March 2026 at 12:00 and DEV events until 27 March 2026 at 14:00; later events form TEST. These source timestamps are used as a common recorded ordering without assigning an unsupported time zone. The training sequence index comprises 24,995,148 eligible events. Deterministic, outcome-independent cohort selection produces 50,049 DEV and 47,575 frozen TEST ranking events, the latter associated with 12,533 users. Eligible targets have at least two prior interactions and a TRAIN-known author; candidates are the distinct TRAIN-known authors with at least one active room at the prediction time. TEST ranking uses the full eligible author roster (median 31,065 authors), not a fixed sampled candidate count. User histories are strictly ordered by observed event time and stable source order. This forward-time author task differs from original KuaiLive room ranking; a later observation period does not establish person-disjointness across releases.

\subsection{Baselines and Compared Methods}
\label{sec:exp-comparators}

Each ranking compares a designated trained incumbent $B$ with the same fixed Short--Long--Popularity relationship-memory alternative $M$ on precisely the same event-specific eligible candidates (Section~\ref{sec:framework-memory}). The original KuaiLive Base is the fixed Dual-ID SASRec room--streamer fusion model; branch scores are standardized using each candidate universe and combined with a development-selected fusion coefficient. Its auxiliary Pop, single-branch SASRec, GRU4Rec and ContraRec--BERT4Rec checks have unequal training/tuning coverage and are not comprehensive state-of-the-art claims (Supplementary S3). Twitch uses the designated availability-aware LiveRec Base. KuaiLive-M3 uses independently trained author-level SASRec and GRU4Rec encoders, each with three prespecified training seeds (20261012, 20261013 and 20261014). In M3, Memory ranks authors from strictly earlier history using the same three conceptual components, fixed weights $0.45/0.45/0.10$, and a TRAIN-only popularity reference; unknown-to-TRAIN past authors still count in the short-history positions and long-history normalization.

The Twitch frozen selector predicts Memory-minus-Base NDCG@10 using 14 pre-outcome features. Its Base-Difficulty regression predicts $1-u_{10}(B,x)$ from the same feature family. Utility's threshold is selected on DEV and held fixed on TEST; Difficulty and the outcome-informed Oracle retrospectively choose the same number of TEST events as the frozen Utility policy, and thus are \emph{equal-count diagnostic controls}, not separately frozen pointwise TEST selectors. The old KuaiLive experiment instead compares the two fixed rankings without a second prospectively frozen routing test.

For M3, the five independently DEV-frozen pointwise selectors are \emph{Utility}, \emph{Difficulty}, \emph{Specialist Quality}, \emph{Uncertainty} and \emph{History}. Three histogram gradient-boosting regressors predict specialist-minus-Base utility, negative Base utility, and specialist utility, respectively, using the same 12 pre-outcome descriptors. These comprise five Base score summaries (margin, standard deviation, top-score standardized value, top score and score mean) and seven shared history/candidate descriptors (prior interaction count, TRAIN-known prior count, distinct prior warm authors, maximum warm-author repetition, short-history distinct count, historical support among eligible authors and log eligible-candidate count). Uncertainty ranks events by the negative Base top-score margin; History ranks by prior interaction count. The two heuristic scores do not use target-defined evidence states. These objective-specific models and thresholds are fitted separately for each frozen Base checkpoint; no trained Twitch selector is transferred to M3.

\subsection{Experimental Protocols}
\label{sec:exp-freeze}
\label{sec:exp-regimes}

\paragraph{Training, OOF estimation and policy freeze.}
All designated Base parameters are fitted without TEST outcomes, and model/checkpoint choices precede the corresponding frozen TEST evaluations. On Twitch, five-fold DEV out-of-fold (OOF) prediction provides the Utility threshold and the fitted Utility and Difficulty regressors are trained on DEV before TEST. The original room-regime analysis fixes one KuaiLive Dual-ID checkpoint pair, its sampled-development fusion coefficient and Memory score definition for both candidate universes.

For M3, 64-dimensional author-level SASRec and GRU4Rec checkpoints are trained with equal prespecified settings across the three seeds. Checkpoints are selected solely on a common 4,096-event DEV panel with 575 eligible author candidates. This screening panel is distinct from the 50,049-event \emph{full-active} DEV evaluation and the 47,575-event \emph{full-active} TEST. On the paired full-active DEV events, five user-group-disjoint OOF folds estimate each supervised gate objective, while Uncertainty and History use their specified observable scores. Each objective's pointwise score threshold is chosen to maximize DEV OOF mean selected ranking gain relative to Base, subject to at most 30\% DEV specialist invocations; all tied threshold scores are selected together. The selected thresholds and final DEV-fitted gate models are frozen before TEST. They are applied pointwise without access to TEST target outcomes, without threshold recalibration, and \emph{without imposing an additional hard cap on realized TEST invocations}. Accordingly, methods share the \emph{DEV selection constraint}, not necessarily an identical realized TEST budget. The conditional nature of DEV threshold optimization is separated from independent frozen TEST confirmation.

\paragraph{Forward-time TEST information boundary.}
In M3, author availability and Base and Memory scores are derived without the current event's target author label. The evaluation is prequential: interactions already observed before the prediction event, including earlier TEST-period interactions, may update that user's history, but the current and future targets cannot enter its feature vector, eligible ranking candidates or threshold selection. The target-blind evaluation first freezes six Base and one Memory top-10 rankings together with all five gate decisions and their checksums. The TEST target labels are then used solely to compute ranking utilities and paired inference. This distinction prevents target leakage while retaining the specified sequentially observable TEST history. The M3 cohort construction, model and threshold identities were fixed before evaluating TEST outcomes; the original prospective/retrospective scope distinctions for Twitch and old KuaiLive remain unchanged.

\paragraph{Candidate-regime comparisons.}
KuaiLive sampled-active and full-active protocols have distinct eligible candidate universes. Supplementary protocol comparisons include development-selected native configurations and a sampled-regime selector applied in the full-active setting. For the main paired ranking analysis, a single Room-SASRec/Streamer-SASRec checkpoint pair, sampled-development fusion coefficient and Memory scoring rule are held fixed. Raw scores are computed over the full-active universe and restricted to the paired sampled subset; each candidate set supplies its own branch-score standardization reference. Thus, the same user event, observed target, eligible history and fixed raw rankers are evaluated under two ranking environments. For event $i$, the paired shift is
\begin{equation}
D_i =
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})\big]
-
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})\big].
\label{eq:paired-regime-shift}
\end{equation}
The KuaiLive analysis is a paired evaluation-protocol diagnostic on the previously examined TEST cohort. Crossing ranked-candidate membership $C\in\{S,F\}$ with score-standardization reference $R\in\{S,F\}$ separates two protocol components while holding raw Base branch predictions and Memory scores fixed. With $g_i(C,R)$ the event-level Memory-minus-Base utility, the order-averaged allocations are
\begin{align}
A_{C,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(F,S)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(S,F)\big],\nonumber\\
A_{R,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(S,F)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(F,S)\big].
\label{eq:factorial-allocations}
\end{align}
By construction, $A_{C,i}+A_{R,i}=g_i(F,F)-g_i(S,S)=D_i$. This decomposition allocates the observed paired contrast within the fixed-score protocol; candidate membership combines list size, composition and competition. Three separately trained checkpoint pairs assess initialization sensitivity on the same cohort, while nine additional sampled-candidate draws are evaluated for the original fixed pair (Supplementary S5 and S8). Historical-eligibility and context-length checks examine complementary aspects of evidence availability.


\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:exp-statistics}

The primary endpoint is single-positive event NDCG@10 from Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg}; hit rate at ten (HR@10) and the realized fraction of events routed to Memory are secondary outcomes. Paired same-event contrasts evaluate Memory versus Base and Utility versus designated baselines. Target-defined relationship states (Eq.~\eqref{eq:diagnostic-states}) support retrospective conditional analyses only and never enter gate features. Twitch's post-hoc matched-call Difficulty and hindsight Oracle comparisons are distinguished from the independently frozen M3 policy outcomes; conditional Random@budget expectations and outcome-based Oracle@budget results serve solely as descriptive bounds.

Uncertainty intervals preserve pairing within users or user clusters. The primary Twitch frozen TEST comparisons use 5,000 paired bootstrap resamples; the original KuaiLive same-event candidate-regime contrasts use 3,000. For M3's 47,575 TEST events from 12,533 users, 5,000 user-cluster paired bootstrap replicates provide percentile 95\% intervals, and 5,000 user-cluster random-sign replicates provide one-sided $p$-values under an approximately sign-symmetric user-cluster null. The three predeclared comparisons are Utility versus (i) Always Base, (ii) Difficulty and (iii) Specialist Quality. Holm's sequential procedure \cite{holm1979} adjusts these \emph{three} comparisons separately within each of the six independently trained Base--seed configurations; the analysis does not assert familywise protection across all 18 correlated tests. Utility versus Uncertainty, History and unconditional Memory are additional comparisons without that preregistered primary-inference status. All six models share the same held-out TEST events, so training-seed variation is reported as fitted-model robustness rather than independent dataset replication. CIs and inference are conditional on the cohort, trained models, fixed candidate regime and frozen decision policies.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:exp-implementation}

The original KuaiLive Dual-ID Base uses ReChorus SASRec encoders with 64-dimensional embeddings, one attention layer, four heads and maximum history length 50. Twitch LiveRec uses context length 16 with context and repeat modules; its 14-feature histogram gradient-boosting Utility estimator uses 200 boosting iterations, learning rate $0.05$, depth $3$, minimum leaf size $50$ and $\ell_2$ penalty $1$. M3 Base encoders use 64-dimensional author embeddings and a maximum input history of 16: one causal self-attention block with four heads for SASRec, and one recurrent layer for GRU4Rec. The three supervised M3 gate objectives use the same 200-iteration, depth-3 histogram boosting configuration and group-disjoint DEV folds, separately for each trained Base. TEST rankings use contemporaneous full-active warm-author candidates and a frozen deterministic score-tie rule based on author identity. The earlier original DEV ranker and the new frozen TEST evaluator have slightly different numerical tie tolerances; neither protocol was altered after TEST outcome evaluation. The deterministic M3 cohort selection used a frozen DuckDB typed-timestamp hash implementation, whose byte identity is recorded; this differs from the original pre-execution timestamp-in-milliseconds notation, without any subsequent outcome-based cohort changes. Supplementary S1--S3 give the full split, model-selection, feature and implementation conventions, S7--S8 the decision and uncertainty details, and S9 the distinct warmed computational-cost protocols. Expert-invocation frequency is not identified with elapsed inference time or measured deployment cost.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

We first examine the incumbent-relative contribution of fixed relationship evidence in two distinct held-out ranking tasks, then evaluate whether this contribution can inform frozen specialist-selection decisions. A paired room-level candidate-regime analysis and model/feature robustness tests clarify the conditions of this decision value, followed by direct evidence on invocation constraints and scoring cost.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Utility of Relationship Memory}
\label{sec:results-heterogeneity}

The incremental value of a fixed relationship-memory specialist differs between the two held-out ranking environments. On 44,221 Twitch TEST streamer-ranking events, always using Memory yields NDCG@10 $0.53980$, compared with $0.58211$ for Base, a paired difference of $-0.04232$ (95\% CI $[-0.04521,-0.03950]$). The retrospective states defined in Section~\ref{sec:framework-states} distinguish where this specialist improves or degrades the incumbent's ranking.

Figure~\ref{fig:results-evidence-states} shows consistent state-wise directions on Twitch DEV and frozen TEST. For 5,539 \emph{recoverable-but-unrepresented} TEST targets, Memory exceeds Base by $+0.22045$ NDCG@10 on average; the represented and unavailable groups instead show differences of $-0.01935$ and $-0.18215$ (Table~\ref{tab:results-evidence-states}). The contrast identifies a substantial source of complementarity beyond the Base's input window despite the specialist's negative aggregate effect.

In the independent forward-time KuaiLive-M3 author-ranking TEST ($n=47{,}575$), the fixed Memory achieves NDCG@10 $0.34679$ against Base values from $0.25800$ to $0.26065$ across six independently fitted SASRec and GRU4Rec checkpoints. Memory's mean advantage is positive for every fit; for the primary SASRec seed it is $+0.08879$ ($0.34679-0.25800$). Thus the direction of mean Memory-minus-Base utility differs across the streamer- and author-level evaluations. The incumbent, ranked unit, candidate universe and temporal protocol also differ, so this between-task contrast does not identify a single cause of the reversal.

The Twitch evidence states are defined from the observed target and diagnose conditional utility retrospectively. The M3 mean advantage likewise establishes the value of its fixed specialist relative to the designated Base, not the advantage of a selective policy. Decision-time usefulness is assessed separately using pre-outcome features and TEST-frozen policies.

\subsection{Decision Value of Predicting Relative Utility}
\label{sec:results-decision}

Predicting specialist-relative utility provides a decision rule for the heterogeneous value identified above. This section compares two frozen TEST selection settings, Twitch streamer ranking and KuaiLive-M3 author ranking, without pooling their absolute NDCG levels. On Twitch, the Utility regressor and pointwise threshold were fitted and selected on DEV out-of-fold predictions, then fixed before the 44,221 TEST events. The resulting policy invokes Memory on 6,650 events (15.04\%).

The DEV-frozen Utility policy increases Twitch TEST NDCG@10 from $0.58211$ for Base to $0.58983$ (Table~\ref{tab:results-twitch-policy}). The paired gain is $+0.00772$, with 95\% CI $[+0.00641,+0.00903]$. HR@10 increases by $+0.00945$, with 95\% CI $[+0.00776,+0.01117]$. Both intervals use 5,000 user-paired bootstrap resamples with the policy decisions held fixed.

Relative-utility prediction also distinguishes profitable specialist calls from difficult Base requests. At the Utility policy's realized count of 6,650 calls, the DEV-trained Difficulty score yields NDCG@10 $0.58340$, leaving a Utility advantage of $+0.00644$ (paired 95\% CI $[+0.00528,+0.00762]$); the corresponding HR@10 difference is $+0.01196$ (95\% CI $[+0.01033,+0.01364]$). Difficulty chooses its top-$m$ events retrospectively over the TEST batch, whereas Utility applies the threshold fixed on DEV; the matched count compares the value of the selected events.

The realized TEST selection masks support this distinction: Memory-minus-Base NDCG@10 averages $+0.05136$ on the 6,650 Utility-selected events and $-0.05890$ on the remaining 37,571, compared with $+0.00855$ for Difficulty's equal-count selections. Complementary DEV out-of-fold diagnostics show recoverable-history enrichment of $2.72$ for Utility versus $1.67$ for Difficulty; Difficulty more strongly enriches unavailable histories ($1.91$ versus $1.03$; Supplementary S7). These target-defined states describe the resulting selections retrospectively.

The same-count hindsight Oracle reaches NDCG@10 $0.64806$ by selecting events using realized Memory-minus-Base utility, an improvement of $+0.06595$ over Base. Utility captures approximately 11.7\% of this available Oracle gain, with a predicted-versus-realized event-utility Spearman correlation of $0.173$. These Twitch results establish a positive selective-ranking benefit while quantifying the remaining predictive headroom for the fixed Base--Memory pair.

\subsection{Candidate-Regime Dependence of Relative Utility}
\label{sec:results-regimes}

Within the original KuaiLive emph{room}-ranking task, changing the eligible candidate environment reverses which of the two fixed rankers has higher measured utility. For the same 10,222 user--time--target events, sampled-active $S$ (575 rooms including the target) is nested within full-active $F$. Component variation offers a complementary explanation of the state contrast. Across seven locally examined Memory parameter settings on Twitch DEV, relative utility remains positive for recoverable targets and negative for represented and unavailable targets (Supplementary S4). These contrasting effects relate specialist advantage to the historical signal it retains; the frozen TEST specialist remains Full Memory.

The incumbent's available history also changes the comparison. Separately trained Twitch DEV Bases with input lengths $L=8,16,32$ achieve NDCG@10 $0.52619$, $0.57185$ and $0.59663$, respectively, against an unchanged Memory score of $0.52170$ (Supplementary Table~S6.1). Where targets move from recoverable to represented, Memory's relative benefit declines as the corresponding trained Base improves. Because each Base is trained separately, the contrast reflects both access to history and model adaptation.

Target--creator recurrence distance provides an additional descriptive check: the Memory-minus-Base difference varies nonmonotonically across retrospective distance bins (Supplementary Table~S6.4), consistent with the absence of a simple monotonic relationship between elapsed interaction history and ranking benefit.

These Twitch DEV diagnostics provide evidence that observable history and incumbent-score features jointly track relative Memory value, while separately trained context-length and Memory-component checks describe variation in complementarity. Additional Base competitiveness checks are reported in Supplementary S3.

\subsection{Invocation Constraints and Computational Cost}
\label{sec:results-efficiency}

Specialist invocation frequency is distinct from the cost of producing the selected ranking. The Twitch Utility policy makes 6,650 Memory calls among 44,221 frozen TEST events ($15.04\%$), whereas the six M3 DEV-frozen Utility policies invoke Memory on $27.72$--$29.21\%$ of TEST events. The latter fractions arise without adjusting the DEV-frozen thresholds to enforce a TEST quota; alternative M3 selectors can exceed 30\% TEST calls, including the History policy at $35.47\%$.

The original KuaiLive 575-room warmed CPU scoring replay reports mean latency $0.991$\,ms for Base and $2.007$\,ms for the selective HGB path (P95 $1.276$ and $2.905$\,ms, respectively), an additional $1.016$\,ms despite a $14.25\%$ observed Memory call fraction. This replay uses prepared features and cached histories and includes the gate inference overhead under its fixed conditions (Supplementary S9).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Operational Value Is Base- and Context-Dependent}
\label{sec:discussion-operational-value}

The principal distinction emerging from the Twitch evidence is between possessing historical relationship information and improving a decision with it. Sequential models already encode regularities in observed interactions, while persistent relationship records may also contain information outside the incumbent's input window. The contrasting Memory outcomes indicate that these sources can overlap for some requests and complement one another for others. The recoverable-history advantage is consistent with the specialist exploiting relationships that the designated Base cannot directly observe within its input, rather than with a uniform benefit from adding historical evidence.

This distinction refines the role of auxiliary knowledge in recommendation. Fusion and reliability methods aim to represent, filter or combine evidence effectively \cite{zhu2026mifusr,huo2026reliability,peng2025negative}; collaborative memory construction can supply richer relational context \cite{chen2026memrec}. For a separately scored specialist, an additional question concerns the \emph{marginal ranking contribution} of that context given an incumbent's existing capabilities. The Twitch DEV comparisons of Memory components and separately trained longer-context Bases support the interpretation that complementarity depends on both the specialist's historical signal and the trained Base against which it is assessed (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}). Knowledge quality and knowledge value for a particular ranking decision are thus related, but distinct, evaluation targets.

\subsection{Interpreting Conditional Evidence and Candidate Regimes}
\label{sec:discussion-candidate-regimes}

The KuaiLive findings extend this decision-relative view from model context to ranking context. For unchanged user--target events and fixed raw Base--Memory model predictions, the sampled-active and full-active candidate regimes give opposite specialist-relative comparisons (Section~\ref{sec:results-regimes}). As the target-history states are identical across the paired events, their prevalence cannot explain the reversal. Instead, the event's ranking utility is conditional on the alternatives competing with its target: a relationship signal that changes the target's relative position in one candidate universe may have a different contribution in another.

Krichene and Rendle establish that sampled recommendation metrics can alter relative model comparisons \cite{krichene2020sampled}. The present paired evaluation connects that known ranking phenomenon to the operational question of whether a particular source of historical knowledge should replace an incumbent ranking. The crossed candidate/reference analysis locates most of the observed difference in ranked-candidate membership rather than the score-standardization reference. This allocation is descriptive: membership changes candidate number, composition and ranking competition together. Directional consistency across the examined model initializations and candidate draws supports the relevance of the observed reversal within this KuaiLive protocol. The resulting implication for knowledge valuation is specific: comparisons between an incumbent and an auxiliary specialist require an explicitly defined candidate environment, because that environment is part of the utility being predicted or measured.

\subsection{From Relative Utility to Selective Decisions}
\label{sec:discussion-selective-decisions}

Conditional evidence value becomes a selection problem when the system can invoke either the incumbent or the specialist. Learning-to-Defer already develops this comparative-decision principle for models and experts, including post-hoc allocation over trained predictors \cite{mozannar2020defer,narasimhan2022posthoc}. Equation~\eqref{eq:expected-selective-gain} applies the same expected-gain logic to the ranking difference between a fixed relationship-memory specialist and its designated Base. The knowledge-specific question is which observable request characteristics predict that difference sufficiently well to improve ranking.

This target also explains the distinction between Utility and Base Difficulty. A request may be difficult for the incumbent without being more accurately ranked by Memory; difficulty measures a need for improvement, whereas relative Utility measures whether this particular historical knowledge source supplies it. GUIDER demonstrates uncertainty-guided adaptation in LLM recommendation \cite{xu2026guider}, but uncertainty about the incumbent's output does not by itself measure the benefit of switching to a given specialist. The frozen Twitch Utility gains over Base and the retrospectively count-matched Difficulty control support the value of estimating specialist advantage for this choice (Section~\ref{sec:results-decision}). The DEV feature-family comparisons further indicate that historical descriptors and Base-score characteristics together help distinguish these opportunities.

The observed ranking improvement does not require perfect event-wise utility prediction. On Twitch TEST, the positive selected-event mean contrasts with the negative unselected-event mean even though utility prediction has a Spearman correlation of $0.173$ and captures approximately $11.7\%$ of the same-count hindsight Oracle gain. Selective ranking can therefore benefit from informative ordering of specialist opportunities while leaving scope for better calibration and allocation. The result supports this particular pre-outcome, DEV-frozen Utility choice; its comparison with Difficulty uses a retrospective TEST-batch equal-count reference.

\subsection{Validity Boundaries}
\label{sec:discussion-validity}

The conclusions rest on three distinct evidence roles. Twitch provides a DEV-frozen policy evaluated on held-out TEST events; its target-history states describe realized outcomes retrospectively, while its selection features are available before the outcome. Development-only component, history-length and feature-family studies inform interpretation of that policy rather than adding independent TEST validations. KuaiLive supplies paired, previously examined TEST-event evidence about candidate-regime sensitivity, supported by training and sampling checks within the same cohort. These one-positive offline ranking protocols establish context-specific relative utility and selection effects, with uncertainty estimates conditional on the evaluated events and model configurations.

Operational adoption also depends on computation and deployment context. Twitch's specialist invocation frequency measures selective use, while the separate warmed KuaiLive HGB replay measures an additional $1.016$\,ms of scoring time over Base (Section~\ref{sec:results-efficiency}). Its prepared inputs and cached histories define a narrower cost measurement than end-to-end serving. Consequently, the offline decision improvements and paired candidate comparisons motivate prospective tests of newly frozen policies, realized request latency and user outcomes before extending the conclusions to production settings.

\subsection{Implications for Knowledge-Based Recommendation}
\label{sec:discussion-implications}

For knowledge-based recommenders that can score a distinct auxiliary specialist, the findings suggest a practical evaluation order. First, specify the incumbent and its eligible candidates, then measure the auxiliary source's incremental utility and its variation across requests rather than relying on aggregate specialist quality or evidence availability. Where candidate regimes differ, evaluate that marginal contribution under the corresponding ranking alternatives. This distinguishes the value of additional knowledge from the properties of its representation and from the metric environment used to assess it.

A second design consideration concerns how knowledge is invoked. Pre-outcome predictors should be trained against the incremental benefit of the available specialist when the decision is whether to use that specialist; difficulty or uncertainty scores remain useful comparators but answer different questions. Invocation policies also need evaluation at the full computation path, including feature construction, gate inference and specialist scoring. These principles apply to the studied fixed-ranker, offline decision setting and provide testable criteria for evaluating additional knowledge sources under prospectively specified candidate environments and resource constraints.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our findings show that persistent relationship memory contributes to recommendation through conditional gains over a trained incumbent rather than uniform improvements from additional history. On Twitch, a fixed Memory specialist underperforms Base in aggregate but has a substantial positive mean advantage for targets whose eligible relationships are recoverable beyond the Base input window. This contrast identifies where historical evidence provides measurable complementary ranking value relative to the incumbent.

For held-out Twitch events, a pre-outcome Utility selector fitted and thresholded on DEV improves ranking over Base and a Difficulty comparator retrospectively matched to the same specialist-use count. The result distinguishes anticipating specialist advantage from identifying requests difficult for the incumbent. Complementary paired KuaiLive analyses show that changing the eligible candidate environment reverses the measured Memory-versus-Base advantage for fixed rankers and user events. This reversal remains directionally stable across the studied training initializations and candidate draws; the descriptive decomposition assigns most of the change to ranked-candidate membership.

Taken together, these results connect the valuation of historical knowledge to the decision to use it. A relationship-memory specialist has operational value relative to a particular incumbent and candidate universe; an estimate of that relative value, available before outcomes, can guide selective ranking. This provides an empirically supported, decision-oriented interpretation of knowledge augmentation in the evaluated offline recommendation settings.
