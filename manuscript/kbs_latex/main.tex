\title{When Does Relationship Memory Help? Base-Relative Evidence Valuation for Live-Streaming Recommendation}

\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{abstract}
Whether persistent relationship history improves live-streaming recommendations depends on what a trained incumbent already captures and which candidates are eligible for ranking. We define the value of a fixed Short--Long--Popularity relationship-memory specialist as its event-level NDCG@10 gain over the incumbent on identical candidates, and examine that value in three complementary protocols. On 44,221 held-out Twitch streamer-ranking events, a development-frozen relative-utility selector raises NDCG@10 from 0.58211 to 0.58983 using Memory on 15.04\% of requests, outperforming a retrospectively equal-call difficulty control. In a paired analysis of 10,222 original KuaiLive room-ranking events, mean Memory-minus-Base utility reverses from -0.04120 with 575 sampled active rooms to +0.04631 with the full active-room set. A separately frozen, forward-time KuaiLive-M3 author-ranking evaluation covers 47,575 TEST events and two incumbent architectures with three trained seeds each. Under a common development-set specialist-call constraint, relative-utility selection outperforms four alternative frozen objectives across all six fitted incumbents; for the primary SASRec fit, NDCG@10 increases from 0.25800 to 0.30287. Unconditional Memory nevertheless reaches 0.34679 and has lower measured warmed scoring time than selective routing in a limited development replay. These findings establish incumbent- and candidate-relative historical-knowledge value and the quality of invocation-constrained selection, without implying unconditional deployment or latency benefits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sequential recommenders derive user preferences from interaction sequences, with models such as SASRec and BERT4Rec learning increasingly expressive representations of recent behavior \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. In live streaming, however, relationships between viewers and creators can persist across many ephemeral sessions. Such relationships may provide evidence beyond a trained recommender's finite input window, but they can also duplicate information already encoded in its scores. Consequently, the availability or standalone predictive strength of historical knowledge does not establish its contribution to a particular recommendation. The operative question is \emph{when a fixed relationship-memory specialist improves the ranking produced by a designated incumbent on the same eligible candidates}.

Knowledge-aware recommendation has developed mechanisms for representing, filtering and fusing historical, structural and semantic signals \cite{xie2026mhgfcl,kim2026kaper,chen2026memrec}. These advances improve how knowledge enters a recommendation model, while leaving a complementary decision problem: given a trained incumbent and an already specified auxiliary specialist, which individual requests warrant using the specialist's ranking? We operationalize its value as the specialist-minus-incumbent difference in event-level ranking utility. This definition makes knowledge value relative to the incumbent's existing capabilities and the candidates competing for the target, rather than attributing an intrinsic benefit to the relationship record alone. Established learning-to-defer methods provide the comparative allocation principle \cite{mozannar2020defer,narasimhan2022posthoc}; our focus is the ranking-specific value of a transparent historical specialist and its observable, pre-outcome prediction.

Live-streaming recommendation provides distinct conditions for examining this question. Available streamers, transient rooms and persistent creators define different prediction units and candidate sets \cite{rappaz2021liverec,qu2026kuailive}. Candidate construction can change relative ranking evaluations, as established by research on sampled recommendation metrics \cite{krichene2020sampled}. For an auxiliary source, the consequence is more specific: the same relationship evidence can yield different specialist-minus-incumbent utility when the target competes against different alternatives. Likewise, a target's historical relationship to the user may be absent from the incumbent's visible sequence without being absent from the complete prior record. These properties motivate a conditional valuation of knowledge with clearly specified history, candidate eligibility and ranking protocol.

We investigate the resulting value-to-decision chain in three complementary protocols. Twitch/LiveRec supplies a frozen streamer-level TEST of relative-utility-guided specialist choice, together with retrospective history-state diagnostics. Original KuaiLive provides a matched, previously examined room-ranking analysis in which the sampled-active and full-active candidate regimes reverse the measured Memory-minus-Base advantage. KuaiLive-M3 adds an independently frozen, globally forward-time \emph{author}-ranking TEST on 47,575 events, covering SASRec and GRU4Rec incumbents with three separately fitted training seeds each. Across this new TEST setting, five DEV-frozen selection objectives enable direct comparisons with difficulty-, quality- and uncertainty-based routing. The two held-out selection settings and the paired candidate-regime analysis answer distinct questions; their absolute NDCG values are not treated as directly comparable across ranked tasks.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Incumbent-relative knowledge valuation (C1).} We measure the incremental ranking contribution of a fixed relationship-memory specialist on the incumbent's eligible candidates, characterizing heterogeneous historical complementarity and documenting opposite mean Memory-minus-Base directions across the Twitch and M3 evaluation settings.
    \item \textbf{Candidate-conditioned evaluation (C2).} We quantify a sign reversal of relative Memory utility on matched original KuaiLive room-ranking events under sampled-active and full-active candidates, with a descriptive allocation of ranked-candidate membership and score-normalization reference effects.
    \item \textbf{Frozen relative-utility-guided selection (C3).} We evaluate pre-outcome prediction of specialist-minus-Base utility as a selection signal on held-out Twitch and KuaiLive-M3 TEST events. The M3 study adds two incumbent architectures, three training seeds, four alternative DEV-frozen gate objectives, user-cluster inference and observed invocation rates, establishing decision-quality gains under specified call constraints rather than unconditional computational savings.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Historical Representation and Auxiliary Knowledge}

Sequential recommendation models learn short- and long-range behavioral dependencies from user interactions. SASRec and BERT4Rec establish self-attentive sequence representations \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}; more recent intent-aware and multi-granular systems seek to capture additional preference structure \cite{ni2025idmarec,sheng2025dsmgrec}. Improving the historical representation of an incumbent is one way to exploit persistent relationships. A complementary question concerns the incremental benefit of a separately defined history-based ranking after the incumbent has already been trained.

Knowledge-enhanced recommendation uses collaborative, structural and semantic information in a variety of forms. CKLF integrates knowledge-graph spreading activation and sequence-sensitive preference modeling \cite{chen2024cklf}. MHGFCL combines multi-head gated fusion with focused-view contrastive learning \cite{xie2026mhgfcl}, while KAPER extracts preference-relevant graph-path evidence for explainable reranking \cite{kim2026kaper}. Mutual-information-guided fusion, denoising and reliability-aware methods likewise regulate auxiliary evidence \cite{zhu2026mifusr,zhang2026mcd4sr,huo2026reliability}. MemRec constructs and distills collaborative-memory structures for LLM-based recommendation \cite{chen2026memrec}. Conditional gating has also been studied for question answering with incomplete knowledge bases \cite{yerragunta2026gating}, and research on negative transfer highlights the consequences of combining heterogeneous information \cite{peng2025negative}. These studies establish that knowledge construction and conditional integration are active research problems; our analysis fixes the knowledge specialist and trained incumbent separately, then evaluates the specialist's \emph{incremental ranking contribution} under their common candidate protocol.

This distinction also relates to the decision-theoretic value of information \cite{abbas2025voi}. The value examined here is not a representation score or a general measure of historical relevance, but the realized event-wise difference in NDCG@10 between two specified rankings. The candidate set and reference incumbent are consequently part of the operational valuation.

\subsection{Selective Prediction and Specialist Allocation}

Learning-to-Defer provides established frameworks for choosing between a predictor and an expert. Mozannar and Sontag study consistent learning to defer \cite{mozannar2020defer}, while Narasimhan and colleagues examine post-hoc expert allocation for trained predictors and its accuracy--cost tradeoff \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc}. Subsequent work considers multiple experts and expert availability \cite{mao2025mastering,montreuil2026online}; adaptive expert designs also appear in sequential recommendation \cite{sun2025cedrec}. Selecting a specialist on the basis of its expected incremental utility is therefore not, by itself, a new general decision-theoretic procedure. Our empirical target differs from the prediction-error quantities commonly examined in deferral: it is the change in top-$K$ ranking utility between two already fixed ranking outputs on identical eligible candidates. This task-specific outcome and its prospective evaluation, rather than a new optimality result for deferral, define the contribution.

The central distinction among potential selectors is their prediction target. Incumbent difficulty estimates the expected weakness of Base, specialist quality predicts the performance of the alternative in isolation, and uncertainty summarizes aspects of the incumbent's output. GUIDER, for example, adapts LLM-based recommendation using decomposed data and model uncertainty \cite{xu2026guider}. None of these signals, by definition, is identical to the expected ranking improvement from using a \emph{particular fixed} historical specialist. Our comparison measures that improvement directly and evaluates it against specified alternative selection objectives. The M3 uncertainty control is a Base top-score-margin proxy, not a reproduction of the complete GUIDER procedure.

Comparison design also matters. Twitch evaluates a DEV-frozen Utility policy against a retrospectively TEST-count-matched Difficulty selection, whereas M3 independently freezes Utility, Difficulty, Specialist Quality, Uncertainty and History pointwise thresholds on DEV. Their common \emph{DEV} invocation constraint does not force equal realized TEST call rates. The evidence accordingly concerns the quality of selecting relationship knowledge under the declared policy protocols, not a general claim of expert-routing novelty or identical deployment expense.

\subsection{Live-Streaming Recommendation and Candidate-Context Evaluation}

Live-streaming recommendation combines durable viewer preferences with time-varying opportunities. Prior models incorporate viewer--anchor interactions, room dynamics, preference histories, hypergraph structure and repeat viewing \cite{zhang2021bidirectional,gao2023driver,chen2024preference,yu2024hypergraph,zhu2025repeated}. LiveRec explicitly accounts for streamer availability and repeated consumption \cite{rappaz2021liverec}; KuaiLive distinguishes transient live rooms and persistent streamer identities \cite{qu2026kuailive}. DCGLive further models evolving collaboration among users, streamers and rooms \cite{guo2026dcglive}. These settings motivate candidate-aware evaluation of the historical knowledge carried by persistent creator identities.

Ranking metrics depend on which alternatives appear in the candidate universe. Krichene and Rendle demonstrate that metrics computed from sampled candidates can fail to preserve model comparisons under full ranking \cite{krichene2020sampled}. Our original KuaiLive analysis examines the implication for a \emph{knowledge-value contrast}: when the same target and fixed Base--Memory pair are evaluated on sampled and full-active room sets, the measured advantage of choosing Memory changes sign. Its candidate-membership/reference allocation is descriptive, since size, composition and competition vary together. The separate M3 author-ranking TEST evaluates prospective specialist choice on a full active author roster, not a second matched room-candidate intervention.

Taken together, the existing literature provides models for learning relationship representations, rules for expert allocation and warnings about candidate sampling. The present study connects these lines through a fixed-specialist, incumbent-relative ranking estimand: how much explicit relationship history adds under the actual ranking environment, and whether pre-outcome estimates of that addition improve the choice of ranking. Frozen Twitch and M3 selection studies supply decision evidence, while paired original KuaiLive results characterize candidate dependence.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

We study the incremental ranking value of a fixed relationship-memory specialist relative to a trained sequential recommender. The object of interest is an event-specific \emph{comparison} between two rankings of the same eligible candidates, rather than the intrinsic informativeness of a historical relationship in isolation. This comparison provides a supervision target for conditional specialist selection and a basis for interpreting candidate-regime effects.

\subsection{Recommendation Setting and Event-Level Ranking Utility}
\label{sec:event-utility}

For user $u$ at decision time $t$ and evaluation regime $r$, let $\xi_r=(u,t,\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t},\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$ denote the pre-outcome context. Here $\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the eligible candidate set and $\mathcal{H}_{u,t}$ contains interactions already observed before the decision. An evaluated event is $x_r=(\xi_r,y)$, where $y\in\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the subsequently observed relevant item. Both rankers operate on $\xi_r$ without access to $y$. The regime indexes the construction of candidates and ranking scores; the same user--time--target interaction may therefore be evaluated under distinct candidate regimes. We omit $r$ when the regime is fixed.

Let $r_A(y\mid\xi)$ be the one-indexed position of $y$ in the ranking produced by model $A$. For a single observed relevant target, event-level NDCG@$K$ is
\begin{equation}
    u_K(A,x)=
    \begin{cases}
        \displaystyle \frac{1}{\log_2\!\left(r_A(y\mid\xi)+1\right)}, & r_A(y\mid\xi)\le K,\\[6pt]
        0, & r_A(y\mid\xi)>K.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:event-ndcg}
\end{equation}
Its mean across a specified set of events is the corresponding aggregate NDCG@$K$. The realized target determines the offline utility, whereas the eligible candidates determine the competition in which that target is ranked. We use $K=10$ in the experiments.

\subsection{Base-Relative Specialist Utility}
\label{sec:relative-utility}

Let $B$ be the designated trained incumbent and $M$ a fixed relationship-memory specialist within an evaluation protocol. Each assigns a ranking to the \emph{same} candidate set for the same event. The event-level incremental utility of invoking the specialist is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_M(x)=u_K(M,x)-u_K(B,x).
    \label{eq:delta-memory}
\end{equation}
Positive, zero and negative values indicate that Memory improves, ties or worsens the incumbent's rank utility, respectively. Although $M$ may use relationship history beyond the incumbent's visible input, its usefulness is defined by the particular $B$, target-ranking unit and candidates. Since $\Delta_M$ depends on the observed target, it is available for supervised development and retrospective analysis, but not at the moment of selection.

\subsection{Conditional Relative Utility}
\label{sec:conditional-utility}

Let $Z$ comprise decision-time observable summaries of user history and the Base's candidate scores. For the event distribution associated with regime $R=r$, define
\begin{equation}
    \eta_r(z)
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid Z=z,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
The estimator $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ predicts the ranking gain of using the fixed specialist in place of its incumbent. Conditioning on the regime makes the candidate and ranking protocol part of the estimation target; it does not require $R$ to be a separately provided input feature.

A pre-outcome decision $a(Z)\in\{0,1\}$ returns the Base ranking when $a=0$ and invokes the Memory ranking when $a=1$. Its realized event utility is $u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(x;a)=(1-a)u_K(B,x)+a\,u_K(M,x)$. By iterated expectation,
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}\!\left[u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(X;a)-u_K(B,X)\mid R=r\right] \\
    &\qquad=\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\Delta_M(X)\mid R=r\right]
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\eta_r(Z)\mid R=r\right].
    \label{eq:expected-selective-gain}
\end{align}
This identity connects the relative-value prediction target to selective ranking quality. The general idea of allocating predictions to an expert is established in learning-to-defer research \cite{mozannar2020defer,narasimhan2022posthoc}; our analysis operationalizes that comparison for a fixed relationship-memory ranker and event-level candidate-dependent ranking utility.

\subsection{Selective Invocation and Invocation-Count Constraints}
\label{sec:decision-rule}

A score-based policy selects Memory when its estimated relative utility exceeds a development-calibrated threshold. Denoting the protocol's deterministic boundary comparison by $\succeq_r$, the Utility policy is
\begin{equation}
    a_{\tau_r}(z)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[
    \hat{\eta}_r(z)\mathrel{\succeq_r}\tau_r
    \right].
    \label{eq:utility-threshold}
\end{equation}
Among candidate thresholds, development data determine a selection rule with a specified maximum fraction of Memory invocations. The finite-sample constraint is $\hat{q}_{\mathrm{DEV}}=n_{\mathrm{DEV}}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{DEV}}}a_{\tau_r}(z_i)\le q_r$, where $q_r$ is the design limit; it is not a guaranteed upper bound on the realized future invocation rate. The threshold-selection and comparator-specific procedures are given in Section~\ref{sec:exp-freeze}. This is an \emph{invocation-count} constraint rather than a measured budget on end-to-end computation.

\subsection{Regime-Level Utility Decomposition}
\label{sec:regime-decomposition}

To interpret aggregate differences, let $S$ index mutually exclusive target-relative relationship-evidence states (Section~\ref{sec:framework-states}). Their contributions to mean specialist value satisfy
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\Delta}_r
    =\sum_s P(S=s\mid R=r)
    \,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid S=s,R=r\right],
    \label{eq:regime-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\Delta}_r=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_M(X)\mid R=r]$. This accounting separates the frequency of the retrospectively defined states from the utility conditional on each state. A separate same-event candidate comparison examines the change in $\Delta_M$ when the ranking environment itself changes (Section~\ref{sec:exp-regimes}).

Observed outcomes subsequently provide development supervision and retrospective explanations of relative value.

\subsection{Strong Sequential Base Recommenders}
\label{sec:framework-base}

The Base represents the recommendation capability to which relationship knowledge is added. The original KuaiLive Base combines room- and creator-identity SASRec branches to rank eligible \emph{rooms}; within-candidate score standardization precedes their development-selected fusion. Twitch uses the availability-aware LiveRec model with context and repeated-consumption components \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. KuaiLive-M3 employs author-level SASRec and GRU4Rec encoders that rank eligible persistent authors. These are designated trained incumbents for their respective tasks rather than a single model transferred across datasets. Their training, candidate construction and checkpoint selection are specified in Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup} and Supplementary S2--S3.

\subsection{Transparent Relationship-Memory Specialist}
\label{sec:framework-memory}

The specialist scores each eligible candidate using user--creator relationships observed strictly before the current event. Let $n_{u,i}(t)$ be the number of prior user interactions with creator $i$, and let $n_{u,\max}(t)=\max_j n_{u,j}(t)$ over the eligible recorded history. The long-term relationship score is $\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)=n_{u,i}(t)/\max\{1,n_{u,\max}(t)\}$. For the last $K_s=10$ user--creator interactions, indexed backward from the most recent by $d=0$, the short-term score is $\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)=\max_{\{d:i_d=i\}}\exp(-d/3)$, with an empty-set maximum of zero. Repeated recent interactions thus contribute their most recent decayed strength. The popularity score $\mathrm{Pop}(i)$ applies log-count transformation and min--max scaling to the training-split creator frequencies, assigning zero to a creator absent from that reference. The fixed relationship ranking score is
\begin{equation}
s_M(u,i,t)=
0.45\,\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)
+0.45\,\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)
+0.10\,\mathrm{Pop}(i).
\label{eq:memory-scoring}
\end{equation}

These weights define the fixed specialist rather than a per-event trained fusion. In the original room-ranking task, multiple eligible rooms can inherit a common creator relationship score. Twitch and M3 directly rank persistent streamers or authors. In M3, the last ten \emph{all-author} prior interactions determine Short, while Long's normalization counts all past authors, including authors absent from the TRAIN-known candidate vocabulary. Such earlier authors can affect historical recency and normalization without becoming eligible current candidates. The frozen incumbent and Memory always rank the same decision-time candidate set; deterministic ranking-tie conventions and dataset-specific history eligibility are documented in Section~\ref{sec:exp-data} and Supplementary S1--S2.

\subsection{Base-Relative Relationship-Evidence States}
\label{sec:framework-states}

The visibility of the subsequently observed target in prior history provides an interpretable partition of realized specialist value. Let $V_L(x)$ indicate whether the target creator occurs within the Base's most recent $L$ input interactions and $H(x)$ whether it occurs anywhere in the eligible pre-target history. Define
\begin{equation}
S_L(x)=
\begin{cases}
\text{represented},&V_L(x)=1,\\
\text{recoverable but unrepresented},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=1,\\
\text{unavailable},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:diagnostic-states}
\end{equation}
Here \emph{represented} describes input-sequence membership, not a claim about what the trained Base representation retains. Since this partition refers to the observed target, it is used to explain results after evaluation; neither $S_L$ nor target-specific recurrence distance is a selector input.

\subsection{Observable Features and Conditional Relative-Utility Estimation}
\label{sec:framework-estimation}

The decision vector $Z$ combines historical relationship descriptors with statistics computed from the Base's candidate scores. Its history component characterizes how much and what kind of repeated creator evidence is available, while score-distribution summaries characterize the incumbent's current ranking context. The relative-utility regressor is trained on DEV event labels $\Delta_M(x)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta-memory}); the current target, its diagnostic state and realized rank utility are excluded from the predictor features.

The Twitch implementation uses six history and eight Base-score descriptors; M3 uses seven history/candidate and five Base-score descriptors. The platforms use different history construction and ranking units, so their fitted models and feature vectors are protocol-specific. In M3, the history-support fraction can be obtained from previously observed creator identities and the available candidate roster \emph{before} evaluating the full Memory ranking; it does not expose the current target. Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparators} defines the comparator prediction targets and exact feature groups, with full transformations in Supplementary S2 and S7.

\subsection{Specialist Selection and Comparator Policies}
\label{sec:framework-controls}

At inference, the Base produces the default ranking and its observable score descriptors. The selector applies the DEV-calibrated pointwise rule from Eq.~\eqref{eq:utility-threshold}, returning the Base ranking or computing Memory on the identical available candidates. To test whether specialist-specific value offers more decision information than a broader request-difficulty signal, we compare Utility against predictors of incumbent difficulty and standalone specialist performance, as well as pre-outcome heuristic policies where the experimental protocol permits. Their complete definitions and inferential roles belong to Section~\ref{sec:exp-comparators}. The selection mechanism changes which ranking is returned, not the two underlying fixed rankings.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

The experiments connect complementary questions through three nonexchangeable live-streaming ranking tasks (Table~\ref{tab:exp-datasets}). Twitch tests whether relative-value prediction improves held-out streamer selection; the original KuaiLive task examines how candidate environments change a fixed room-ranking comparison; KuaiLive-M3 tests specialist decisions on independently frozen forward-time author-ranking events. Each numerical comparison uses the ranking unit and candidates specified for its own protocol.

\subsection{Datasets and Prediction Tasks}
\label{sec:exp-data}

\paragraph{Twitch/LiveRec.}
We use the availability-aware LiveRec protocol to rank streamers at ten-minute observed activity steps \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. The filtered study has 46,878 DEV and 44,221 held-out TEST requests, each with one recorded relevant streamer. The Base has an input length of 16; eligible relationships precede the target step, and the Memory popularity reference uses training records. Recorded observation boundaries are not interpreted as exact physical session entry or exit times. The primary TEST examines a DEV-frozen Utility policy; alternative history timing assumptions are evaluated in Supplementary S1.

\paragraph{Original KuaiLive.}
We rank a time-bounded live \emph{room} using a Dual-ID Base and a specialist that aggregates histories through persistent creators \cite{qu2026kuailive}. A per-user chronological leave-last-two-out split yields 10,222 eligible users with paired development and previously examined test events. In the sampled-active regime, each target is ranked alongside 574 distinct contemporaneously active non-target rooms; the full-active regime includes the contemporaneous room roster. Session eligibility is defined by the recorded half-open time interval, with the observed positive retained where its source interval is inconsistent with its presence. The 575 candidates include the target; sampled non-targets are ranking alternatives, not verified negative preferences. The same 10,222 TEST targets are reused in the \emph{retrospective} paired candidate analysis.

\paragraph{KuaiLive-M3.}
The M3 evaluation ranks persistent \texttt{author\_id} identities; contemporaneously active room sessions define author availability. Its global recorded-time TRAIN boundary is 23 March 2026 12:00, followed by DEV until 27 March 2026 14:00 and TEST thereafter, using source ordering without assigning an unverified time zone. From 24,995,148 eligible training interactions, the protocol builds a deterministic, outcome-independent evaluation cohort of 50,049 DEV and 47,575 TEST requests, with 12,533 users in TEST. Each eligible target is TRAIN-known and has at least two prior valid interactions; candidates are distinct TRAIN-known authors with at least one active room at decision time. TEST uses the complete eligible author roster (median 31,065 candidates). The 4,096-request, 575-author DEV panel is used only for neural checkpoint screening, not final full-active evaluation. The author task and global forward-time split are distinct from original KuaiLive room prediction; the protocol does not assert user-disjointness between temporal segments.

\subsection{Baselines and Compared Methods}
\label{sec:exp-comparators}

Within every task, the two fixed scorers rank the same available candidates, and Utility predicts their single-event difference (Eq.~\eqref{eq:delta-memory}). Twitch uses the trained LiveRec Base and the fixed 14-feature Utility regressor. Its \emph{Difficulty} comparator predicts $1-u_{10}(B,x)$ from the same decision-time feature family. The Utility threshold is DEV-frozen; for evaluation, Difficulty and the outcome-based Oracle choose their top-scored or highest-realized-gain requests at the \emph{retrospectively matched TEST call count}. These equal-count comparisons measure selection quality but are not separately frozen pointwise deployment policies.

The original KuaiLive candidate analysis uses the same fixed Dual-ID Base checkpoint pair, sampled-DEV-selected room fusion coefficient and fixed Memory scoring across the paired sampled/full candidate regimes. Its separate baseline-competitiveness tests use additional model configurations with different tuning budgets (Supplementary S3), rather than a claim of equivalently optimized state-of-the-art models. No new prospectively frozen room-level gate test is inferred from the paired candidate diagnosis.

M3 uses SASRec and GRU4Rec author-level Base families with three fitted initialization seeds each (20261012, 20261013 and 20261014). For each Base, five policy objectives are compared: \emph{Utility} predicts $u_{10}(M,x)-u_{10}(B,x)$, \emph{Difficulty} predicts $-u_{10}(B,x)$, and \emph{Specialist Quality} predicts $u_{10}(M,x)$; all three employ the same regression class and available pre-outcome descriptors. \emph{Uncertainty} uses the negative Base top-two score margin and \emph{History} uses prior interaction count. Uncertainty is a score-margin heuristic, not a calibrated predictive-uncertainty estimator or a reimplementation of an external uncertainty-guided method.

For M3, 12 available descriptors comprise five Base-score summaries (top-two margin, score standard deviation, standardized top score, top score and mean score) and seven history/candidate summaries (prior interaction count, prior TRAIN-known interaction count, number of distinct prior warm authors, maximum warm-author repetition, distinct recent warm authors, candidate-history support share and log candidate count). Twitch uses 14 features: history size, repetition, entropy, historical drift, temporal regularity and a history-complexity index, together with eight Base score-spread, margin and concentration summaries. Feature construction, model training and prior-work alignment are specified in Supplementary S2 and S7. The corresponding selector estimators are trained separately within each task; none is transferred between Twitch and M3.

\subsection{Experimental Protocols}
\label{sec:exp-freeze}
\label{sec:exp-regimes}

\paragraph{Training, out-of-fold estimation and policy freeze.}
All incumbent checkpoints and specialist score definitions are fixed before their corresponding TEST comparisons. Twitch uses five-fold DEV out-of-fold (OOF) utility estimation on its filtered DEV cohort, followed by DEV-frozen Utility threshold selection and a single held-out TEST evaluation. Original KuaiLive pairs the same trained checkpoint and raw score functions over sampled/full candidate protocols; its subsequent candidate analysis reuses already examined TEST interactions.

M3 uses 64-dimensional, length-16 SASRec/GRU4Rec author encoders. Checkpoint selection is based only on a common 4,096-request sampled-candidate DEV panel; final DEV fitting and TEST scoring use full-active author candidates. Separately for each of the six Base checkpoints, five user-group-disjoint DEV OOF folds estimate the three supervised gate objectives. Each of the five policy scores—including the two deterministic heuristics—is threshold-calibrated to maximize DEV OOF incremental ranking value subject to at most $30\%$ DEV Memory invocations. The score comparison is inclusive, with all events tied at the selected threshold counted together. Final selectors and thresholds are then fixed before TEST.

The five M3 selection objectives consequently share a \emph{development calibration constraint}, but their realized TEST invocation fractions may differ and can exceed $30\%$. The frozen policies are applied pointwise, without recalibration or batch-level TEST top-count matching. The original Twitch retrospective equal-count Difficulty/Oracle comparisons and the original KuaiLive retrospective candidate diagnostics have distinct inferential status (Table~\ref{tab:exp-methods}).

\paragraph{Forward-time information boundary.}
M3 evaluation proceeds chronologically. Earlier observed interactions—including preceding TEST-period events, whether or not those events belong to the sampled TEST scoring cohort—can enter later users' histories once observed. No current or future target is used for Base/Memory scores, the observable gate features, candidate membership or threshold selection. Base predictions, Memory rankings and policy decisions are recorded before the corresponding TEST target labels are revealed for metric evaluation. Candidate rank ties use a deterministic author-identity convention. Cohort and checkpoint identities are immutable in the accepted evaluation; additional source-level timestamp/hash and numerical tie conventions are documented in Supplementary S1--S2 rather than treated as alternative evaluation outcomes.

\paragraph{Paired candidate-regime evaluation.}
For the original KuaiLive room task, the same user history, target and fixed raw scorers are evaluated on the sampled-active subset $S$ and full-active set $F$. The room/streamer Base standardizes branch scores using a candidate-set reference that may change with the regime. Thus, for event $i$ we define the paired change
\begin{equation}
D_i =
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})\big]
-
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})\big].
\label{eq:paired-regime-shift}
\end{equation}
To describe the contribution of candidate membership versus the reference used for branch-score standardization, let $g_i(C,R)$ denote the specialist-minus-Base utility with ranked candidates $C\in\{S,F\}$ and standardization reference $R\in\{S,F\}$. Their order-averaged allocations are
\begin{align}
A_{C,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(F,S)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(S,F)\big],\nonumber\\
A_{R,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(S,F)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(F,S)\big].
\label{eq:factorial-allocations}
\end{align}
The identity $A_{C,i}+A_{R,i}=g_i(F,F)-g_i(S,S)=D_i$ provides a within-protocol descriptive accounting. The membership term combines candidate number, composition and competition rather than identifying a candidate-count-only causal effect. Three checkpoint pairs and nine sampled draws on the same examined cohort support a sensitivity analysis in Supplementary S5 and S8.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:exp-statistics}

The primary endpoint is event-level NDCG@10 (Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg}); secondary endpoints are HR@10 and the realized specialist invocation fraction. Comparisons are paired by event, with user-level dependence retained in resampling. Target-specific visibility states (Eq.~\eqref{eq:diagnostic-states}) and recurrence diagnostics are assigned retrospectively and never used as selector inputs. Random@budget expectations and outcome-using Oracle references are descriptive, not prospective learned policies.

Twitch TEST confidence intervals are based on 5,000 user-paired bootstrap replicates; original KuaiLive candidate-regime intervals use 3,000 paired user resamples. For the M3 cohort of 47,575 author requests from 12,533 users, 5,000 user-cluster paired bootstrap samples yield 95\% percentile intervals, and 5,000 user-cluster sign-randomization replicates yield one-sided $p$-values under the stated approximate sign-symmetry null. The predeclared M3 primary family contains Utility versus (i) Always Base, (ii) Difficulty and (iii) Specialist Quality; Holm's procedure \cite{holm1979} adjusts these three contrasts \emph{within each} Base--seed fit. The six fits share the same TEST users and do not imply six independent population tests or familywise control across all 18 contrasts. Utility versus Uncertainty, History and unconditional Memory are additional comparisons, not members of that preregistered three-contrast family. Inference is conditional on the fixed models, candidate rules, cohort and selection policies.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:exp-implementation}

The original KuaiLive room/creator Base uses 64-dimensional ReChorus SASRec branches with one attention layer, four heads and a maximum input length of 50. LiveRec uses a context length of 16 with context and repeat modules. The M3 Base models use 64-dimensional author embeddings and length-16 histories, with one self-attention block for SASRec and one recurrent layer for GRU4Rec. Twitch Utility and the three M3 supervised selector objectives use histogram gradient-boosting regression with 200 iterations, learning rate $0.05$, depth $3$, minimum leaf size $50$ and an $\ell_2$ penalty of $1$. Training and deterministic preprocessing details, full component specifications, six-fit registry and event/metric tie rules are provided in Supplementary S1--S3 and S7--S8. Observed Memory-call rates and warmed scoring time are reported separately because one is not a substitute for the other.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

We assess when a fixed relationship-memory specialist adds ranking value beyond a trained incumbent, whether that marginal value can guide specialist selection, and how its evaluation depends on the ranking environment. The evidence proceeds from relative-utility heterogeneity to frozen selection decisions, followed by a paired candidate-regime analysis, developmental explanations and measured computational costs.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Utility of Relationship Memory}
\label{sec:results-heterogeneity}

The ranking contribution of relationship memory is heterogeneous rather than uniformly positive. On 44,221 Twitch held-out streamer-ranking requests, unconditional Memory obtains NDCG@10 $0.53980$, below LiveRec Base at $0.58211$ (paired difference $-0.04232$, 95\% CI $[-0.04521,-0.03950]$). Yet the negative aggregate masks a substantial positive contribution for previously encountered creators outside the incumbent's visible input window.

Figure~\ref{fig:results-evidence-states} and Table~\ref{tab:results-evidence-states} show that Memory improves NDCG@10 by $+0.22045$ on the 5,539 \emph{recoverable-but-unrepresented} TEST events. Its contribution is $-0.01935$ on 24,285 represented events and $-0.18215$ on 14,397 unavailable events. These target-defined groups identify the conditional pattern after the outcome is observed; the online selector does not receive their labels. The recoverable-group gain demonstrates that older observed relationships can add ranking information even when the same fixed specialist underperforms on average.

In the distinct KuaiLive-M3 author-ranking TEST, Memory reaches NDCG@10 $0.34679$ on 47,575 events, exceeding each of six fitted SASRec and GRU4Rec incumbents ($0.25800$--$0.26065$). For the primary SASRec fit its advantage is $+0.08879$. Thus, the sign of aggregate Memory--Base value changes across the two held-out tasks. Because Base design, ranking unit, candidates and time protocol differ together, this contrast characterizes incumbent- and setting-relative knowledge value without assigning the difference to one isolated cause.

\subsection{Decision Value of Predicting Relative Utility}
\label{sec:results-decision}

Given heterogeneous specialist value, the relevant decision signal is the predicted improvement of \emph{this} Memory over \emph{this} Base, rather than predicted Base difficulty alone. On Twitch, a DEV-frozen Utility rule selects Memory on 6,650 of 44,221 TEST requests ($15.04\%$) and improves NDCG@10 from Base $0.58211$ to $0.58983$ (paired gain $+0.00772$, 95\% CI $[+0.00641,+0.00903]$). \subsection{Candidate-Regime Dependence of Relative Utility}
\label{sec:results-regimes}

The candidate environment is part of the value being estimated. On the original KuaiLive \emph{room}-ranking task, 10,222 identical user--time--target events yield opposite Memory--Base results when the same fixed raw ranker scores are evaluated under sampled-active and full-active candidates. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Operational Value Is Base- and Context-Dependent}
\label{sec:discussion-operational-value}

The evidence distinguishes the informational content of historical relationships from their value relative to a trained recommender. In Twitch, the same transparent Memory specialist performs worse than Base on average, yet provides a large positive ranking gain for target creators present in prior interactions but absent from the incumbent's visible input. This pattern is consistent with relationship evidence complementing a truncated sequential representation on a subset of requests. On the full-active KuaiLive-M3 author task, by contrast, Memory's mean NDCG@10 exceeds each of six independently trained SASRec and GRU4Rec incumbents. The two environments thus exhibit different signs of aggregate specialist-minus-Base value while retaining an operationally meaningful event-level comparison within each task.

This contrast clarifies the unit of knowledge valuation. A specialist's standalone accuracy and its improvement over an incumbent answer different questions: the latter depends jointly on the incumbent's learned history representation, the specialist's evidence and the ranking alternatives available at the decision. Twitch and M3 change these factors together, so the difference in mean signs is not attributable to candidate count, architecture or temporal period alone. Complementarity is better interpreted at the level of a specified Base--Memory pair and ranking regime than as a universal advantage of longer relationship history. This extends the practical relevance of knowledge fusion and memory-construction research \cite{xie2026mhgfcl,chen2026memrec} by treating downstream incremental ranking value as a separately measurable outcome.

\subsection{Interpreting Conditional Evidence and Candidate Regimes}
\label{sec:discussion-candidate-regimes}

The original KuaiLive paired room-ranking analysis demonstrates why eligible candidates are part of this outcome. For unchanged user--time--target events and fixed raw Base and Memory scores, Memory-minus-Base mean utility changes from negative under 575 sampled-active rooms to positive under the corresponding full-active room universe (Section~\ref{sec:results-regimes}). The target-relative historical states remain fixed across the paired comparisons, so the observed shift cannot be explained by a changed prevalence of those states. Instead, the alternatives against which the target is ranked alter the measured contribution of the specialist.

The broader inconsistency of sampled ranking comparisons is established in prior work \cite{krichene2020sampled}. The distinct implication here concerns \emph{which knowledge source to use}: the sign of the specialist's measured incremental value, and hence the supervision available to a gate, depends on the candidate protocol. The crossed candidate-membership/score-reference analysis assigns most of the original KuaiLive shift to the membership dimension within that accounting, but membership changes list length, composition and competition together; it is not an isolated causal effect of candidate count. The distinctive implication is therefore about the candidate-conditioned label used to value a particular specialist, not a new proof of sampled-metric inconsistency. The new M3 TEST strengthens evaluation under a large full-active \emph{author} candidate universe, without constituting another paired \emph{room} candidate-size experiment.

\subsection{From Relative Utility to Selective Decisions}
\label{sec:discussion-selective-decisions}

The comparative decision principle follows established learning-to-defer theory \cite{mozannar2020defer,narasimhan2022posthoc}: choose an alternative when its expected incremental value justifies doing so under the specified decision rule. The relevant prediction target for relationship knowledge is the NDCG@10 gain of \emph{this} Memory specialist over \emph{this} incumbent, not merely whether the incumbent is likely to fail. An event can be difficult for Base without becoming better ranked by Memory, and a strong Memory ranking need not represent a gain over an equally strong Base. Historical context and Base score descriptors together provide pre-outcome information with which to estimate that distinction.

The frozen Twitch TEST demonstrates improved selection relative to Base and to a retrospectively equal-count Difficulty control. KuaiLive-M3 adds a separately frozen author-ranking evaluation: across two Base families and three fitted seeds each, Utility surpasses Base and four DEV-frozen alternative gate objectives. In the M3 primary comparisons, Utility's gains over Base, Difficulty and Specialist Quality have positive user-cluster intervals and survive the three-comparison Holm procedure within every fitted Base--seed condition (Section~\ref{sec:results-decision}). The strongest M3 alternative, a simple Base-margin Uncertainty score, remains below Utility, although it is a secondary comparison and does not reproduce GUIDER's uncertainty model \cite{xu2026guider}. The six fitted models share the same TEST cohort and therefore provide model-initialization and architecture sensitivity evidence rather than six independent population replications.

The results support the discriminative value of predicting relative ranking improvement without requiring perfect event-wise utility prediction. On Twitch, the selected-event gain is positive despite a modest realized-utility rank correlation and a substantial gap to the outcome-informed Oracle. On M3, the corresponding frozen selection gains persist across the fitted incumbents, but the Oracle@budget remains a retrospective upper bound. This evidence identifies an actionable \emph{decision signal}, not an optimal selector for every feasible information or resource condition.

\subsection{Invocation Constraints, Computational Cost and Validity}
\label{sec:discussion-validity}

The interpretation of selective decisions depends on the resource being constrained. The M3 policies have independently selected thresholds under a common maximum 30\% \emph{DEV} Memory-call criterion; their TEST invocation rates need not be equal or bounded by 30\%. The original Twitch Difficulty and Oracle controls instead match the realized frozen Utility count retrospectively. Both designs permit conclusions about the stated comparison, but neither establishes a universally enforced online quota or equal measured compute cost. The distinction is material because Base scoring, history-feature construction and gate inference contribute computation even when Memory is not invoked.

The M3 results also identify a concrete boundary for deployment. Fixed Always Memory reaches NDCG@10 $0.34679$, above the six Utility policies ($0.30177$--$0.30400$). In the separate 256-event warmed M3 DEV replay, Always Memory averages $0.803$\,ms per event versus $1.802$\,ms for Utility routing. Hence, under the measured precision and warmed scoring conditions, choosing the selective path is not justified as either more accurate or faster than simply using Memory. A specialist-call restriction could matter when knowledge access incurs an independently established external cost, permission requirement or resource limit; no such operational scarcity is measured here. The study therefore establishes \emph{selection quality under a stipulated call constraint}, not a general claim that a gate is economically preferable or that fewer specialist calls reduce serving latency.

Evaluation scope further conditions generalization. Twitch and M3 rank streamers and authors, whereas original KuaiLive ranks live rooms; their candidates, incumbent architectures and history construction differ. M3 uses a global forward-time TEST with prequentially observed earlier TEST interactions available to subsequent requests, not a person-disjoint cross-platform holdout. Models and pointwise policies are frozen before current TEST outcomes, while original KuaiLive candidate comparisons and target-defined relationship states are retrospective diagnostics. The M3 user-cluster intervals and within-fit Holm adjustments support the prespecified comparisons on this shared cohort, not a single globally adjusted family of 18 independent tests. Finally, warmed offline timing omits history maintenance, candidate-availability construction and production scheduling. These scope distinctions determine which claims the three protocols establish, rather than altering the observed improvements.

\subsection{Implications for Knowledge-Based Recommendation}
\label{sec:discussion-implications}

The combined findings suggest a decision-oriented way to evaluate additional relational evidence. A knowledge specialist should be assessed against a designated trained incumbent on the same time-eligible candidates, with standalone specialist quality and incumbent-relative improvement reported separately. Conditional analysis can then identify when earlier interactions provide complementary ranking information, while comparisons across candidate protocols test whether that evaluation remains appropriate for the intended ranking task. These steps make the contribution of historical knowledge empirically interpretable beyond its representation in a neural encoder.

Where an expert-invocation restriction is substantively justified, a selector should be trained for the specialist's predicted incremental utility rather than assuming that Base difficulty, standalone expert quality or an uncertainty proxy answers the same allocation question. Reporting frozen TEST call rates alongside ranking metrics and the complete observed computation path is necessary to assess whether such routing improves a real system. The present evidence supports relative-utility-aware decision evaluation for fixed relationship specialists, while indicating that unconditional Memory may be the better choice when no genuine invocation constraint applies.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study evaluates historical relationship knowledge by its incremental ranking contribution relative to a designated trained recommender and the eligible candidate set. In Twitch, fixed relationship Memory underperforms the incumbent on average but produces substantial gains for retrospectively identifiable recoverable relationships. In the distinct KuaiLive-M3 author-ranking evaluation, Memory instead exceeds six fitted incumbent models in aggregate. These findings locate the value of relationship evidence in a particular Base--specialist comparison, rather than in historical information alone.

Pre-outcome prediction of this relative value supports selective ranking decisions. A DEV-frozen Utility policy improves held-out Twitch ranking over Base and a retrospectively matched Difficulty control; in the independent forward-time M3 TEST, Utility outperforms four other DEV-frozen selection objectives across SASRec and GRU4Rec fits. The original paired KuaiLive room experiment shows why this value must be evaluated under the relevant candidate environment: sampled-active and full-active candidate protocols reverse the measured Memory advantage for unchanged target events. Together, these results connect the conditional value of relationship knowledge with the decision to use it.

The resulting contribution is a ranking- and candidate-aware evaluation of fixed relational evidence and invocation-constrained decision quality. It does not imply that selective routing is universally preferable: on M3, unconditional Memory achieves higher absolute ranking quality and lower measured warmed scoring time than the selective path. Distinguishing expert-call constraints from actual system costs is therefore essential when translating relative-utility predictions into deployment decisions.
