\section{Introduction}

Sequential recommendation models evolving user preferences from ordered interaction histories. Strong sequence encoders, including SASRec and BERT4Rec, have improved next-item ranking \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}, but a related decision remains unresolved: \emph{when does an auxiliary history-based specialist improve upon an already trained base recommender?} Richer evidence can be redundant with the base, informative in only some contexts, or insufficient to support a particular recommendation. Consequently, the availability of additional history and its incremental ranking value should be distinguished.

Recent approaches combine collaborative, semantic, structural, and temporal information through adaptive gating and multimodal fusion \cite{xie2026mhgfcl,zhu2026mifusr}. These methods primarily optimize evidence integration within a recommender. We instead study the operational, event-level advantage of a separately specified expert over a fixed base under an identical candidate-ranking task. We call this perspective \emph{Base-relative evidence valuation}: its target is the difference in ranking utility between two fixed predictors, rather than an intrinsic measure of the information content of historical records. This distinction connects evidence assessment to the established problem of routing among predictors \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc,mao2025mastering}.

Live-streaming recommendation is a natural test bed because candidate availability varies over time and user--creator relationships often persist across sessions \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. KuaiLive provides room-session and streamer identities, while Twitch/LiveRec provides availability-aware streamer recommendations \cite{qu2026kuailive,rappaz2021liverec}. We construct a transparent relationship-memory specialist from past user--creator interactions and study its contribution relative to strong sequential bases. Target-specific relationships may already be visible in the base input, appear only in longer history, or have no eligible prior occurrence. These retrospective states permit analysis of heterogeneity; actual invocation is decided from pre-outcome features.

Two empirical observations motivate the evaluation. In a post-hoc KuaiLive comparison using one fixed checkpoint pair and fusion coefficient, the specialist's aggregate advantage changes sign between sampled-active and full-active candidate protocols, even though test events and evidence-state membership are paired. On a separately frozen Twitch/LiveRec held-out evaluation, a development-trained relative-utility selector improves ranking over the base and a matched-count base-difficulty control. The former examines sensitivity to the candidate environment; the latter tests whether predicting an expert's \emph{relative benefit} is more useful than identifying difficult base cases. A separate factorial score analysis shows that the ranked candidate universe accounts for most of this paired regime shift under a two-order algebraic allocation; the reversal also persists across three trained checkpoint pairs.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operational valuation of relationship evidence.} We define and measure specialist-minus-base ranking utility at the event level, conditional on the evaluation candidate universe and a fixed expert--base pair.
    \item \textbf{Analysis of conditional utility.} We characterize how relationship visibility and recency relate to specialist value and examine candidate-regime differences through matched events and controlled model checkpoints.
    \item \textbf{Selective use of a fixed specialist.} We estimate conditional relative utility using observable context and evaluate its held-out decision value against difficulty-based, always-on, and hindsight controls under comparable invocation counts.
\end{itemize}
The formulation can apply to other fixed specialists; the empirical conclusions here concern the evaluated live-streaming datasets, candidate protocols, and offline ranking outcomes.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Sequential and Long-Horizon Recommendation}

Sequential recommendation learns representations of user preference from temporally ordered interactions. SASRec and BERT4Rec established self-attentive sequence modeling as a strong basis for next-item prediction \cite{kang2018sasrec,sun2019bert4rec}. Subsequent augmentation, contrastive, multi-interest and multi-granular methods aim to represent diverse or longer-range behavioral dependencies \cite{ni2025idmarec,sheng2025dsmgrec}. These approaches improve how history enters the base predictor. For a fixed trained base, however, the additional value of a distinct historical specialist remains an event-dependent empirical quantity: effective sequence encoding does not imply that any separate relationship signal will always help.

\subsection{Auxiliary Evidence Integration and Reliability}

Auxiliary information is commonly incorporated through learned representations or adaptive fusion. Multi-head gating combines collaborative, semantic and structural views while distinguishing short- and long-term preferences \cite{xie2026mhgfcl}; mutual-information-guided fusion seeks complementary multimodal cues while reducing redundancy \cite{zhu2026mifusr}. Denoising and modality balancing similarly address nuisance information in multimodal sequential recommendation \cite{zhang2026mcd4sr}. At a different level of granularity, KAPER selects temporally and structurally relevant knowledge-graph paths for re-ranking and explanation \cite{kim2026kaper}. Across these designs, evidence can be constructed, weighted or selected as part of the recommendation pipeline.

A complementary line examines whether auxiliary signals are reliable or potentially harmful. Content- and interaction-level reliability estimation allows multimodal models to reduce the influence of uncertain inputs \cite{huo2026reliability}. Studies of negative transfer in multimodal sequential recommendation show that adding pretrained information can degrade downstream ranking unless its contribution is managed \cite{peng2025negative}. Conditional gating has also been examined for heterogeneous structured and textual knowledge in question answering \cite{yerragunta2026gating}. Reliability and complementarity are related but distinct targets: a reliable signal may add little utility when the current base already ranks correctly.

The classical value-of-information perspective assesses changes in expected decision utility when information becomes available \cite{abbas2025voi}. Here the evidence source is already instantiated as a fixed ranking specialist. The empirical object is therefore the \emph{incremental utility of choosing that specialist over a particular base}, rather than the value of acquiring a previously unknown variable.

\subsection{Expert Routing and Learning-to-Defer}

Learning-to-Defer (L2D) considers how a system selects between a predictor and one or more experts. Foundational work treats deferral through cost-sensitive prediction \cite{mozannar2020defer}; post-hoc deferral provides decision rules for an existing model and an expert \cite{narasimhan2022posthoc}. Later work studies stronger consistency guarantees and dynamically varying expert pools \cite{mao2025mastering,montreuil2026online}. Recommendation architectures also use adaptive experts within sequence representations, as in CeDRec \cite{sun2025cedrec}.

These results establish selective routing as an existing decision framework. Our empirical question concerns a \emph{particular fixed relationship-memory expert}: whether its base-relative ranking utility has interpretable conditional structure and whether that structure yields better routing than a generic prediction of base weakness. Using a matched invocation count isolates the event-selection comparison without claiming a new L2D objective.

\subsection{Live-Streaming and Repeat-Aware Recommendation}

In live streaming, eligible items change with session availability and viewers may revisit the same creators. Bi-directional viewer--anchor modeling, dynamic room representation, interaction-aware preference learning, and graph-based representations address aspects of these behaviors \cite{zhang2021bidirectional,gao2023driver,chen2024preference,yu2024hypergraph,zhu2025repeated}. LiveRec explicitly models dynamic availability and repeat consumption \cite{rappaz2021liverec}, while KuaiLive supplies a further benchmark connecting live rooms to streamers \cite{qu2026kuailive}. These settings permit the relationship between a target creator and the base-visible sequence to vary while candidate availability and ranking difficulty also change.

\subsection*{Research Gap}

The preceding literature develops representations of history, integration and reliability of auxiliary information, and general expert-routing decisions. The remaining question addressed here is \emph{how the event-level ranking advantage of one fixed, transparent relationship-evidence specialist varies relative to a trained base under changing history and candidate conditions, and whether that advantage can be predicted before the outcome is observed}. This leads directly to the operational formulation in Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

We formalize Base-relative evidence valuation as an event-level ranking-utility problem. The formulation distinguishes the offline evaluation of a fixed relationship-memory specialist from the pre-outcome decision of whether to invoke it. This distinction is central to the paper: the realized marginal benefit of the specialist can be measured only after the relevant target is observed, whereas a serving policy must act using information available beforehand.

\subsection{Recommendation Setting and Event-Level Ranking Utility}
\label{sec:event-utility}

For user $u$ at recommendation time $t$, let the pre-outcome decision context under evaluation regime $r$ be $\xi_r=(u,t,\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t},\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$, where $\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the candidate set under that regime and $\mathcal{H}_{u,t}$ is the interaction history available strictly before $t$. An evaluated event is $x_r=(\xi_r,y)$, where $y\in\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{u,t}$ is the observed relevant target. The underlying interaction instance $(u,t,y,\mathcal{H}_{u,t})$ may be held fixed while different candidate-set constructions produce distinct decision contexts and, hence, distinct full events. Ranking models receive the context but not $y$, which is used only for offline evaluation. For concision, the subscript $r$ on $\xi$ and $x$ is omitted when a regime is fixed.

Let $A$ be a ranking model and let $r_A(y\mid\xi)$ denote the 1-indexed rank assigned to $y$ among $\mathcal{C}_{u,t}$. For cutoff $K$, define the event-level ranking utility
\begin{equation}
    u_K(A,x)=
    \begin{cases}
        \displaystyle \frac{1}{\log_2\!\left(r_A(y\mid\xi)+1\right)}, & r_A(y\mid\xi)\le K,\\[6pt]
        0, & r_A(y\mid\xi)>K.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:event-ndcg}
\end{equation}
With one relevant target per event, Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg} is the event-level contribution to NDCG@$K$; aggregate NDCG@$K$ is its empirical mean over evaluation events. Because rank utility is defined relative to the event-specific candidate set, changing candidate construction can change Base-relative specialist value even when the underlying user--target history is unchanged. The formulation below requires only a scalar event-level utility; NDCG@$K$ is used because it is the primary ranking metric in our evaluation.

\subsection{Base-Relative Specialist Utility}
\label{sec:relative-utility}

Let $B$ denote a fixed base recommender and $M$ a fixed relationship-memory specialist. For a given selective-decision experiment, both rank the same event-specific candidate set and are fixed before the invocation policy is learned. We define the specialist-minus-base utility
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_M(x)=u_K(M,x)-u_K(B,x).
    \label{eq:delta-memory}
\end{equation}
A positive value indicates an event-level ranking gain from using the specialist instead of the base, zero indicates no marginal gain, and a negative value indicates that replacing the base ranking with the specialist ranking is harmful for that event. Importantly, $\Delta_M(x)$ is an \emph{operational} marginal value of persistent relationship evidence as instantiated by the fixed specialist $M$ relative to the fixed base $B$. It should not be interpreted as an intrinsic or model-independent information value of the underlying history. Its magnitude can depend on the base, specialist scoring rule, popularity component, ranking cutoff $K$, candidate environment, and operating regime. In particular, a positive difference does not by itself distinguish novel relationship information from differences in scoring behavior or inductive bias.

The realized $\Delta_M(x)$ is an offline supervisory quantity because computing it requires the observed target $y$ and the rankings produced by both $B$ and $M$. Selective specialist use must therefore estimate its conditional expectation from information available before $y$ is known.

\subsection{Conditional Relative Utility}
\label{sec:conditional-utility}

Let $R$ index the operating recommendation condition under which candidate construction, temporal availability, or related protocol elements are fixed, and let $Z$ denote the information available at invocation time. We condition on $R=r$ to describe a regime-specific event distribution; $R$ need not be an explicit feature of $Z$. The variable $Z$ is constructed only from pre-outcome information, such as user and history summaries, candidate-set descriptors, and confidence signals produced by the base recommender. Define the conditional specialist-minus-base utility as
\begin{equation}
    \eta_r(z)
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid Z=z,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
When training and evaluating within a fixed regime, we suppress $r$ for notational simplicity. A learned estimator $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ scores events by expected specialist-minus-base value; Section~4 specifies its implementation.

Let $a(Z)\in\{0,1\}$ denote a pre-outcome selection policy, where $a(Z)=0$ uses the base ranking and $a(Z)=1$ uses the specialist ranking. This includes development-frozen score-threshold policies but excludes any dependence on the realized target. For an evaluated event $x$, define $u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(x;a)=(1-a)u_K(B,x)+a\,u_K(M,x)$. The expected improvement over always using the base satisfies
\begin{align}
    &\mathbb{E}\!\left[u_K^{\mathrm{sel}}(X;a)-u_K(B,X)\mid R=r\right] \\
    &\qquad=\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\Delta_M(X)\mid R=r\right]
    =\mathbb{E}\!\left[a(Z)\eta_r(Z)\mid R=r\right].
    \label{eq:expected-selective-gain}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:expected-selective-gain} gives the decision rationale for estimating $\eta_r$: under a fixed regime, expected gain over the base is determined by which events are selected and their conditional relative utility.

The information boundary is strict. The observed target $y$, realized ranks, realized $\Delta_M(x)$, and the Base-relative evidence states used later for mechanism analysis are offline quantities and are excluded from $Z$. In particular, the represented, recoverable-but-unrepresented, and unavailable states are diagnostic variables rather than selector inputs. This formulation also distinguishes conditional relative utility from generic base difficulty: a difficulty score concerns the performance or uncertainty of $B$ alone, whereas $\eta_r(z)$ depends on the relative ranking utility of both $M$ and $B$. Base weakness therefore does not imply positive specialist-minus-base utility.

\subsection{Selective Invocation and Equal-Count Comparison}
\label{sec:decision-rule}

A pre-outcome selector can use an estimate $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ of conditional specialist value to choose between the two rankings. With a development-selected threshold $\tau_r$, its pointwise rule is
\begin{equation}
    a_{\tau_r}(z)=\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{\eta}_r(z)>\tau_r\right],
    \label{eq:utility-threshold}
\end{equation}
where a selected event uses $M$ and an unselected event uses $B$. This rule does not enforce an exact invocation count across future requests. The training and threshold-selection protocol is given in Section~\ref{sec:exp-freeze}.

For a retrospective equal-count comparison on $n$ events, let $m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{\tau_r}(z_i)$ be the number of specialist invocations produced by the fixed policy. A comparator selects $m$ events by its own score while preserving the same invocation count. Base Difficulty orders events by predicted base loss, whereas a hindsight Oracle uses realized $\Delta_M(x_i)$; the latter provides a nondeployable upper bound. This comparison distinguishes the utility of \emph{which events are selected} from differences in invocation frequency. It does not imply that a serving policy has advance access to a full evaluation batch.

\subsection{Regime-Level Utility Decomposition}
\label{sec:regime-decomposition}

To analyze why aggregate specialist value changes across operating conditions, let $S$ denote a mutually exclusive and exhaustive partition of Base-relative relationship-evidence states, instantiated operationally in Section~4 and used only for analysis. Writing $\bar{\Delta}_r=\mathbb{E}[\Delta_M(X)\mid R=r]$, the law of total expectation gives
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\Delta}_r
    =\sum_s P(S=s\mid R=r)
    \,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\Delta_M(X)\mid S=s,R=r\right].
    \label{eq:regime-decomposition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:regime-decomposition} separates variation in evidence-state prevalence from variation in state-conditional utility. It is a descriptive identity: the specialist's relative ranking value may change because the base or candidate protocol changes, even without a change in evidence-state composition. Section~\ref{sec:framework} operationalizes the states, and Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup} specifies the comparisons.

\subsection{Strong Sequential Base Recommenders}
\label{sec:framework-base}

For KuaiLive, the base $B$ is a dual-identity sequential recommender (Dual-ID) with room-level and streamer-level SASRec branches. Room identities distinguish live sessions, while streamer identities connect sessions by the same creator. Each branch produces scores for the eligible \emph{room} candidates; scores are standardized within the candidate set before linear fusion. In the primary sampled-active policy experiment, the development-selected room and streamer coefficients are $0.1$ and $0.9$; other candidate-regime evaluations use their own stated development-selected settings. Thus the streamer branch contributes persistent identity without changing the prediction unit from rooms to creators.

For Twitch/LiveRec, we use the availability-aware LiveRec base \cite{rappaz2021liverec}, with its contextual and repeat-consumption components enabled and a maximum sequence length of $16$. The two platforms instantiate different base architectures, while the event-level relative-utility definition remains identical. Candidate eligibility, model selection and protocol-specific checkpoint identity are specified in Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}.

\subsection{Transparent Relationship-Memory Specialist}
\label{sec:framework-memory}

The specialist $M$ scores eligible candidates from historical user--creator interactions available before the target time. Let $i$ denote a candidate creator, let $n_{u,i}(t)$ be its count in the eligible pre-target history, and let $n_{u,\max}(t)$ be the largest such creator-specific count for user $u$. Define a long-term relationship strength as $\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)=n_{u,i}(t)/\max\{1,n_{u,\max}(t)\}$. For the ordered last $K_s=10$ eligible user--creator interactions, let $d$ be the reverse interaction index ($d=0$ for the most recent). The short-term component is $\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)=\max_{\{d:i_d=i\}}\exp(-d/3)$, with a maximum over an empty set defined as zero. Hence repeated appearances contribute their most recent decayed strength rather than an unbounded sum.

The popularity component $\mathrm{Pop}(i)$ is derived from eligible training-split creator counts by applying $\log(1+n_i)$ and min--max scaling over observed creators; unseen creators receive zero. The fixed specialist score is
\begin{equation}
s_M(u,i,t)=
0.45\,\mathrm{Short}(u,i,t)
+0.45\,\mathrm{Long}(u,i,t)
+0.10\,\mathrm{Pop}(i).
\label{eq:memory-scoring}
\end{equation}

The same scoring coefficients, short-history horizon and decay are used on both platforms; a candidate without prior interaction may still receive a nonzero popularity score. Candidates are ordered by score with deterministic identifier-based tie-breaking. In KuaiLive, distinct rooms mapped to one creator can consequently share a Memory score; Twitch candidates are streamers directly. The eligibility of pre-target records and platform-specific timing conventions are given in Section~\ref{sec:exp-data}. The fixed specialist supplies an interpretable alternative ranking rather than a learned fusion layer.

\subsection{Base-Relative Relationship-Evidence States}
\label{sec:framework-states}

We operationalize the diagnostic variable $S$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:regime-decomposition} using the target creator and pre-target history. Let $V_L(x)$ indicate that the target creator occurs in the sequence visible to a base with input length $L$, and let $H(x)$ indicate that the same creator occurs anywhere in the eligible pre-target relationship history. Then
\begin{equation}
S_L(x)=
\begin{cases}
\text{represented},&V_L(x)=1,\\
\text{recoverable but unrepresented},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=1,\\
\text{unavailable},&V_L(x)=0,\ H(x)=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:diagnostic-states}
\end{equation}

The three states partition evaluated targets relative to the particular base input length and eligible history. \emph{Represented} means visible to the base as input, not proven to be encoded by its learned representation. State membership, target-specific relationship distance and realized $\Delta_M(x)$ are computed only after the target is observed; none enters the pre-outcome selector feature vector $Z$. The states describe conditional association rather than an intervention on visibility.

\subsection{Observable Features and Conditional Relative-Utility Estimation}
\label{sec:framework-estimation}

The estimator $\hat{\eta}_r(z)$ predicts the development-event utility difference $\Delta_M(x)$ from pre-outcome history and Base-score descriptors. Six history features summarize log history length, creator repeat rate, normalized preference entropy, old-versus-recent Jensen--Shannon drift, circular temporal regularity, and composite history complexity. On Twitch, regularity uses phases modulo $144$ ten-minute steps, representing a daily cycle. Eight Base-score features comprise score standard deviation and range, top-1/top-2, top-1/top-5 and top-10/top-11 margins, the top score's standardized value, normalized score entropy, and top-ten softmax mass. These quantities summarize candidate ranking patterns; entropy and softmax mass are not assumed to be calibrated uncertainty estimates.

The composite history-complexity descriptor weights percentiles of entropy, preference drift and log history length by $0.4$, $0.4$ and $0.2$. For Twitch, the reference percentile distributions are determined from unlabeled development features and then reused during evaluation; KuaiLive uses eligible split-specific past-history reference pools. These transformations are based on pre-outcome information, not the target or the diagnostic evidence state. Training the regression estimator, selecting its threshold and evaluating frozen policies are described in Section~\ref{sec:exp-freeze}.

\subsection{Specialist Selection and Matched-Budget Controls}
\label{sec:framework-controls}

The primary decision rule in Eq.~\eqref{eq:utility-threshold} selects one of the two candidate rankings using observable features and a development-selected threshold. The relationship-memory scorer need only be invoked when the policy selects it; offline model assessment computes both rankings to obtain relative-utility labels.

A Base-Difficulty comparator estimates $1-u_K(B,x)$ using the same pre-outcome feature family, whereas Utility estimates $\Delta_M(x)$. An analysis-only Oracle orders events by their realized relative utility. To compare event selection fairly, Difficulty and Oracle can be evaluated at the invocation count produced by the fixed Utility policy (Section~\ref{sec:decision-rule}). These controls distinguish predicting \emph{benefit from Memory} from predicting \emph{weakness of Base}. Their training, offline budget matching and statistical evaluation are specified in Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

The evaluation addresses two questions: whether the ranking utility of a fixed relationship-memory specialist varies with the candidate environment and the base-visible history, and whether estimates of its relative utility improve specialist selection over predictions of base difficulty. All comparisons are made within the corresponding dataset and candidate protocol. Table~\ref{tab:exp-datasets} summarizes the main evaluation settings.

\subsection{Datasets and Prediction Tasks}
\label{sec:exp-data}

\paragraph{KuaiLive.}
KuaiLive provides live-shopping interactions associated with time-bounded live-room sessions and streamer identities \cite{qu2026kuailive}. The task is next-\emph{room} recommendation; relationship-memory features are defined at the \emph{streamer} level and assigned to candidate rooms through the room--streamer mapping. The primary evaluation uses a chronological leave-last-two-out split per eligible user: earlier interactions form the training history, the penultimate event supplies the development target, and the final event supplies the test target. After eligibility filtering and interaction deduplication, development and test each contain one target for each of 10,222 users. At test time the earlier development interaction is eligible history, while global streamer popularity is estimated from training interactions.

Room availability is determined from recorded session start and end times, using the half-open interval $[t_{\mathrm{start}},t_{\mathrm{end}})$. In the \emph{sampled-active} protocol, the positive target is ranked with 574 distinct, contemporaneously active unobserved rooms. In the \emph{full-active} protocol, the same user--time--target event is ranked against the active-room set, retaining the observed target where its recorded session interval is inconsistent with that set. The two protocols share evaluated events but differ in candidate-set size and score-normalization population. Unobserved rooms are evaluation negatives, not verified negative preferences; consequently, sampled ranking metrics are not interpreted as unbiased full-candidate estimates \cite{krichene2020sampled}.

\paragraph{Twitch/LiveRec.}
The second task recommends \emph{streamers} using the chronological, availability-aware Twitch/LiveRec protocol \cite{rappaz2021liverec}. User--streamer activity is recorded in ten-minute crawl steps; start and stop denote first and last observations rather than exact session boundaries. Candidate streamers are restricted to those available at the prediction step. The primary LiveRec base uses a maximum context of 16 interactions. The frozen development and test sets contain 46,878 and 44,221 one-positive target events, respectively. Memory history in the primary analysis admits records first observed before the target step, whereas the training-only popularity prior uses records ending before the training cutoff. A stricter split-end history definition is evaluated separately as a sensitivity analysis. Neither protocol assumes observation timing finer than the crawl resolution.

\subsection{Baselines and Compared Methods}
\label{sec:exp-comparators}

For KuaiLive, the principal base is the Dual-ID sequential recommender described in Section~\ref{sec:framework-base}. Room- and streamer-identity SASRec branches produce scores that are standardized separately within the eligible candidate set and combined using a development-selected fusion weight. To establish the competitiveness of this reference, we also compare popularity ranking, the individual SASRec branches, GRU4Rec, and ContraRec--BERT4Rec on the documented common-candidate evaluation protocol. For Twitch, the principal base is the official LiveRec model with contextual and repeat-consumption components enabled. The two platforms use different base architectures; the object of comparison is the specialist's utility \emph{relative to each platform's designated base}.

The relationship-memory specialist uses the fixed short-term, long-term, and popularity components of Eq.~\eqref{eq:memory-scoring} and ranks the same eligible items as its base. Selection experiments compare always using the base, always using Memory, and the relative-Utility selector of Section~\ref{sec:framework-controls}. Two controls test the specificity of Utility-based selection. A Base-Difficulty regressor predicts $1-u_{10}(B,x)$ from the same pre-outcome feature family; a hindsight Oracle ranks events by observed $u_{10}(M,x)-u_{10}(B,x)$. For offline comparison, both controls select the same number of events as the frozen Utility policy, separating event choice from invocation frequency. Only the Oracle uses realized test utility; neither Top-$m$ control is an online budgeted routing policy. The estimator and threshold are fitted separately on each platform's development data, so cross-platform evaluation does not constitute zero-shot transfer of a learned gate.

\subsection{Experimental Protocols}
\label{sec:exp-freeze}
\label{sec:exp-regimes}

\paragraph{Training and model selection.}
Base models are trained on the designated training split; architecture-specific hyperparameters and fusion coefficients are selected using development data. The relative-Utility and Base-Difficulty estimators use the same 14 observable descriptors specified in Section~\ref{sec:framework-estimation} and histogram-gradient-boosted regression. Five-fold out-of-fold (OOF) development predictions support Utility threshold selection by mean development ranking utility, with ties favoring fewer specialist invocations. The one-target-per-user protocols use shuffled KFold; the separate global-time KuaiLive analysis, which can contain multiple events per user, uses user-grouped folds. Final estimators are fitted on development data and evaluated with fixed transformations and thresholds on the held-out test set. On Twitch, feature percentiles use an unlabeled development reference retained for test transformation; KuaiLive constructs its history percentiles from the eligible past-history pool of the corresponding split. Detailed transforms and hyperparameters are provided in the supplementary implementation protocol.

\paragraph{Candidate-regime comparisons.}
KuaiLive is evaluated under sampled-active and full-active candidate sets. Native regime comparisons use their respective development-selected base configurations; a strict-transfer analysis instead carries the sampled-regime selector into the full-active environment. To assess the effect of the evaluation protocol without confounding trained model identities, a separate paired comparison fixes a single Room-SASRec/Streamer-SASRec checkpoint pair, a sampled-development-selected fusion coefficient, and the Memory rule. For each common test event, raw scores over the full-active candidates are computed once and restricted to the sampled subset. Branch scores are standardized separately within each candidate set. This paired design therefore varies candidate membership \emph{together with} the associated normalization reference, while keeping checkpoints, target, and eligible history constant. For event $i$, the contrast is
\begin{equation}
D_i =
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{full}})\big]
-
\big[u_{10}(M,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})-u_{10}(B,x_i^{\mathrm{sampled}})\big].
\label{eq:paired-regime-shift}
\end{equation}
The paired candidate-regime analysis is a retrospective supporting diagnostic on previously examined test instances, not an independently held-out confirmation. We further cross candidate membership $C\in\{S,F\}$ with the set $R\in\{S,F\}$ supplying the room- and streamer-score standardization moments, where $S$ and $F$ denote sampled-active and full-active candidates. With $g_i(C,R)$ denoting event-level Memory-minus-Base utility for this configuration, the two-order allocations are
\begin{align}
A_{C,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(F,S)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(S,F)\big],\nonumber\\
A_{R,i}&=\tfrac12\big[g_i(S,F)-g_i(S,S)+g_i(F,F)-g_i(F,S)\big].
\label{eq:factorial-allocations}
\end{align}
By construction, $A_{C,i}+A_{R,i}=g_i(F,F)-g_i(S,S)=D_i$. This is an algebraic decomposition under fixed raw scores, not a randomized causal effect. Three independently trained checkpoint pairs are compared with the same fusion coefficient and evaluation events; across-seed variability is distinguished from within-seed user-bootstrap uncertainty. Additional context-length and history-eligibility analyses assess sensitivity to input capacity and history definitions without reselecting the original test policy.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:exp-statistics}

The principal metric is NDCG@10, computed from the single-positive event utility in Eq.~\eqref{eq:event-ndcg}; hit rate at 10 (HR@10) is secondary. We report mean ranking utility and paired differences for Memory versus Base, Utility versus Base, and Utility versus the equal-count Difficulty control. Specialist invocation rate is the proportion of events assigned to Memory. Evidence-state analyses use the retrospective partition of Eq.~\eqref{eq:diagnostic-states} and distinguish differences in state prevalence from differences in conditional utility.

Confidence intervals for mean ranking differences use paired percentile bootstrap resampling at the user level. Primary one-target-per-user exports permit event-wise resampling; global-time analyses resample user clusters to preserve within-user dependence. The paired candidate-regime diagnostic uses 3,000 bootstrap replicates, with its full-versus-sampled differences computed on identical users. Variation across independently trained base seeds is reported separately from user-bootstrap uncertainty. Evidence-state, recency and context-length subgroup analyses are treated as explanatory rather than independent, multiplicity-adjusted confirmatory tests.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:exp-implementation}

The KuaiLive Dual-ID branches use ReChorus SASRec encoders with 64-dimensional embeddings, one attention layer, four heads and a maximum input sequence of 50. The primary Twitch LiveRec base uses an input length of 16 with both context and repeat components enabled. The Utility estimator uses histogram gradient boosting with 200 iterations, learning rate $0.05$, depth $3$, minimum leaf size $50$, $\ell_2$ regularization $1$ and seed $20260918$. Source revisions, remaining training configurations, candidate construction, frozen evaluation splits and reproducibility files are specified in the supplementary protocol. Latency and memory requirements are interpreted only when supported by measured resource configurations; offline invocation frequency alone is not an estimate of serving-time speedup.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Heterogeneous Utility of Relationship Memory}
\label{sec:results-heterogeneity}

We first examine whether the fixed relationship-memory specialist offers uniform incremental utility over the LiveRec base or whether its advantage depends on the target's relationship to the user's preceding interactions. On the frozen Twitch test set ($n=44{,}221$), always using Memory obtains an aggregate NDCG@10 of $0.53980$, below the Base value of $0.58211$ (Memory minus Base: $-0.04232$). The aggregate comparison, however, masks differences across the three target-relative relationship-evidence states defined in Section~\ref{sec:framework-states}.

Figure~\ref{fig:results-evidence-states} contrasts the state-conditional differences on development and test events; Table~\ref{tab:results-evidence-states} reports the corresponding held-out Base and Memory scores and paired confidence intervals. In the \emph{recoverable-but-unrepresented} state, where the target creator occurs in eligible history but falls outside the Base's visible input, Memory exceeds the Base by $+0.22045$ NDCG@10 on 5,539 test events (95\% CI $[+0.21140,+0.23001]$). By contrast, the difference is $-0.01935$ for the 24,285 \emph{represented} events and $-0.18215$ for the 14,397 \emph{unavailable} events. The signs match the development results ($-0.01995$, $+0.23097$, and $-0.19562$, respectively), although their magnitudes are not identical. Thus, even when an identical scoring rule is applied to every event, the relative ranking outcome depends strongly on the relationship-evidence state.

These results characterize \emph{retrospective} heterogeneity in ranking utility rather than an observable rule for invoking Memory. In particular, the states are determined using the realized target creator, whereas the selection policy must use only pre-outcome features. The relevant decision question is therefore not whether the recoverable group benefits on average, but whether incremental specialist benefit can be predicted sufficiently well \emph{before} the target is observed to improve ranking under a fixed invocation policy. This question motivates the subsequent selective-decision evaluation.
