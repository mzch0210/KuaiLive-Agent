# 2. Related Work

> **Status:** manuscript draft; references below were selected from formally published versions rather than preprints when a final venue version was available.

## 2.1 Sequential, Long-Term, and Repeat-Aware Recommendation

Sequential recommendation models users’ preferences from ordered interaction histories. Self-attentive architectures such as SASRec and BERT4Rec established strong Transformer-style baselines by learning dependencies within interaction sequences (Kang and McAuley, 2018; Sun et al., 2019). Subsequent work has expanded this paradigm through richer sequence representations, augmentation, auxiliary information, and adaptive representation modules. For example, Sun et al. (2025) proposed CeDRec, which combines hierarchical data augmentation with an adaptive in-context expert network to improve sequential representation learning under sparsity, noise, and cold-start conditions. More recent KBS work also integrates collaborative or non-behavioral knowledge with sequential preference modeling; CollaPPA, for instance, combines collaborative multi-task fine-tuning with personalized preference alignment in an LLM-based sequential recommender (Yue et al., 2026).

A related line of research explicitly models longer-term, repeated, or multi-scale preference signals. These approaches establish that historical information beyond the immediately visible context can be useful and that user preference is not adequately described by a single short-term state. This observation is particularly salient in live-streaming settings, where repeat consumption and repeated interactions with the same channel are common (Rappaz et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2025). Accordingly, the contribution of the present study is not to argue that long-term or repeated behavior matters, nor to propose another mechanism for encoding broader history. We instead assume a strong sequential Base and ask a different question: **when does a separately maintained persistent relationship-evidence source retain positive marginal value after accounting for what the Base already represents?** This shifts attention from improving the Base representation itself to evaluating the residual value of an auxiliary evidence source relative to that representation.

## 2.2 Auxiliary Evidence Integration, Reliability, and Harmful Information

A large body of contemporary recommendation research focuses on incorporating heterogeneous auxiliary evidence. Knowledge-aware recommendation combines collaborative signals with semantic or structural information, while multimodal recommendation incorporates content modalities such as text and images. Recent KBS work illustrates increasingly sophisticated mechanisms for such integration. Xie et al. (2026) construct collaborative, semantic, and structural views and use multi-head gating for fine-grained adaptive fusion, explicitly distinguishing long-term interests from short-term preferences. Zhu et al. (2026) formulate multimodal sequential fusion through mutual information, maximizing cross-modal complementary information while reducing intra-modal redundancy and noise. Zhang et al. (2026) further address noisy and imbalanced modalities through collaborative denoising and modality balancing. These methods therefore improve recommendation by learning better joint representations from multiple available evidence sources.

Other studies focus more directly on evidence quality. Huo et al. (2026) introduce a reliability-aware multimodal recommendation framework that models both content-level and interaction-level reliability. CollaPPA similarly aims to align collaborative knowledge with personalized sequential preferences rather than treating auxiliary knowledge as uniformly useful (Yue et al., 2026). These approaches are important conceptual neighbors because they recognize that auxiliary information differs in usefulness or trustworthiness. However, **evidence reliability and Base-relative marginal value are not equivalent quantities**. An auxiliary signal may be reliable in itself yet add little value when the Base already represents overlapping information; conversely, a relatively simple source may be useful when it contains evidence absent from the Base. Our study therefore treats the auxiliary source as an object of event-level valuation relative to the current Base, rather than only as an input to representation fusion.

Research on negative transfer provides a complementary perspective on the risks of additional information. Peng et al. (2025) show that multimodal pretraining can induce negative transfer in sequential recommendation and propose a re-learning strategy to improve target-task adaptation. This establishes that additional or transferred information need not be uniformly beneficial. Our setting differs in both mechanism and objective: we do not study pretrained representations or transfer mismatch, but the event-level marginal ranking utility of a fixed persistent relationship-evidence specialist relative to a strong Base. Negative transfer is therefore a useful conceptual connection rather than the methodological formulation of our problem.

Taken together, these lines of work primarily ask **how heterogeneous evidence should be represented, aligned, denoised, weighted, or fused**. The present work centers on a preceding decision question: **is this evidence source incrementally useful relative to the Base for this event and operating regime?** We refer to this distinction as *Base-relative evidence valuation*. It does not replace evidence-integration methods; rather, it asks when additional evidence has sufficient marginal value to justify its use at all.

## 2.3 Expert Routing, Selective Prediction, and Learning-to-Defer

Selective prediction and Learning-to-Defer provide the closest general decision-theoretic background for choosing among predictors or experts. Mozannar and Sontag (2020) formulate learning to defer as jointly learning a predictor and a rejector that can pass decisions to a downstream expert, and provide consistency results through a reduction to cost-sensitive learning. Mao et al. (2025) extend this line to single- and multiple-expert routing, including one-stage and two-stage settings, and study surrogate losses, consistency guarantees, accuracy, and computational cost. These works make clear that **the general idea of selectively assigning instances to experts is established and is not a novelty claim of this paper**.

Expert-style architectures also appear directly in recommendation. CeDRec uses an adaptive in-context expert network to strengthen sequential representations under data sparsity and interaction noise (Sun et al., 2025). Such architectures use multiple experts as components of the recommender representation. By contrast, our relationship-memory specialist is maintained as a separate and deliberately transparent evidence source, and the principal object of study is its **marginal ranking utility relative to an already strong Base**. The question is therefore not only which expert should process an instance, but why this particular specialist contains incremental evidence for some recommendation events and not for others.

This distinction also motivates our use of a generic Base-Difficulty model. Difficulty is included as a **mechanistic control**: it tests whether identifying cases where the Base is weak is sufficient to identify cases where relationship Memory can help. It is not intended as a representative baseline for the full Learning-to-Defer literature. The empirical comparison between Difficulty and specialist-relative Utility is thus used to examine whether Base weakness and specialist solvability are equivalent decision signals, rather than to claim superiority over generic deferral theory.

## 2.4 Live-Streaming Recommendation

Live-streaming recommendation differs from conventional static-item recommendation because both available rooms and user attention can change rapidly, while viewers often return to the same channels and interact repeatedly with streamers. Early work modeled the two-sided dynamics of viewers and anchors: Zhang et al. (2021) proposed a bi-directional prediction framework that jointly models a viewer’s next preferred anchor and an anchor’s future audience. Rappaz et al. (2021) highlighted dynamic availability and repeat consumption as defining properties of live-streaming recommendation and introduced LiveRec to model these characteristics directly.

Subsequent studies have expanded the domain representation in several directions. Gao et al. (2023) proposed DRIVER, which dynamically represents live-streaming rooms using room states and users’ paths of entering and departing. Chen and Liu (2024) modeled interaction-aware preference decline during live-stream viewing, emphasizing the temporal dynamics of preference within a session. Yu et al. (2024) represented multi-viewer and multi-channel interaction structure through hypergraphs, while Zhu et al. (2025) explicitly modeled multiple types of repeated behaviors, including repeated room entry, chat, and gifting. Together, these studies demonstrate that live-stream recommendation benefits from modeling dynamic availability, viewer–streamer interactions, repeated behaviors, and richer room or interaction structure.

Our use of the live-streaming domain therefore does not rest on a claim that previous work ignores persistent or repeated viewer–streamer behavior. Instead, the domain provides a particularly informative setting for studying **Base-relative evidence value**: target-specific relationship evidence may already appear in the Base-visible sequence, may remain recoverable only from older history, or may be unavailable altogether, while the candidate environment itself changes with room availability. KuaiLive offers a complementary real-time benchmark with detailed live-room timing and user–streamer interactions, whereas Twitch/LiveRec provides a strong availability- and repeat-aware sequential Base. We use these environments to study when persistent relationship evidence remains incrementally useful relative to a strong sequential representation, rather than to introduce a new live-stream benchmark or a new generic model of repeated behavior.

### Positioning summary

The nearest research lines can be distinguished by their primary question:

| Research line | Primary question | Typical adaptation unit | Position of this study |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sequential / long-term modeling | How should broader history be encoded? | User/sequence representation | Assume a strong Base and evaluate residual evidence value |
| Auxiliary / multimodal fusion | How should heterogeneous signals be integrated? | Joint representation | Ask whether a source is incrementally useful before or instead of integrating it |
| Reliability / denoising | Which signals are trustworthy or noisy? | Modality, item, or interaction signal | Reliability does not imply positive marginal value relative to the Base |
| L2D / expert routing | Which predictor or expert should handle an instance? | Predictor/expert assignment | Characterize why a transparent evidence specialist is useful on particular events |
| Live-stream recommendation | How should dynamic rooms, interactions, and repeated behavior be modeled? | Domain-specific representation | Use live streaming as the empirical setting for Base-relative evidence valuation |

This positioning motivates the methodological focus of the paper: **the value of an auxiliary evidence source is evaluated relative to what the Base already represents, for the current event and recommendation regime.**
